Ko Yamori's insomnia didn't start because he was restless. It started because he was honest.

The backstory is almost comedically Japanese in its cruelty: Ko, in middle school, was [blamed by his classmates for rejecting a girl's confession](https://call-of-the-night.fandom.com/wiki/Kou_Yamori). Not for leading her on. Not for being unkind. For saying no clearly and directly, the way honest people do. His classmates sided with the girl, his social standing collapsed, and he stopped sleeping — as if his body had correctly assessed that the daylight world was not somewhere worth returning to.

This is where *Call of the Night* actually begins, and it's the most important thing to understand about everything that follows: Ko's exile is not self-pity. It's a reasonable response to a system that punishes honesty and rewards performance.

## The Social Contract Ko Refused

Japanese adolescence runs on a specific protocol. You study through middle school, perform the correct social behaviors, survive the gauntlet of entrance exams, repeat the cycle for university, emerge into a salaryman trajectory that, if you're lucky, pays off three decades from now. [Hikikomori](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hikikomori) — Japan's epidemic of social withdrawal, estimated at over a million people — is the pathologized name for what happens when someone refuses this protocol entirely. Ko doesn't fit the clinical definition cleanly, but the structure of his refusal is the same: the system demanded he keep performing, and his body said no. The entire contract is transactional — you subordinate your actual preferences, perform the expected role, and collect the social reward.

Ko did that. He [performed charismatic friendliness](https://www.cbr.com/call-of-the-night-yamori-ko-chunibyo/) in middle school despite never actually enjoying it — the series establishes that he appeared outgoing as a social facade, not because it reflected anything real. He wore the mask. He played the game. And when the game demanded he either accept a confession he didn't feel or absorb punishment quietly, he refused the lie and paid the price.

What's remarkable is that Kotoyama is not sympathetic about this in the way you might expect. Ko's insomnia is not presented as tragic. His refusal to return to school is [not framed as a disorder](https://www.cbr.com/call-of-the-night-main-male-character-insomnia/) — it represents "a lack of satisfaction with his day," which is the body's correct verdict on a life that wasn't actually working. As [the CBR school subversion piece notes](https://www.cbr.com/call-of-the-night-subverts-anime-school-setting/), the series treats school as alien and uncomfortable precisely because Ko sees it clearly. The other kids aren't having a better time. They've just been more successfully domesticated into performing that they are.

His nighttime wandering isn't escapism. It's the first honest life he's had.

## The Social Contract Nazuna Refused

Here's where the series gets structurally interesting: Nazuna has done exactly the same thing.

The [vampire society in *Call of the Night*](https://call-of-the-night.fandom.com/wiki/Vampires) operates by a specific code. Vampires are expected to create offspring — to turn humans, building out their household of underlings. They treat humans as livestock rather than companions. Eldest vampires like Hirata Niko function as [the hierarchy's enforcers](https://call-of-the-night.fandom.com/wiki/Nazuna_Nanakusa), and the central imperative is something close to: you are predator or you are failure. Fraternizing with prey as equals, refusing to create underlings, running a massage parlor — these are violations of a social order Nazuna has simply never accepted.

She is, within her own kind, exactly what Ko is within his: the one who refused to perform.

The parallels are specific. Ko was charismatic and failed to feel it. Nazuna is [estimated to be 30–40 years old](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Call_of_the_Night) but hasn't accumulated the emotional repertoire her age implies — she doesn't know how to love, doesn't know how to receive affection, operates through the controlled transaction of her cuddle service because actual intimacy is territory she's never navigated. She refused vampire society's predatory framework but didn't replace it with anything functional. Like Ko, she just stopped performing the expected role. And like Ko, this left her with the night and nothing else.

![Nazuna Nanakusa in the neon-lit night](https://get.wallhere.com/photo/Nazuna-Nanakusa-Yofukashi-no-Uta-2166727.jpg)

What makes Nazuna's position particularly acute is that she's been doubly excluded. She's not fully legible to humans — centuries old, vampiric, operating on a different register of time and need. She's also not legible to vampires — refusing offspring, treating Ko as a person rather than a conversion project, accumulating zero of the social capital her kind recognizes. There is no community that has a slot for her. The night isn't a refuge so much as the only remaining territory.

## The Night Belongs to Neither System

What makes the night function as more than atmosphere is that it genuinely belongs to no social order. [As one reviewer frames it](https://sonatano1.wordpress.com/2022/10/04/a-review-of-call-of-the-night/), the night city operates like the 17th-century Caribbean for pirates: outside the law, outside the system, carrying real danger but also the specific freedom that comes from operating where no jurisdiction reaches. Neither Ko's school system nor Nazuna's vampire coven has authority here. No attendance records. No offspring obligations. No performance metrics of any kind.

The series is careful about this. Ko doesn't discover the night as a romantic concept — he walks out one evening, swings in an empty park, and for the first time in the narrative is shown as comfortable in his own body. The night doesn't demand anything from him. It's not therapeutic. It's just the first time he's been somewhere with no social contract to fail.

Nazuna inhabits the same space for the same reason. Her massage parlor, her video game sessions, her general failure to perform the predatory vampire she's expected to be — these aren't hobbies. They're the shape of someone who stepped out of the system entirely and found she has no idea what to do with the freedom. [The Star Crossed Anime review](https://starcrossedanime.com/yofukashi-no-uta-anime-review-68-100/) describes the pair as "fundamentally lonely" people who "revitalize each other's worlds" — but the more precise framing is that they recognize each other's particular shape of exile.

## Mutual Recognition as the Foundation

The episode 7 CBR analysis makes this explicit: Ko and Nazuna are [surprisingly similar](https://www.cbr.com/call-of-the-night-episode-7-nazuna-ko-surprisingly-similar/) — both "fellow outcasts wandering through the night seeking purpose." What that piece captures is something more specific: when the vampire coven questions Nazuna's methods, Ko defends her not from romance but from recognition. He knows exactly what it looks like when someone who refused to play the assigned role gets punished for it. He's seen it in the mirror.

This is why the series' central conceit — that transformation requires genuine love — is more structurally interesting than it first appears. The condition is not attraction or desire. It's *love*: genuine feeling of a kind you can't manufacture. Kotoyama has built a series where the only way forward requires both characters to develop the emotional capacity they specifically lack. Ko can't love yet because he's never let himself fully inhabit his own life. Nazuna can't love yet because she's never figured out what she actually wants beyond the refusal of what was expected.

The transformation condition, in this reading, is Kotoyama saying: you can't get what you want through the same avoidance strategy that got you here. [TV Tropes' entry on the manga](https://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/Manga/CallOfTheNight) notes Kotoyama's signature use of "adolescent confusion and yearning for freedom" — but what the series makes clear is that freedom without direction is just a different kind of being stuck.

Consider what Ko actually does in the night: he drifts. He follows Nazuna around. He participates in her cuddle service transactions, observes other vampires, accumulates experiences he never asked for. The posture is passive — someone waiting to be transformed rather than someone doing the transforming. The series gradually makes this ironic: Ko entered the night to escape a social role he hadn't chosen, and found himself in another social role he also didn't choose, drifting through a world structured by Nazuna's rules rather than school's. Freedom from one system delivered him into dependence on another. The series won't let him have the exit he wanted without forcing him to ask what he actually wants to arrive at.

## Akira Asai and the Cost of the Argument

Before accepting the exile thesis entirely, it's worth sitting with Akira Asai — Ko's childhood friend, the one who stays behind.

Akira represents the series' honest counterweight. She chose the daylight world, maintained her social relationships, kept attending school. She doesn't [abandon Ko](https://call-of-the-night.fandom.com/wiki/Kou_Yamori), even as the gap between his life and hers becomes unbridgeable — she keeps showing up on the margins of his night world, unable to cross fully into it without abandoning what she's built. The series doesn't frame her choice as inferior to Ko's. Her persistence is genuine care, not weakness.

What Akira demonstrates is that the social contract Ko refused isn't purely hollow. It builds real things: friendships, continuity, a recognizable life. Ko couldn't perform it without feeling like he was lying. That doesn't mean everyone who performs it is lying. The exile thesis the series defends is not "the daylight world is wrong" — it's "some people are incapable of performing it authentically without destroying something in themselves." Akira is the evidence that this is a narrower claim than it initially sounds.

## What the Series Is Actually Arguing

[The *Anime Herald* piece](https://www.animeherald.com/2025/09/20/how-call-of-the-night-uses-its-vampires-to-build-its-structure/) cuts to it cleanly: "if we take 'vampires' to be anyone who lives outside normal society, the show's theming clicks into place perfectly." The vampire mythology isn't shallow metaphor — it's a formal structure for the same problem Ko faces in a school uniform. How do you build a real life after you've refused the one that was available to you?

*Call of the Night* is a series about the gap between opting out and actually constructing an alternative. Both Ko and Nazuna have successfully refused their respective social systems, and neither has any idea what comes next. What they find, improbably, is each other — two people who understand the specific shape of having walked away without a plan, of inhabiting a world that isn't designed for them. The series functions as the story of "nocturnal, liminal" space — not the destination but the territory between what you left and what you haven't built yet.

Their connection works because they're the only people each has encountered who understands the specific shape of having refused something without knowing what to put in its place. Not love, not yet. Mutual recognition of a particular kind of exile. The night holds them both because it's the only place with no system to fail.

Whether they can actually build something from that is the question Kotoyama spends fourteen volumes trying to answer honestly. The [chapter 200 ending](https://www.cbr.com/call-of-the-night-season-2-need-to-know/) doesn't resolve the love conditions so much as formalize them as the relationship's permanent structure — not a resolution but a commitment to keep trying. Which might be the most honest thing Kotoyama could have done: two people who spent their entire lives outside every social contract, finally writing their own.
