The Fourth Holy Grail War ends with exactly one servant still standing, and it is not the one who died for his king's vision, or the one who wanted nothing more than to be condemned for his sins, or the one who released his curse so Saber could use her Noble Phantasm and died for it. It is Gilgamesh — the King of Heroes, who owns everything, loves no one, and has nothing the War can take.

That is not a coincidence. It is Fate/Zero Season 2's central argument.

![Fate/Zero 2nd Season](https://myanimelist.net/images/anime/1522/117645l.jpg)

[Fate/Zero](https://myanimelist.net/anime/10087/Fate_Zero), written by Gen Urobuchi and adapted by ufotable, has always been read as a tragedy built around Kiritsugu Emiya's collapse — [the man who kills hundreds to save thousands](https://gamerant.com/kiritsugu-emiya-failed-hero-fatezero/), who wins the War and then destroys the prize because the prize was the problem. That reading is correct. But it focuses on the human side of the board. The servant side tells a different story, one that's grimmer and more structurally precise: the Holy Grail War is not a system that tests heroism. It is a system that *consumes* it. Every servant in Season 2 who embodies a genuine human virtue — honor, loyalty, the desire for atonement — dies because that virtue is exploitable. The one who survives is the one whose defining quality is that he cannot be touched.

## The War Has No Use for Honor

[Diarmuid Ua Duibhne](https://myanimelist.net/character/13793), summoned as Lancer, is the most literally honorable combatant in the war. His entire arc in Season 2 runs on the bitter irony that his virtue is what makes him useful to kill. Kiritsugu does not defeat Lancer in combat. He coerces [Kayneth El-Melloi Archibald](https://myanimelist.net/character/14065) at gunpoint — using Sola-Ui as leverage — and forces him to expend his final Command Spell ordering Lancer to suicide. Diarmuid drives Gáe Dearg through his own chest. As the [TYPE-MOON Wiki documents](https://typemoon.fandom.com/wiki/Diarmuid_Ua_Duibhne), he dies cursing the Holy Grail, the participants, the world.

What makes this deaths-by-virtue pattern visible is what Diarmuid actually wanted. He joined the war for a chance at recovering his honor — the legendary love spot that doomed him in life forced Sola-Ui's obsession, a repetition of the same tragedy that killed him the first time. He recognized the pattern and spent the entire war trying to be different from the man in his legend. The War would not let him. [As analyzed on The Afictionado](https://theafictionado.wordpress.com/2015/09/28/fatezero-16-chivalrys-not-dead/), "Lancer effectively went from an honorable, noble hero, to a total dark hero, burning with hate and regret." His honor was not insufficient — it was weaponizable. The more he refused to betray it, the more useful his restraint became to the people around him who had no such restraint.

Kiritsugu's pragmatism wins not by being stronger than Lancer's sword but by simply not playing the same game.

## The King Whose Love Is Not Enough

![Rider/Iskandar in Fate/Zero](https://cdn.myanimelist.net/images/characters/9/142869.jpg?s=e130372dee2406626a19b9871c526a0c)

The death of [Iskandar](https://typemoon.fandom.com/wiki/Iskandar) — summoned as Rider, the King of Conquerors — is Fate/Zero's most emotionally devastating sequence, and it is devastating in a very specific way. His Noble Phantasm, Ionioi Hetairoi, is literally a world made of bonds: [a Reality Marble](https://typemoon.fandom.com/wiki/Ionioi_Hetairoi) containing every soldier who ever followed him, all of them reconstituted as Heroic Spirits who chose to keep following him after death. His philosophy of kingship — [laid out in the famous three-kings debate in Season 1's Episode 11](https://altairandvega.wordpress.com/2011/12/12/fatezero-11-and-the-theories-of-kingship/) — is that a king's greatness is measured by those who follow him freely, not by authority or divine right. The king must be worth following. The bonds must be genuine.

Gilgamesh destroys that world. He wipes out half the army with Enuma Elish. The bounded field dissipates. Iskandar, left with nothing but Bucephalus and whatever remained of his will, charges alone. He dies alone, which is exactly the inversion of everything he believed.

[The Alexander the Great essay on The Artifice](https://the-artifice.com/fate-zero-alexander-the-great-king/) argues that Iskandar is Fate/Zero's "true king" — the one whose philosophy of leadership is most worth having. It is not wrong. Which is exactly why his death lands like it does. The show does not present Iskandar's philosophy as naive or self-deceiving. It presents it as genuinely superior — and still insufficient. Gilgamesh does not beat Iskandar because Iskandar was wrong about kingship. He beats him because Gilgamesh's nihilistic possessiveness is immune to what Iskandar was doing. Love for your comrades is a motivation. Gilgamesh's arrogance is not a motivation — it is an absence of vulnerability. You cannot threaten something he values by taking it, because he cannot be made to value things in the way Iskandar could.

The Ionian Hetairoi is the most beautiful weapon in the War. It is also the most fragile, because it is made of something real.

## The Knight Who Wanted to Be Punished

The Berserker/Lancelot reveal in Season 2 is the show's darkest twist not because it is shocking — though it is — but because of what it reveals about how the War has already destroyed two people simultaneously.

[Lancelot of the Lake](https://typemoon.fandom.com/wiki/Lancelot_(Berserker)) is summoned as the Berserker-class servant of Kariya Matou. His madness, as the TYPE-MOON Wiki explains, stems from his guilt over betraying King Arthur — he fell in love with Guinevere, whose relationship with Artoria was already a painful compromise, and his love affair triggered the collapse of Camelot. When he became a Heroic Spirit, he could not forgive himself, and that self-condemnation manifested as Berserker's insanity. He chose a class that strips him of identity and reason because he felt he deserved to be stripped of identity and reason.

When Saber finally sees him — when Lancelot, having regained his sanity after defeat, tells her that he had wished she had condemned him — the scene's weight comes from a horrible clarity. He wanted punishment. He received forgiveness. [As documented on the TYPE-MOON Wiki](https://typemoon.fandom.com/wiki/Lancelot_(Berserker)), her willingness to forgive him made it *impossible* for him to forgive himself. The mercy he was given was the wound he couldn't survive.

[Kariya Matou](https://typemoon.fandom.com/wiki/Kariya_Matou) is the human mirror of this. He entered the War to save Sakura from the Matou family — to do one selfless thing in a world that had worn him down — and instead spent the war being consumed alive by his own servant's mana drain, hallucinating, becoming something his loved ones couldn't recognize. The parallel is structural, not just thematic: Kariya is destroyed by his love for Sakura just as Lancelot is destroyed by his love for Guinevere, and both men's suffering is made worse by the people who care about them being unable to give them what they actually need. Kariya needed someone to pull Sakura out before the War started. Lancelot needed condemnation he could accept. Neither got it. His death is not even dignified enough to be tragic in the classical sense. He just stops. [As the Star Crossed Anime review notes](https://starcrossedanime.com/fatezero-season-2-review-86100/), Season 2's emotional devastation comes specifically from characters "completely breaking down with a lot of big emotions" that go nowhere — effort spent, nothing changed, no one saved.

## The Survivor Who Cannot Be Consumed

![Gilgamesh in Fate/Zero](https://cdn.myanimelist.net/images/characters/12/338672.jpg?s=371d0ab7f558421873f80899afcd2b67)

Gilgamesh survives the war for reasons that are as much symbolic as mechanical. [Animehunch explains the technical side](https://animehunch.com/how-gilgamesh-survived-at-the-end-of-fate-zero/): the Grail's corrupted mud gives him a physical body because it could not digest him. He is saturated with it, declared a foreign object, and expelled back into the world whole. He boasts about this. Of course he does.

The symbolic logic is the same. [The corrupted Grail works through Angra Mainyu](https://typemoon.fandom.com/wiki/Angra_Mainyu_(Holy_Grail)), and its particular malice is exploiting the gap between a wisher's desire and the wisher's methods — granting exactly what is asked for, by exactly the means the person's own logic permits. Kiritsugu wanted to save the world; the Grail offered to kill everyone except his family, because his calculus permits acceptable sacrifices. That gap — between desire and the cost a person is willing to pay — is what the mud finds and widens. Gilgamesh has no such gap. His desire is absolute possession. He wants everything to belong to him because he is the King of Kings and everything already belongs to him. There is nothing the mud can offer him that he does not already consider his. He is all pride and no attachment. The things he values are not things that can be taken from him; his treasures are not people.

Lancer dies because his honor was turned against him. Iskandar dies because his love created a vulnerability Gilgamesh had no equivalent of. Lancelot dies because his guilt consumed him from the inside. None of these are external defeats. They are all deaths by what made them admirable in the first place.

[As noted in the Infinite Zenith's retrospective on the series](https://infinitemirai.wordpress.com/2024/12/11/fate-zero-a-thematic-reflection-from-another-perspective-and-a-whole-series-review/), Fate/Zero positions the Holy Grail War as a system that "cannot accommodate genuine virtue." The war's mechanics — Command Spells that make servants obey against their nature, Masters who are defined by how willing they are to betray — are structurally hostile to what makes the best servants great. Lancer's honor is a liability once Kayneth holds it. Iskandar's love is a liability once Gilgamesh can aim at the thing he loves. Lancelot's guilt was a liability before the War even started.

## What Waver Keeps

The one grace note in Season 2 is Waver Velvet, and it matters that he is not a hero. He is a young magus who stole a relic to summon a servant without permission, who is embarrassed by Iskandar's overbearing personality, who spends most of the war afraid. [His Wikipedia article](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Waver_Velvet) notes that after the war he travels the world — India, Persia, Greece, Macedonia, Iskandar's historical route — before returning to the Clock Tower to rebuild the El-Melloi faction, taking on a title and a debt that wasn't his and making something from it.

He is not transformed by heroism. He is transformed by grief. [As the essay "Fakers Chasing Their King's Shadow" argues](https://thereforeitis.wordpress.com/2019/10/19/fate-zero-and-lord-el-melloi-ii-fakers-chasing-their-kings-shadow/), Waver's arc into Lord El-Melloi II is entirely about carrying what Iskandar left — not his power, not his philosophy of conquest, but the specific weight of having been loved by someone that large. Iskandar gave him his Command Seals back. Iskandar called him a friend. Iskandar died in front of him, and Waver watched the world made of bonds get destroyed.

He survives because he never claimed to be worth the Grail. He just tried not to die, and then — because that's what surviving looks like — decided to go on.

The Grail War has a survivor problem. It kills its best soldiers, the ones with honor and love and guilt deep enough to be real, and it leaves behind the arrogant and the broken. Gilgamesh gets a body and ten years to prepare for the Fifth War. Waver gets a title he didn't earn and a grief that never fully lifts.

This is what Urobuchi's tragedy-as-argument looks like in practice. Across his career he has maintained that ["good intentions, kindness, and hope will not necessarily make people happy"](https://wiki.puella-magi.net/Gen_Urobuchi) — a position he has articulated in multiple interviews about Madoka Magica and Fate/Zero alike. Season 2 is his most rigorous structural proof of that claim: not nihilism dressed up as tragedy, but a precise argument that the systems we build tend to reward exactly the wrong things, and the cost is paid by the people who deserved better. Season 2 doesn't make that argument through Kiritsugu's collapse alone. It makes it through every servant death, structured and precise, each one a proof that virtue in the Fourth Holy Grail War is not a path to victory. It is a target.
