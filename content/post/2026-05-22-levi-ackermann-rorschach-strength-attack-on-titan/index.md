Isayama Hajime confirmed it in an [early blog post](http://blog.livedoor.jp/isayamahazime/archives/2543862.html) and the fandom has treated it as a curiosity ever since: Levi Ackermann was modeled on Rorschach from *Watchmen*. Same compressed stature, same moral rigidity, same obsessive relationship with cleanliness — Isayama inverted Rorschach's legendary filth into Levi's germaphobia, but kept everything else. The trauma-forged personality. Violence as a first language. The complete absence of any social architecture that might soften impact.

This is interesting the way an author's reading list is interesting — until you think about what *Watchmen* actually does to Rorschach, and what *Attack on Titan* does instead.

In *Watchmen*, Rorschach dies. He has to. He discovers the truth of what Ozymandias has arranged and refuses to keep the secret that might prevent global catastrophe, so Dr. Manhattan disintegrates him in the snow of Antarctica. The story's logic is explicit: Rorschach cannot be allowed to live because his absolutism — pure, incapable of pragmatic compromise — would contaminate any possible peaceful future. He dies saying "do it," and Alan Moore treats this as both tragic and necessary. You cannot build a better world with a man who cannot bend.

*Attack on Titan* makes Levi survive. It lets humanity's strongest soldier bury everyone he loved, lose an eye, lose two fingers, and arrive at Chapter 139 alive — sitting in a wheelchair surrounded by orphaned Eldian children with no one left to ask if it was worth it. That is not the same story. It is, in fact, the opposite story, and it is the most revealing thing Isayama did with the character he built from Alan Moore's blueprint.

## The Underground and the Template

[Levi's origin in the Underground City](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Attack_on_Titan:_No_Regrets) — the criminal district beneath Mitras where the poor are born and die without ever seeing sky — maps almost exactly onto the Rorschach model. His mother Kuchel was a prostitute who died of illness while he was still a child. His uncle Kenny found him sitting beside her body, apparently for some time, and took him in briefly before abandoning him to fend for himself in the underground's network of theft and violence. The [manga spinoff *No Regrets*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Attack_on_Titan:_No_Regrets), written by Hikaru Suruga from an original scenario, reconstructed what came next: Levi building a micro-family from two people he found in the dark — a group of skilled thieves with no futures and no other options.

Those people — Furlan Church and Isabel Magnolia — are the first thing the story takes from him. They die on his first expedition above ground, killed by Titans while Levi was chasing what turned out to be a fabricated mission: a document that didn't exist, a target who had already anticipated him. He comes back to find two bodies in the grass and the realization that his skill, his speed, his absolute capability as a fighter, had been elsewhere when it mattered.

"No regrets" is not a philosophy Levi arrives at through reflection. It is the only frame that remains functional after the alternative collapses.

[Hiroshi Kamiya, Levi's Japanese voice actor](https://mantan-web.jp/article/20180901dog00m200005000c.html), has noted that when he was first cast, nobody in the production staff could tell him anything about Levi's backstory because it had not been written yet. The character was designed aesthetically first, emotionally second. Isayama gave him the Rorschach framework — the look, the manner, the violence — and the backstory arrived later to explain something that was already working. What was already working was the impression of a man whose entire personality is a scar that healed wrong.

## The Cost of Being the Strongest

[*Attack on Titan*'s in-universe designation](https://myanimelist.net/character/36765/Levi) — "Humanity's Strongest Soldier" — is meant to register as genuine. Levi is extraordinary in ways the series establishes with care: precision of movement, speed of decision, the ability to reconfigure a combat approach mid-motion in ways that approach the superhuman. His pursuit of the Female Titan in Season 1 is designed to answer the audience's recurring question of whether anyone in this world can fight back effectively.

The series then spends the next hundred chapters demonstrating that the answer is still no.

Petra Ral, Oluo Bozado, Gunter Schultz, Eld Jinn — his handpicked Special Operations Squad, selected precisely because they were the Survey Corps' best — all die across Chapters 26 through 28, while Annie Leonhart systematically dismantles Levi's section of the formation. [Levi arrives too late every time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Levi_Ackermann). The anime's most careful beat in this sequence: Petra's father finds Levi after the battle and tells him about the letter she sent home, about how she had written that she planned to dedicate herself to Levi's goals for as long as it took. Levi has already found her body. He has to listen to a man talk about his daughter's future in the past tense and the episode cuts before he has to respond.

This pattern continues with such regularity that it begins to read as design rather than tragedy. Hange Zoe — the division commander who becomes Levi's closest professional relationship after the original Squad — dies in the final arc in a suicide action to buy time during the Rumbling. [Erwin Smith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erwin_Smith_(Attack_on_Titan)), the commander who transformed the Survey Corps from a suicide apparatus into something resembling a purposeful institution, charges the Beast Titan with full knowledge of what he's running into. The charge kills almost everyone. Levi has to choose whether to give a regenerating serum to Erwin, who might be revivable, or Armin, whose dream is less complete. He chooses Armin, partly because Erwin's expression in that moment reads — to a man who knew him better than anyone — as wanting to be released.

Then, immediately, Zeke Yeager uses a Thunder Spear detonation to reduce Levi to what should be a corpse. [His survival is treated by the narrative almost as administrative oversight](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Levi_Ackermann): he is too structurally necessary to actually die, but the series does not make the survival clean or triumphant. His fight against Zeke had, in an earlier season, been [nominated at the 4th Crunchyroll Anime Awards for Best Fight Scene](https://www.animenewsnetwork.com/news/2020-01-08/crunchyroll-anime-awards-announces-nominees-for-2020/.154900) — the high-water mark of the series' action choreography. The Thunder Spear sequence treats the same man's body as something to be discarded. He emerges missing two fingers on one hand, partially blind in one eye, face permanently altered. The rebuild is purely internal — the same principle that put him back together after Isabel and Furlan died, scaled up to fit a larger ruin.

## The One Completed Mission

Levi makes exactly one explicit promise across the full run of the series, and he keeps it. He promises Erwin's memory that he will kill the Beast Titan.

[He does, in Chapter 134](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Attack_on_Titan), in the story's final arc. Zeke Yeager dies, finally, at the hands of the man who spent three arcs getting close enough to finish it. The narrative does not treat this as triumph. Zeke has been complicit in enough coordinated mass death — the massacre of Survey Corps soldiers by thunder spears, the plan to sterilize Eldians through the royal bloodline, Erwin's charge into the Beast Titan's barrage — that killing him registers as settling an account rather than achieving anything. The bill is paid. The people who would have wanted to see it paid are already dead.

[Matthew Mercer, Levi's English voice actor](https://www.comingsoon.net/anime/news/1198354-matthew-mercer-attack-on-titan-levi-ackerman-interview), described the role as "an absolute joy to perform — this extremely complicated, dark, intense, and still kind of heroic character" who is "forced to take dark decisions to survive." What Mercer identified as moral ambiguity is real, but it exists in the observer, not in Levi's mind. Levi has never been morally ambiguous from the inside. His decisions are brutal and clear. The ambiguity is that his clarity does not prevent loss; it just lets him function while the loss accumulates, professionally, across the entire runtime.

## What Rorschach Could Not Be Asked

Here is where Isayama's adaptation of the template diverges most sharply.

Watchmen identifies Rorschach as a problem to be solved — a contaminating absolute in a narrative about moral compromise as the necessary cost of preventing catastrophe. Alan Moore has him disintegrated in the snow because keeping him alive would be an argument for his worldview. You cannot build a better world with a man like that. Removing him is the story's ethical position stated as plot.

Isayama does not remove his Rorschach. [Chapter 139](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Attack_on_Titan) shows Levi post-war, alive, apparently caring for a small group of Eldian orphan children. He asks — aloud, to no one present — whether Erwin and Hange are watching. He receives no answer. The reader knows both are dead. The reader knows Levi knows this. The question is not for them. It is the closest the series ever shows Levi to the ordinary human need for someone to witness what you survived.

What *Attack on Titan* was asking, by keeping its Rorschach alive into the aftermath, is the question *Watchmen* could not follow through on: what does the violent absolutist do when the war ends and his absolutism has nowhere to point? Rorschach's principle was "no compromises, ever" — a statement that only has operational meaning when there is something to refuse to compromise with. Levi's principle, articulated as "no regrets," faces the same structural problem: what does it mean to have no regrets when the things you regret would have required a different person to prevent?

[In the 36th Anime Grand Prix](https://www.animenewsnetwork.com/news/2013-10-13/attack-on-titan-wins-top-prizes-in-newtype-anime-awards), Levi won Best Male Character. [Funimation named him one of the "Best Boys of the Decade" for the 2010s](https://blog.funimation.com/2019/12/27/a-decade-of-anime-best-girl-best-boy-my-hero-academia-re-zero/) — a decade-long catalogue vote that placed him alongside characters with dramatically different narrative functions. [On MyAnimeList, he ranks third among all favorited characters](https://myanimelist.net/character.php) in the database — behind only Lelouch Lamperouge and Monkey D. Luffy, characters defined by grand plans and decisive outcomes. The third most popular anime character of all time is a man whose defining characteristic is that his strength was never enough to protect the people he cared about, and who survived anyway, and who is asking the dead if they can see him.

That is not the audience admiring power. That is the audience recognizing something. Levi's popularity is a reading of the arc that is more honest than any "badass soldier" framing could account for. People respond to the structure of what the series is actually doing to him — the accumulation, the refusal to give him redemption through dramatic sacrifice, the specific cruelty of "humanity's strongest" losing everyone around him while remaining operational.

Watchmen solved Rorschach by removing him. *Attack on Titan*'s more difficult, more honest maneuver was to keep Levi alive past any point where life felt like reward, and leave the question open. The man survives. The series never says whether that was the point or just the part that couldn't be helped. The question Levi asks toward an absent Erwin at the story's end gets no answer.

That is, finally, the most Rorschach thing about it.
