The title of Nanashi's manga is a passive construction. *Don't Toy with Me, Miss Nagatoro* names the action from the perspective of its recipient. The whole premise positions Senpai — introverted, unnamed for most of the series, eventually revealed as Naoto Hachioji — as the object being acted upon, and Nagatoro as the subject doing the acting. The series reinforces this at every level: the POV is primarily his, the comedy comes from his reactions, and the conventional takeaway is that a shy boy gets dragged out of his shell by an aggressive girl who turns out to like him. That reading isn't wrong. It's just incomplete. The more closely you follow Nagatoro's actual arc across the manga's [154 chapters](https://nagatoro.fandom.com/wiki/Chapter_154), the harder it becomes to argue that Senpai's coming-of-age is the story's real center. The real center is his tormenter.

## The Conventional Reading and Its Limits

The standard interpretation of *Nagatoro* goes roughly like this: girl discovers terrified boy in empty art room, decides he's funny to mess with, begins a teasing relationship that gradually softens into genuine romance while Senpai gains enough confidence to assert himself. [Anime News Network's coverage](https://www.animenewsnetwork.com/this-week-in-anime/2021-06-14/.173952) of the anime's first season engaged heavily with whether Nagatoro's behavior crossed the line into cruelty — which tells you exactly where viewer attention was focused: on what she was doing to him. [Rice Digital described it](https://ricedigital.co.uk/miss-nagatoros-fresh-take-on-young-love/) as "two kids who clearly have feelings for each other attempting to start a romantic relationship without being the one to actually talk about it explicitly." Accurate enough for a capsule summary. But it still frames the relationship as the story, with both characters equally caught in its orbit.

Senpai's growth is real. He starts the series barely able to look Nagatoro in the eyes and ends it capable of giving a graduation speech. The [CBR piece on the sadodere archetype](https://www.cbr.com/dont-toy-with-me-miss-nagatoro-sadodere-type/) describes the series as documenting how "indirect communication paradoxically reveals authentic connection" — and most of the discourse runs with that framing, treating Nagatoro as the engine and Senpai as the story being driven.

The problem is that Nagatoro doesn't behave like a supporting character. She behaves like someone working something out.

## Brilliant, Lazy, and Running

Before she ever walked into that art room, Nagatoro had already quit something. As the [Nagatoro Wiki documents](https://nagatoro.fandom.com/wiki/Hayase_Nagatoro), she was a naturally gifted judoka who coasted on talent until Orihara defeated her decisively — and then she panicked and abandoned the sport entirely. This detail arrives mid-series and immediately recontextualizes everything before it. The girl who projects total confidence, who never loses her footing around Senpai, who teases him specifically for being timid — she had already run from a challenge when it got serious.

[TV Tropes categorizes this](https://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/Characters/DontToyWithMeMissNagatoro) as "Brilliant but Lazy," which is accurate but undersells the psychological dimension. Nagatoro's cockiness isn't just personality quirk. It's armor. She never trained seriously because she never had to — and when she finally met someone she couldn't defeat on talent alone, she decided the sport wasn't worth it rather than confront what the defeat revealed about her. That is not the behavior of a character who arrived already complete, ready to catalyze someone else's growth. That is the behavior of someone still figuring out who they are.

Her relationship with Senpai, viewed through this lens, looks different. [Dave Dalesio's character analysis](https://davedalessiowrites.wordpress.com/2022/09/18/character-analysis-hayase-nagatoro/) focuses on a telling design detail: Nagatoro wears modest swimwear around everyone except Hachioji, reserving provocative bikinis and crop tops exclusively for him. The interpretation is about adolescent sexuality — she's using him as a safe space to explore something new. But the same logic applies more broadly. He's non-threatening. She can practice being vulnerable, in every sense, with someone who poses no real risk to her image. This isn't dominance play; it's a girl who picked an arena where she couldn't lose before she'd learned how to lose. The power dynamic was never as lopsided as it looked. She needed those interactions as much as he did.

## The Arc She Actually Has

The judo arc is where the series makes its real argument. When Nagatoro returns to the sport, it's initially for revenge — she wants to beat Orihara. [The wiki is explicit about this](https://nagatoro.fandom.com/wiki/Hayase_Nagatoro): revenge was the entry point, not any genuine love for judo. But as the arc progresses, something shifts. She trains seriously for the first time. She loses to Orihara again, but shakes her hand and promises to meet her again. The [Animixly analysis](https://animixly.com/articles/analysis-dont-toy-with-me-miss-nagatoro/) identifies this as Nagatoro "finally stopping to rely on her natural talent to actually train and become better" — and that shift is enormous in the context of who she's been. This is a character choosing difficulty over avoidance for the first time in the story.

Senpai encourages her through this, and [KawaiiRealm's examination of the series' dynamics](https://kawaiirealm.com/articles/depths-of-relationships-in-nagatoro/) makes the point that the power dynamic "is constantly flipping" — this arc is the clearest evidence. Nagatoro is the one who needs support here. She's vulnerable in a way that her teasing of Senpai never was; when she's on the mat against a real opponent, there's no performance, no retreat into playful cruelty. She has to actually show up.

[Nanashi, in his creator interview with Anime Corner](https://animecorner.me/interview-dont-toy-with-me-miss-nagatoro-creator-nanashi/), described his approach as imagining "what would make Senpai happy" when developing scenarios. That's revealing about where his conscious attention sat. But writers produce richer material than their stated intentions. What he built in Nagatoro's judo arc is a complete coming-of-age trajectory: talent, avoidance, forced confrontation, genuine discipline, and earned respect for an opponent she once only wanted to defeat. It's a more fully realized arc than "shy boy gets teased into confidence."

## What the Ending Makes Undeniable

[The final chapter](https://nagatoro.fandom.com/wiki/Chapter_154) is 41 pages — the longest chapter of the series — and it's structured around two graduation speeches. Nagatoro announces her dream: the Olympic judo team. Senpai tells the assembled students that his high school years taught him "the world could be nerve-wracking but also fun and beautiful," and that this had given him "courage to look forward despite not having a clear dream yet."

She has a dream. He doesn't. Three years of a romance framed as his growth story, and at the finish line, the teasing girl is the one who arrived somewhere.

This isn't a knock on Senpai — his growth is real, and the ending wisely refuses to force a false resolution on him. He's heading to university in Tokyo to figure things out, which is honest. But the structural weight of the final chapter lands on Nagatoro's declaration. That's where Nanashi put the emotional stakes. [Fan reactions on the MangaDex forums](https://forums.mangadex.org/threads/ijiranaide-nagatoro-san-vol-20-ch-154-senpaii-nagatoro.1825849/) noted the finale felt somewhat abrupt and that some readers wanted more romantic closure — a common complaint about any ending — but Nagatoro's announcement is the one thing that doesn't feel rushed. It lands because it was built toward across the entire second half of the manga.

When [ANN announced the series was ending in three chapters in mid-2024](https://www.animenewsnetwork.com/news/2024-06-03/dont-toy-with-me-miss-nagatoro-manga-ends-in-3-chapters/.211500), [AniList forum discussions](https://anilist.co/forum/thread/75609) worried whether the current arc could give the conclusion proper weight. But nobody expressed surprise that Nagatoro's judo ambitions were where the series was heading. That part felt earned.

## Why the Misreading Sticks

The [AniTAY essay on the wave of teasing-romance manga](https://medium.com/anitay-official/of-course-you-can-step-on-i-mean-tease-me-the-age-of-tease-is-upon-us-22ae0ba9d679) situates *Nagatoro* alongside *Takagi-san* and *Uzaki-chan* as series where teasing is the primary love language. Within that genre frame, Nagatoro is almost always analyzed as a delivery mechanism: she creates the tension, she generates the embarrassment, she drives the slow burn. She's the teaser. That's her function.

There's a legitimate version of the Senpai-as-protagonist argument. His POV gets far more page time. His reactions generate most of the comedy. His graduation speech — about learning the world could be "nerve-wracking but also fun and beautiful" — is as emotionally sincere as hers, just less anchored to a concrete destination. [MyAnimeGuru's analysis of the series](https://myanimeguru.com/unveiling-hayase-nagatoro-the-art-of-teasing-and-transformation/) describes his growth as a gradual "emergence into confidence," which is accurate; the person who can assert himself in the final chapters is genuinely different from the one who couldn't look up from his sketchbook. You can make a case.

But *Nagatoro* — unlike *Takagi-san*, where Takagi arrives essentially complete and is cheerfully guiding a dense boy toward the obvious — spends real ink on who Nagatoro is when she's not performing. The judo arc is not a side story. It is the explicit account of her confronting the same failure-and-avoidance pattern that Senpai's arc is usually credited with resolving in him. The ending exists to show that the person in this story who figured out what she wanted to do with her life was her, not him.

The title is a passive construction. The POV is primarily his. The comedy is his reactions. All of this pulls attention toward Senpai, which is probably why the conventional reading sticks so hard. But Nagatoro was never simply what she did to him. She was someone who had run from difficulty, found a safe person to practice being real with, and spent three years actually earning the confidence she'd been performing all along. The title is hers. So is the arc.

She just had to do most of the actual work to deserve it.
