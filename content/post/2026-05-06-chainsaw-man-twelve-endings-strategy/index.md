The easy dismissal of Chainsaw Man's ending strategy goes like this: MAPPA hired twelve popular acts, got twelve viral moments, walked away with twelve YouTube embeds. Marketing dressed up as art. The series already had "KICK BACK" by Kenshi Yonezu as its opening — [a song that racked up over 100 million views](https://milanrecords.com/j-pop-singer-songwriter-and-producer-kenshi-yonezu-releases-opening-theme-for-new-anime-series-chainsaw-man/) and became the first Japanese-language track to earn RIAA Platinum certification — so stacking twelve endings on top was just more of the same momentum.

That reading is wrong, and the wrong parts are the interesting ones.

The twelve endings — one per episode, each by a different artist, each with unique animation tailored to that episode's content — are [unprecedented in a Jump adaptation of this scale](https://letsweaboo.com/news/chainsaw-man-had-a-new-ending-theme-in-every-episode-but-what-was-the-point_a147). They are also a coherent artistic statement. Each ending functions as a standalone epilogue: a coda that reframes what just happened before the next episode begins. The variety isn't superficial. It mirrors something essential about what Chainsaw Man is.

## The First Episode's Black Screen

The initial shock, for anyone watching in Fall 2022, was that Episode 1 had no ending at all. [Just rolling credits against black](https://www.cbr.com/every-end-credit-sequence-chainsaw-man-season-1-explained/). No music, no animation, no announcement of the visual feast to come. This was director Ryu Nakayama making his intentions clear before he'd committed to anything else.

Nakayama, a first-time series director who [MAPPA CEO Manabu Otsuka specifically chose to "inject new blood"](https://www.slashfilm.com/1187583/why-the-company-behind-chainsaw-man-wanted-ryu-nakayama-to-direct-the-series/) into the adaptation, had one central goal: bring cinema closer to anime. His explicit [cinematic reference approach](https://www.animenewsnetwork.com/interest/2022-10-11/the-film-references-in-the-chainsaw-man-opening/.190724) was already evident in the OP sequence's 15+ movie allusions. But the black screen at the end of Episode 1 goes further. In film, many opening chapters end on silence. *No Country for Old Men* ends its opening segment with Anton Chigurh's arrest and no music. The mood survives in stillness. Chainsaw Man's first episode introduced Pochita, Denji, his father's debt, and the massacre that creates Chainsaw Man — and then went quiet. The silence wasn't absence. It was restraint.

From Episode 2 onward, the restraint broke, and each episode got its own world.

## How the System Works

The [twelve artists](https://epicstream.com/article/all-chainsaw-man-ending-songs-list-artists-spotify-more) span enormous stylistic range: Vaundy's indie pop, ZUTOMAYO's hyperpop, Maximum the Hormone's erratic metal, TK from Ling tosite sigure's post-rock, Aimer's orchestral balladry, Eve's melancholy folk-pop, Queen Bee's electro-cabaret. Each one was selected not just for popularity — though many of these are major acts in contemporary Japanese music — but for fit. The MAPPA team created unique ending animations for eleven of the twelve episodes (Episode 1's credits being the exception), each specifically keyed to the episode's emotional aftermath.

Episode 2's ending opens on a heartbeat monitor, then traces the cast through their respective circumstances as dominoes fall — encoding Makima's manipulation before the audience has understood who Makima is. Episode 4's Power sequence uses rapid costume changes — police officer, cheerleader, soldier — to visualize her chaos and her compensatory braggadocio. [Episode 7's retro RPG aesthetic](https://www.cbr.com/every-end-credit-sequence-chainsaw-man-season-1-explained/), ending on a group toast with the whole Division 4 team, is the most devastating choice in the entire run: it creates a moment of false warmth three minutes before the audience realizes they've been watching a last supper.

## Episode 8 and What "First Death" Does

The conversation about the ending strategy almost always pivots to Episode 8, and for good reason. ["First Death" by TK from Ling tosite sigure](https://www.dualshockers.com/chainsaw-man-best-ending-themes-ranked/) arrives after the massacre that ends Himeno — a character the series spent seven episodes making you care about, killed in the space of a few pages of manga time, with almost no ceremony. The episode is titled "Gunfire." It earns the name.

TK's music is suited to exactly one emotional register: devastation dressed as beauty. His ["Unravel" from Tokyo Ghoul](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unravel_(TK_song)) — still his most-streamed track over a decade after its 2014 release — established his signature: post-rock structures that surge and collapse under vocal weight, beauty wrapped around something that can't be fixed. "First Death" carries the same DNA. It moves with the energy of an opening theme — driving, propulsive — but the visual track intercuts the battle's aftermath with scenes of Aki and Himeno together, showing what was just destroyed alongside the act of destruction. The title names what happened before any of the characters can articulate it. First Death. There will be more.

This is what distinguishes the endings from marketing: they do interpretive work the episode itself doesn't do. The episode depicts what happens. The ending tells you what it means.

## The Religious Iconography Problem

Episodes 3 and 5 form a deliberate diptych. In Episode 3, Makima appears in the ending as a Virgin Mary figure — [ascending against a kaleidoscopic background, Denji bowing below her](https://www.cbr.com/chainsaw-man-end-credit-sequence-elevates-storytelling/). Episode 5 extends this: Makima and Denji are posed as [Michelangelo's Pietà](https://www.cbr.com/every-end-credit-sequence-chainsaw-man-season-1-explained/), the sculpture of Mary holding the crucified Christ. At the time these episodes aired, most viewers had no reason to interrogate Makima's role beyond "mysterious authority figure who is nice to Denji." The endings were encoding the answer — she is not his savior, she is his Madonna, his manufactured purpose, the vacuum his desire pours into — weeks before the narrative gave you language to describe it.

This is the most sophisticated function the endings serve: foreshadowing that reads differently in retrospect. A viewer watching Episode 3 for the first time sees religious imagery and notes it as thematic texture. A viewer who knows how Part 1 ends watches the same sequence and sees Fujimoto's entire thesis about Denji's condition — his worship of a woman designed to exploit his desire for unconditional love — compressed into thirty seconds of animation.

## Why the Variety Is the Point

*Chainsaw Man* is [not a genre-stable work](https://screenrant.com/chainsaw-man-change-shonen-forever-pushed-boundaries-denji/). It is a workplace comedy until it's a horror film. It's a horror film until it's a romantic tragedy. It's a romantic tragedy until it's an action thriller. Fujimoto's manga switches registers without apology, and part of what makes it work is that the tonal whiplash is the texture — the series is about a person with no stable footing, and the genre instability represents his lived experience.

The twelve endings are the formal analog to that instability. Having a consistent ending theme across a cour imposes a kind of tonal continuity — you finish every episode in the same register. Twelve different artists in twelve different genres mean you can never predict what state you'll be left in. Episode 7's pixelated RPG warmth gives way to Episode 8's post-rock devastation. Episode 9 uses Aimer's vocal lament over the image of [Himeno's limbs fading as she walks away](https://www.cbr.com/chainsaw-man-end-credit-sequence-elevates-storytelling/) — the most purely grief-oriented sequence in the run. Episode 11 shifts to something almost clinical.

![Chainsaw Man Denji character visual](https://cdn.myanimelist.net/images/characters/7/522168.jpg)

The variety mirrors the series. That's not an accident.

## What the Reze Arc Movie Confirms

When the Reze Arc movie was announced, one of the first details [confirmed by MAPPA was a unique ending strategy](https://fandomwire.com/chainsaw-man-the-movie-reze-arc-reveals-ending-song-confirming-mappa-isnt-changing-one-strategy/). The studio isn't treating the twelve-endings approach as a one-off stunt. It's a house style decision, carried forward into theatrical releases, by a team that understood what made it work the first time.

Nakayama eventually [departed MAPPA for Studio Mayflower](https://comicbook.com/anime/news/chainsaw-man-anime-director-studio-mayflower/) and won't direct future Chainsaw Man productions. MAPPA kept the ending strategy anyway. That's the actual proof point: the original director is gone, but the formal decision he championed survived him at the studio. You don't institutionalize a marketing gimmick. You institutionalize something that turned out to be genuinely understood by the people making it.

The twelve endings worked because MAPPA read the source material carefully enough to understand that this was a series whose content was fundamentally about the instability of experience. Fujimoto writes manga that refuses to hold still — tonally, generically, emotionally. The endings were the adaptation's formal response to that refusal: we won't hold still either. Every episode ends differently because every episode *is* different. That's not a gimmick. That's comprehension.
