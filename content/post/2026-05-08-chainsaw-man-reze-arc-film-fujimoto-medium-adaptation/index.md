Tatsuki Fujimoto drew the Reze Arc. He wrote the ending. He chose every beat, every panel, every cut. He is not a passive participant in what happens to these characters — he is the person who decided it.

After watching MAPPA's film adaptation in 2025, Fujimoto reportedly could not sleep. He questioned why Makima had to intervene. He said, in conversation with his editor Shihei Lin, that [he had "forgotten" he knew the outcome](https://www.toonpoor.com/2025/12/tatsuki-fujimoto-reacted-as-spectator.html). He experienced his own work as a member of the audience.

Then he drew an alternate happy ending: Denji carrying Reze, who holds the bouquet he originally bought her. He released it as a theatrical bonus for Japanese audiences and stated publicly that he "couldn't stop thinking about Reze-chan" after the film. He had also said, in the same period, that he liked when protagonists and heroines don't meet again. [Both statements appear in the same media cycle](https://www.cbr.com/chainsaw-man-denji-reze-arc-happy-ending-fujimoto-movie-art/), and they are not a contradiction. They are the full account.

This is the most unusual creative event in Chainsaw Man's history, and it tells you everything important about what MAPPA's film did to this story that the manga, for all its extraordinary power, could not.

## What the Manga Did

The Reze Arc in the manga is twelve chapters of compressed devastation. Fujimoto introduced a girl working at a café, built a relationship with Denji through mundane details — cooking, swimming, a telephone booth listening to music together — and then revealed that she is a Soviet human-weapons project, a Bomb Devil hybrid dispatched to steal Chainsaw Man's heart. Her intimacy was mission-designed. Her departure — Makima's assassins intercepting her at the harbor before her escape boat — ends the arc before Denji can reach her.

The manga's pace is controlled by the reader. Each chapter reveals information at a rate you set by turning pages. The love story and the mission briefing exist in the same panels, and you process them at your own speed. The gap between the telephone booth scene (warmth, connection, a moment that feels genuinely mutual) and the revelation of Reze's true purpose is as wide or as narrow as you read it. Fujimoto draws the arc to be devastating, but its devastation operates through accumulation — pages of ordinary life, then the revelation, then the ending.

[Fujimoto cited *Before Sunrise* as his primary structural inspiration](https://www.imdb.com/news/ni65544740/), specifically how "the emotional distance between the man and woman gradually shrinks." The telephone booth scene directly mirrors the record store listening booth in Linklater's film. He also cited [*Jin-Roh: The Wolf Brigade*](https://www.animesenpai.net/chainsaw-man-author-reveals-the-film-that-inspired-the-upcoming-reze-arc/) — a soldier falling for a girl who self-detonates — as the structural template for the Denji/Reze dynamic. Both of these are films. Fujimoto was, in some sense, writing a film arc inside a manga. MAPPA completing that circuit was not just adaptation — it was formal completion.

## What MAPPA Changed

The most immediately visible difference between MAPPA's film and the original Chainsaw Man Season 1 is aesthetic. [As SlashFilm documents in detail](https://www.slashfilm.com/2016560/why-chainsaw-man-the-movie-reze-arcs-looks-different-show/), Season 1 director Ryu Nakayama pursued an "animating live-action" philosophy: muted naturalism, hidden linework, cinematic realism. The Reze Arc film, directed by Tatsuya Yoshihara, broke from this deliberately — thicker lines, brighter colors, restored character designs closer to Fujimoto's distinctive manga penciling. Makima's hair changed from light pink to bright red for stronger visual impact.

This is not simply a stylistic preference. Season 1's visual approach produced a Chainsaw Man that many Japanese fans felt was misaligned with the manga — [BD sales significantly underperformed relative to MAPPA's other major productions](https://www.avclub.com/chainsaw-man-the-movie-reze-arcs-outsized-impact-anime-industry), which contributed to the studio's decision to recalibrate both visually and commercially with the film. Yoshihara's shift toward "more anime-style techniques" was a formal argument: Fujimoto's work requires a visual language closer to its source, not translation into naturalism. The thicker lines aren't decoration — they are the same emotional insistence that makes Fujimoto's panels feel immediate rather than observed.

[MAPPA also added material not present in the manga](https://www.cbr.com/chainsaw-man-movie-manga-changes-additions/): a recurring spider web motif (Reze as spider, Denji as moth drawn toward her), airplane imagery that runs through the film as a symbol of her mission looming over her desire for escape, and an expanded beach scene that gives Reze more interior space than the manga allowed. These additions are the film's bid to do what the manga's compression prevented: give Reze a recognizable inner life rather than a revealed mission.

The [production pipeline was itself unusual](https://80.lv/articles/see-how-denji-vs-reze-fight-animation-sequence-from-chainsaw-man-the-movie-reze-arc-was-turned-from-3d-to-2d/): MAPPA used Blender to construct scenes in 3D before converting them into 2D animation, a hybrid approach that maintained fluidity in the action sequences while allowing the character work to feel drawn rather than rendered. The Denji vs. Reze fight is technically extraordinary. But the pipeline exists to keep performances readable at cinema scale — where a face occupying a large screen cannot afford the slight wooden quality that 3D-sourced animation sometimes produces at television size. The technical choice serves the emotional one.

## What Film Does That Manga Cannot

The key difference is temporal. Manga is controlled by the reader. A two-page spread of Reze and Denji in the telephone booth can be absorbed in three seconds or thirty; the reader determines how long the warmth lasts before they turn the page. The revelation of Reze's true mission arrives when the reader turns to it. Grief is self-paced.

Film removes that control. The 120-minute runtime is not determined by the viewer. [The *Arts Fuse* review describes it as "action spectacle and character melodrama"](https://artsfuse.org/319160/film-review-chainsaw-man-the-movie-reze-arc-or-how-i-learned-to-stop-worrying-and-love-the-bomb-girl/) that diagnoses exploitation "better than any Marvel film" — and part of what makes that work is the fixed duration: the warmth lasts exactly as long as the director decides it should, and the cuts between warmth and violence are not in your hands. Anime on a cinema screen, at cinema runtime, with cinema's temporal sovereignty over its audience, produces a different emotional experience than the same events across twelve manga chapters.

[*Rotten Tomatoes* aggregates the critical consensus](https://www.rottentomatoes.com/m/chainsaw_man_the_movie_reze_arc) at 96%: "a bloody feast rendered in eye-popping animation... revving up the carnage while maintaining a perversely sweet emotional core." The "perversely sweet" is the operating phrase. The sweetness is perverse precisely because the film doesn't let you linger in it the way manga does. The pace is the instrument of the wound.

This is what Fujimoto encountered when he watched it. He had written the arc with a mangaka's relationship to time — deciding panel by panel, controlling each beat's duration through layout. MAPPA's film placed the same events on a fixed timeline, gave Reze's interior life room the compressed chapters hadn't, and presented the ending at an appointed moment after approximately two hours of earned warmth. The [*GamingTrend* review's reading of Denji as "a groomed mouse experiencing silent emotional abuse"](https://gamingtrend.com/reviews/chainsaw-man-the-movie-reze-arc-review-the-groomed-mouse-learns-to-love-and-swim/) sharpens the tragedy: the film makes visible the full weight of what Denji loses, which is not just a girl but the shape of a life he might have had. Fujimoto had written this. He had not experienced it. Not at cinema speed, with cinema sound, without the ability to slow down.

## The Country Mouse Who Couldn't Escape

The Town Mouse and Country Mouse allegory that Fujimoto built into the arc runs underneath all of it. [The CBR breakdown of the quote](https://www.cbr.com/chainsaw-man-reze-arc-town-mouse-country-mouse-quote-explained/) explains the central irony: Reze identifies as the Country Mouse — someone who prefers safety and quiet over ambition. She wants to leave. She wants a café, stillness, the version of ordinary life she was never allowed. Denji, choosing Town Mouse (experience, chaos, risk), can't follow her there.

But Makima also prefers the Country Mouse. [She prefers it because its disposition makes it easier to control.](https://www.cbr.com/chainsaw-man-reze-arc-town-mouse-country-mouse-quote-explained/) The Country Mouse's desire for safety is the property that makes it tractable, that makes it choose the warm trap over the dangerous freedom. Reze's choice to flee is also, on a different level, the Country Mouse default — she wants the quiet life, which is exactly what Makima's system wants to offer and then own. The film makes this double-bind visible: Reze cannot escape toward safety because safety is what the system uses to control. The expanded beach scene — added by MAPPA, not present in the manga — gives this extra weight: we see Reze wanting stillness before the arc's violence makes stillness impossible, and the added screen time makes the impossibility hurt in a way the manga's compression left implicit.

[Fujimoto confirmed at Jump Festa that Reze is his favorite character](https://fandomwire.com/its-a-challenge-for-me-to-draw-a-cute-girl-tatsuki-fujimoto-went-out-of-his-comfort-zone-for-his-favorite-chainsaw-man-character/) — that drawing a "cute girl" was outside his comfort zone and he found it "a challenge, like an assignment." He drew her, killed her, and then when the film arrived he couldn't stop thinking about her. The creator who said "I like when the protagonist and heroine don't meet again" then drew them meeting again, in a private illustration, in an alternate life the canon does not permit.

![Fujimoto drew an alternate happy ending for theatrical-goers — Denji carrying Reze, who holds the bouquet he bought her — a non-canon illustration that functions as the author's own grief made visible](https://www.cbr.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/09/chainsaw-man-reze-arc-denji-happy-ending.jpg)

The film is [100% MAPPA's financial risk](https://respawn.outlookindia.com/pop-culture/pop-culture-news/mappa-president-on-why-chainsaw-man-was-100-studio-funded) — self-funded through the Cool Japan Fund with no production committee, the studio retaining full creative control and all profits. President Manabu Otsuka called this the fulfillment of his reason for choosing animation. [It hit #1 at the US box office](https://www.avclub.com/chainsaw-man-the-movie-reze-arcs-outsized-impact-anime-industry) on opening weekend and has crossed $174 million worldwide. The gamble worked, in every commercial sense.

But the more consequential thing it did was make Fujimoto feel what his readers felt. He designed the loss. The film made him experience it. That is the argument for adaptation — not that film improves manga, not that MAPPA's visual choices are superior to Fujimoto's pages, but that a different medium can return a creator to their own work as a stranger and show them what they made from the outside.

Reze exits the story. Fujimoto said he likes it when protagonists and heroines don't meet again. He also drew them together at the café. The alternate ending is non-canon. It is also the truest thing he made about her.
