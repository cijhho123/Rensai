The man who becomes Koro-sensei was called The Reaper. He was born into a slum with no name, learned that money and killing were the only reliable constants, and spent decades becoming the world's most feared assassin. He took a student — the orphaned son of a target — and trained the boy with complete indifference to his psychological wellbeing, treating him as a tool. When the training was done, the student betrayed him, handed him over to the government, and stole his name. The government put him in a laboratory and subjected him to antimatter experiments. He was not expected to survive. He did.

During his captivity, one person talked to him as if he were a person. [Her name was Aguri Yukimura](https://loveinterest.fandom.com/wiki/Aguri_Yukimura). She was the teacher assigned to the government's Class E — the original one, running alongside the experiments — and she came to the laboratory late at night to speak with the creature in the tank. She gave him a birthday. She told him she liked his tentacles. She listened to what he said. They spoke like this for a year. When he escaped and she died in the chaos — impaled on a trap she had triggered trying to protect the students she was responsible for — she asked him with her last words to take care of her class.

He became Koro-sensei. And the student who had betrayed him became the Second Reaper.

## The Same Person, Two Outcomes

The Second Reaper — the student who betrayed him and claimed his name — is not introduced as a monster. He is introduced as a casualty. Like Koro-sensei, he was a person of exceptional ability who was used as a tool and discarded when convenient. Like Koro-sensei, he experienced betrayal at the hands of someone he trusted. Like Koro-sensei, he carried that trauma into everything he did afterward.

The difference is what happened between the trauma and the aftermath. Koro-sensei had Aguri. The Second Reaper had no one.

[Assassination Classroom](https://myanimelist.net/anime/28977/Ansatsu_Kyoushitsu) is generally read as a series about education — and it is, at one level. [Yusei Matsui's stated premise](https://www.animenewsnetwork.com/interview/2016-10-18/assassination-classroom-yusei-matsui/.107576) involves students who must kill their teacher, and the ironic paradox that doing so requires the very growth Koro-sensei is engineering. The series is an extended argument about what good teaching looks like, and it makes that argument well. But underneath the education thesis, there is a more fundamental claim the series is making, and the two Reapers are where it becomes visible: whether a person becomes capable of care or incapable of it often comes down to whether a single person was willing to treat them as worth knowing. Not a system. Not a curriculum. One person.

## What Aguri Actually Did

The year of nightly conversations between the creature in the tank and the teacher who shouldn't have been there is the series' most quietly devastating passage. [Koro-sensei's wiki documentation](https://ansatsukyoshitsu.fandom.com/wiki/Korosensei) captures the structural facts — the late-night talks, the birthday, the growing emotional attachment — but the significance is harder to locate in summary. What Aguri did was specific: she refused to treat him as what the institution said he was.

The government classified him as an experimental subject and potential weapon. The laboratory classified him as a test case. The world had always classified him as a threat, an asset, or an obstacle. Aguri classified him as someone worth talking to. She was curious about him as a person rather than interested in him as a specimen. She did not change him by arguing with him or presenting evidence for his worth — she changed him by simply acting on the assumption that the question was already answered.

This is the mechanism the series is arguing for. [What makes Assassination Classroom a tragedy](https://gamerant.com/what-makes-assassination-classroom-a-tragedy/), according to the most precise readings, is not Koro-sensei's death — it is the distance between who he was and who he could have been, and the understanding that the only thing that bridged that distance was one year of conversations with a woman who chose to have them. The series does not argue that education saves people. It argues that being seen by one person — being treated as worth the attention — changes the trajectory of a damaged life. One year was enough.

## The Face He Learned to Make

[The Second Reaper, by contrast](https://ansatsukyoshitsu.fandom.com/wiki/The_Reaper), had no Aguri. He received betrayal, found confirmation for everything he had always believed about other people, and continued in the direction those beliefs pointed. He became more efficient, more cold, more thoroughly alone. When the series presents him as a threat, what it is actually presenting is the counterfactual: this is what happens when nobody shows up.

Koro-sensei's expressiveness — the color-coded face cycling through emotions at Mach speed, the exaggerated reactions to tangerines and bare feet, the face the series uses for most of its comedy — needs to be understood against the Second Reaper's blankness. The Reaper, before Aguri, trained himself to suppress everything. He did not feel, because feeling was a liability. The creature who emerged from the laboratory with tentacles and a permanent smile is not naturally expressive. He is a man who was taught that suppression was survival, given one year of permission to feel, and has been overcorrecting ever since. [The expressiveness is learned](https://screenanarchy.com/2016/10/interview-assassination-classroom-creator-matsui-yusei-on-movie-adaptations-and-saying-goodb.html). The comedy that runs through the entire series — the face cycling through shock, joy, embarrassment — is the portrait of someone who cannot stop feeling now that he started, who does not know how to modulate yet, who loves being alive in a way that is indistinguishable from grief.

## Class 3-E as the Reproduction of One Year

What Koro-sensei does for Class 3-E is structurally identical to what Aguri did for him: he treats as worth his attention people who the institution has declared unworthy of it.

[The Class E system at Kunugigaoka Junior High](https://ansatsu-database.tumblr.com/post/117414628805/the-class-e-system-a-satire-of-real-life) is built on what Principal Asano calls the "worker ant ratio": a bottom 20% whose visible failure motivates the middle 60%. The End Class is not a remedial program — it is a display case. The students exist to be looked down on. That is their institutional function.

[Asano constructed this from genuine grief](https://ansatsukyoshitsu.fandom.com/wiki/Gakuho_Asano): one of his students was bullied to suicide, and Asano concluded that his kindness had left the boy too gentle to survive. The wrong lesson, sincerely drawn. The system he built to prevent that outcome produces exactly it — students who treat Class E with contempt because contempt is the only way to manage their own terror of becoming Class E. [The A-class students are not confident](https://catharsisofsoul.medium.com/the-birth-of-discrimination-the-psychology-of-the-a-class-assassination-classroom-ef1788803c64); they are panicked into performance. Asano made more of the thing he was trying to prevent. The difference between his approach and Koro-sensei's is the same as the difference between the laboratory and the nightly conversations: one treats its subjects as what the institution says they are, the other refuses to.

What Koro-sensei introduces into this system is the same disruption Aguri introduced into the laboratory: he treats the students as worth his attention. Not his modified attention, not his attention contingent on their improvement, not his conditional attention — his genuine interest. He reviews their tests in thirty seconds and writes personalized notes on every paper. He maps their psychological profiles with supernatural precision not to control them but to reach them. He is not teaching them to be less of a burden to their institution. He is seeing them.

[The CBR analysis of Assassination Classroom's educational critique](https://www.cbr.com/assassination-classroom-japan-educational-system-bullying-suicide/) contextualizes this against the actual Japanese educational system's stigma practices — published rankings, high-stakes sorting exams, the cultural weight of academic failure — and notes that the series is not pure satire because it takes seriously what students in those systems actually experience. What it argues against is not academic rigor but institutional indifference to students as people. The system doesn't need to be cruel. It just needs to be indifferent. The cruelty is what indifference produces.

## The Ending as Confirmation

The world refuses to let Koro-sensei live even after his power fades. The reasoning is preemptive: he *could* become a threat again. The possibility is sufficient. [The actual Koro-sensei](https://www.looper.com/865635/the-ending-of-assassination-classroom-explained/) — the one who spent a year cooking for thirty students, writing notes on their tests, learning that he loved tangerines — is not the subject of the judgment. The thing he was classified as, before Aguri, remains the judgment. Institutions remember their classifications.

This is the series' deepest point of contact with Class E's experience. The students were classified as failures. The classification is what the system acts on, not who they actually are. [Matsui has said](https://fandomwire.com/there-is-not-going-to-be-a-manga-yusei-matsui-was-almost-ready-to-wrap-up-assassination-classrooms-plot-in-3-pages/) that the series began as a three-page concept he thought would last no time at all — students try to kill their teacher, teacher evades, end. The four years and 180 chapters that followed emerged from a single question: why can't the teacher be killed? The answer shaped everything. But the deeper question the series ended up answering was different: what turns a Reaper into a Koro-sensei? The answer is not a curriculum. It is a person who chose to show up.

## Nagisa's Inheritance

The series' thesis is not abstract. It is reproduced in Nagisa Shiota, whose [talent for assassination](https://animerants.net/2024/04/18/nagisa-shiotas-unusual-gender-expression-assassination-classroom/) — the ability to suppress his killing intent so completely that even Koro-sensei's paranormal perception cannot detect it — is not natural predation. It is a survival mechanism developed over years of living with an unpredictable parent. Nagisa learned to read emotional wavelengths because his mother's moods were volatile and illegible and he needed advance warning. The talent that makes him exceptional in the field is the talent he built to survive his childhood.

Koro-sensei saw this. He saw it without making it the center of his pedagogical interest, without weaponizing it as a skill to develop, without reducing Nagisa to his damage. He saw Nagisa as a person who had an unusual ability that came from an unusual place, and he taught the class in a way that gave Nagisa room to figure out what he actually wanted to do with both.

What Nagisa ultimately does — becoming a teacher of delinquents, standing in front of a class that takes its best shots at him — is the second reproduction of the Aguri/Koro-sensei dynamic. The chain is: Aguri saw Koro-sensei as worth knowing. Koro-sensei saw Class 3-E as worth knowing. [Nagisa sees his students as worth knowing](https://www.looper.com/865635/the-ending-of-assassination-classroom-explained/). The series is not arguing that good teaching is self-replicating — it is arguing that being seen changes what you are capable of seeing in others. The gift isn't a technique. It is an act of attention, passed forward.

The Second Reaper died in the gap between what he was and what someone seeing him might have made possible. Koro-sensei died having already given away everything he'd received. The series makes clear which of those deaths it believes in.

Whether any of this would have been legible without the specific person it required — the woman who chose late-night conversations in a government laboratory, who liked his tentacles, who gave him a birthday — is the question [Assassination Classroom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assassination_Classroom) does not flinch from. One person was enough. One person was also required. The system alone never would have produced it.
