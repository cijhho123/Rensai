The discourse around *Oshi no Ko*'s finale settled fast and hard. Aqua dies without choosing life. Kana gets no closure. Ruby ends up exactly where Ai started: performing happiness over private grief, lying to herself to keep moving. And [the narrative frames this as something approaching peace](https://comicbook.com/anime/news/oshi-no-ko-series-finale-explained/). "What was even the point of this manga?" became the [defining fan response](https://www.sportskeeda.com/anime/oshi-ko-finale-leaves-fans-wondering-what-even-point-of-this-manga).

The critique is coherent. But it's also wrong — not about what happened, but about what the series was arguing.

![Oshi no Ko manga volume 1 cover](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/en/9/90/OshiNoKo_Manga_vol1.jpg)

[The ending Akasaka built](https://siliconera.com/aka-akasaka-planned-oshi-no-ko-ending-from-the-start/) was planned before the first chapter was serialized. He warned readers two months before the finale that unlike *Kaguya-sama*, he wasn't giving each character a proper send-off. [He expressed satisfaction](https://gamerant.com/oshi-no-ko-author-finally-speaks-about-controversial-ending/) that he'd ended exactly as intended. This isn't the defense of a writer who ran out of ideas. This is a writer who drew to his thesis and found that it was not the thesis his audience had written for him.

That thesis is: lies are not the enemy. They are the medium.

## The Lie at the Origin

Ai Hoshino enters the story as the purest possible expression of performed authenticity. [A survivor of childhood abuse and institutional care](https://www.animenewsnetwork.com/feature/2023-05-10/how-accurate-is-oshi-no-ko-about-the-japanese-entertainment-industry-an-interview-with-aka-akasaka/.197795), she lacked the emotional vocabulary to identify or express genuine love. The idol industry selected her precisely because she was a natural liar — her disconnection from genuine feeling made her a perfect performer of manufactured feeling. She could say "I love you" convincingly because she had no internal reference for what it would cost to mean it.

Her manifesto: "Lies are the weapon of love. A lie that makes people happy — that is the truest love an idol can give."

This is presented not as cynicism but as her version of grace. She cannot give what she doesn't have. What she can give is the performance of what she doesn't have, and she has decided that this performance is itself a gift. The lie becomes care, offered in the only currency available to her.

Her death complicates this. Dying, she tells Aqua she loves him — and the narrative frames this as [the first genuinely true thing she has ever said](https://screenrant.com/oshi-no-ko-ending-explained/). The lie was the rehearsal. The truth arrived at the moment it could no longer be used.

## The Lie as Weapon

The series' middle sections follow what happens when lies stop being oriented toward care. Aqua lies constantly — about his motivations, his feelings for Kana and Akane, his relationship with his own revenge. [In the Fullfrontal interview](https://fullfrontal.moe/oshi-no-ko/), Akasaka described the series' core theme as miscommunication: "many people are brushing past each other, hurting each other even though they're actually saying the same thing." Aqua's "switch" — the dissociative coldness he activates during pursuit mode — is exactly this: a man saying "I am doing this for love" while the method of doing it destroys the conditions for love.

Ruby lies too, but in her Phase 1 she lies about grief rather than purpose. She performs cheerfulness. She performs that she's okay. This mirrors Ai exactly, and [the series knows it](https://cbr.com/oshi-no-ko-anime-internet-celebrity-culture-realism/) — the imagery of Ruby in B-Komachi is constantly crosscut against footage of Ai in her idol years. The inheritance is not subtle.

But the show's most rigorous examination of weaponized lying comes from the documentary arc. The reality TV episode sequence — [based explicitly on the case of Hana Kimura](https://www.animenewsnetwork.com/interest/2023-05-24/deceased-pro-wrestler-hana-kimura-mother-criticizes-oshi-no-ko-episode-6/.198375), the pro wrestler who died by suicide after coordinated cyberbullying following a manipulated edit on *Terrace House* — shows the industry's lie-machine operating at full power. A producer's cut weaponizes a real moment into a villain narrative. The audience consumes it as truth. A real person is destroyed. Here the lie is not care. It is indistinguishable in form from Ai's lie, and it causes maximum harm.

This is the series' darkest structural observation: the same formal act — performing a self that doesn't exist — can be care or destruction depending on who is doing it, to whom, and for what. The lie has no inherent moral value. The ethics live in the intent and the context. And the industry does not distinguish. It uses whichever version is profitable.

## The Lie Turned Inward

Ruby's radicalization makes this explicit. When she discovers that Ai's murderer is alive — and is the same man whose obsession caused Gorou's past-life death — her lies stop being about grief and start being about access. She performs cheerfulness to approach Kamiki. She performs innocence to conceal plans. She lies to herself most destructively: that she can pursue revenge without becoming its cost.

[The series frames this as her most dangerous phase](https://gamerant.com/oshi-no-ko-ending-explained/) — not because she's lying, but because she's lying without the orientation that made Ai's lies function as care. Ai's performance was directed outward, toward an audience, with the intention of giving them something. Ruby's lies have turned inward, toward a goal that will give nothing to anyone. The form is identical. The direction is inverted.

This is the series' formal argument at its sharpest: the same tool, pointed at yourself, becomes a wound.

## The Lie Redeemed — Or Not

![Oshi no Ko — promotional visual featuring the cast](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/en/9/9f/Oshi_no_Ko_anime_key_visual.jpg)

The ending that infuriated fans: Ruby performs at the Tokyo Dome, fulfilling Ai's unfinished dream. She tells herself she's not sad. She knows this is a lie. And she does it anyway, calling it an act of love and rebellion.

The betrayal reading: Ruby has ended up in the same position as Ai — lying to hide suffering, performing for the industry's benefit — and the series frames this as resolution. [Critics articulated this precisely](https://screenrant.com/oshi-no-ko-ending-good-bad-controversy/): "Ruby ended up in the same position that Ai was in where she's constantly lying to hide her suffering, with the series framing it as a good thing." One fan critical essay, ["Perfect Blue Criticizes The Idol Industry. Oshi no Ko Does Not."](https://letsdiscoverthingsthataregood.wordpress.com/2023/04/29/perfect-blue-criticizes-the-idol-industry-oshi-no-ko-does-not/), makes the charge explicit: unlike Satoshi Kon, who refused to let the idol machine aestheticize what it destroys, *Oshi no Ko* ultimately lets the idol machine win and calls it growth.

The completion reading: Ruby has ended up in the same *formal position* as Ai, but with one crucial difference — she knows she's lying. [Ai struggled her whole career to distinguish](https://www.cbr.com/oshi-no-ko-japan-idol-industry-social-commentary-dark-reality/) whether the emotions she performed were genuine or manufactured. The ambiguity was not a performance; it was a symptom of damage. Ruby has no such ambiguity. She is choosing the lie consciously, naming it to herself, and deciding that the consciousness of the choice is what transforms its meaning.

What Ai stumbled into dying — a moment of truth visible to herself — Ruby has arrived at as a practice, alive. This is not the same thing, even if it looks the same from outside.

[Akasaka said in interview](https://fullfrontal.moe/oshi-no-ko/) that he hoped the work would make readers "aware of this pattern" — particularly the pattern of performative extremism online, where people say and do the same things for opposite reasons. The ending is his final application of that theme. Ruby and Ai, performing the same act, are not the same person doing the same thing. The audience — like the fans who read the performance as identical — has to decide whether they're watching the same performance for the same reason. Akasaka leaves that judgment available.

## What the Ending Refuses

[Mengo Yokoyari refused to publicly comment](https://screenrant.com/oshi-no-ko-ending-controversy-manga-artist/) on the ending controversy, saying only that she'd put her heart and soul into it. She separately revealed she had personally wanted to draw an Aqua x Ruby romantic arc — an arc Akasaka rejected. This matters not because she was right, but because it tells you the ending is not accidental. Akasaka made a decision not to give the audience the transformative exit it wanted, and he made it deliberately.

The series was never asking how to fix the idol industry. [Akasaka stated directly](https://www.animenewsnetwork.com/feature/2023-05-10/how-accurate-is-oshi-no-ko-about-the-japanese-entertainment-industry-an-interview-with-aka-akasaka/.197795): "I want people to know how young talents are being hurt, exploited, and suffering." Knowing is not the same as fixing. The series refuses to imagine a repair because imagining it would be the most consequential lie the series could tell — more dishonest than any performance its characters give, because it would be telling the audience that recognition leads to change. Ruby is still inside the machine, still lying, still performing. And she has chosen this, with full knowledge, as Ai never could.

[Kana Arima never learns](https://www.cbr.com/oshi-no-ko-kana-arima-best-character-development/) whether Aqua loved her. [The Futari no Etude companion novel](https://comicbook.com/anime/news/oshi-no-ko-novel-kana-akane-endings/) expands her post-finale arc — which is the novel form's acknowledgment that the manga's structure couldn't hold everyone — but the main text's silence on her is not careless. Her unresolution is the series' observation about the industry applied to narrative: the people who aren't the center of the story don't get closures, because the industry doesn't give them closures either. [Kana's arc](https://www.cbr.com/oshi-no-ko-kana-arima-best-character-development/) is the best individual development in the manga precisely because it tracks how someone recovers their artistic identity inside that system — which makes her absence from the finale's resolution a structural choice, not an oversight.

The "what was the point" reading assumes the point was supposed to be escape. The point was the argument. Lies are the medium through which the idol industry makes people care about each other, exploit each other, destroy each other, and survive each other. Ruby at the Tokyo Dome is the argument's proof: a woman who has run the full circuit of the medium — received as care, watched it weaponized, used it as weapon, turned it inward, and finally turned it outward again as an act of conscious love — and is still standing.

Not because the industry deserved her. Because she decided what the performance would cost, and paid it herself.

That is not Ai's tragedy completed. That is Ai's lesson, learned by the one person who watched her die holding it.
