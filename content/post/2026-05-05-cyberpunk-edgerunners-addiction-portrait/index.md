In [episode 6 of *Cyberpunk: Edgerunners*](https://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/Recap/CyberpunkEdgerunnersS1E6GirlOnFire), David Martinez watches Maine — his mentor, his surrogate father figure, the most capable mercenary he knows — dissolve into cyberpsychosis at a neon-lit intersection, overwhelmed by augmentations he could no longer manage. Maine has been warned. He was told to downgrade. He refused. He believed he was different. And then he wasn't.

Immediately after, David [takes Maine's implants for himself](https://cyberpunk.fandom.com/wiki/David_Martinez).

This is not dramatic irony in service of plot efficiency. It is the most honest thing the show does: it shows you exactly how addiction works, in real time, and then shows you the person who just watched the warning sign choose to become it.

*Cyberpunk: Edgerunners* is described as a tragedy about ambition, about the price of surviving Night City, about what it costs to become something extraordinary. These readings are accurate but secondary. At its structural core, the show is an addiction narrative — and one of the most formally precise ones in anime. David Martinez doesn't fail because Night City is cruel. He fails because [Night City offered him something he needed and he couldn't stop taking it](https://cyberpunk.fandom.com/wiki/Cyberpsychosis).

## Pondsmith's Model

The addiction reading isn't a critical imposition. It's the explicit design framework. [Mike Pondsmith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mike_Pondsmith), creator of the Cyberpunk tabletop RPG that underlies the entire franchise, has stated directly: ["In some ways, I tend to treat cyberware as an addiction — heavy anabolic steroid use being my favorite model."](https://cyberpunk.fandom.com/wiki/Cyberpsychosis) He explained further that not everyone who uses steroids experiences roid rage, but those with a predisposition toward needing more are more likely to hit the threshold. The fiction follows the same pattern. Cyberpsychosis is not a punishment for power; it is what happens when someone with a predisposition toward escalation exists in an environment that rewards escalation without limits.

Night City is that environment. The Edgerunner life is its accelerant. And David Martinez, from the first episode, is a person who has every psychological precondition for this specific kind of destruction.

## The Pattern

Every element of David's arc maps onto [the clinical addiction sequence](https://addictionresource.com/addiction/stages/) with precision that can't be accidental. Addiction researchers describe initiation, functional use, escalation, tolerance buildup, and final crisis as the standard progression — and David follows each stage while the show refuses to editorialise it as anything other than comprehensible.

**Initiation through trauma.** David's mother dies in a corpo hit that was never meant for them. He has nothing, is failing at the one school that was supposed to save him, and then discovers he can do something nobody else in Night City can: tolerate augmentations far above the normal human threshold. The first hit is free. It gives him identity when he had none.

**The supportive environment as buffer.** [Pondsmith has noted](https://gamerant.com/cyberpunk-edgerunners-is-cyberpsychosis-avoidable-or-inevitable/) that David's resistance to cyberpsychosis comes from his starting psychological conditions: a loving mother, a mentor in Maine, a chosen family in the crew, a relationship with Lucy. High "Humanity" in the game's terms — genuine connections that cushion the loss of self that augmentation accelerates. The functional addict phase. He handles it fine, for a while.

**The warning that arrives exactly when it cannot be heard.** Maine goes down in episode 4. David [watches the man he looked up to lose his mind in a parking lot](https://www.dualshockers.com/cyberpunk-edgerunners-david-cyberpsychosis/) and be put down. This is the moment in every addiction narrative where the protagonist encounters the end-stage version of their own path and has to decide whether to recognize themselves in it. David's decision is immediate: he takes Maine's implants. He doesn't pause. He doesn't process. The warning is visible, and he walks past it, because the substance offers something that the warning cannot compete with.

**The functional addict delusion.** For several episodes, David's higher tolerance looks like a superpower rather than a liability. He can take more than Maine could. He can take more than anyone. [The "I'm different" narrative](https://thepathwitcher.blog/2024/05/16/david-martinez-myth-special/) is the addicted person's most reliable companion — the conviction that the rules apply to everyone else, that their relationship with the substance is exceptional rather than pathological. The crew reinforces this. Night City reinforces this. Even Lucy, watching his hands shake, reinforces this by staying.

**The enabler network.** This is where Edgerunners is quietly devastating. The crew doesn't push David toward more chrome because they want him dead. They push him because they need him functional at increasingly higher levels. Faraday — the fixer who orchestrates David's missions throughout the second half of the series — needs him capable of increasingly suicidal jobs, [up to and including the Arasaka Tower assault](https://cyberpunk.fandom.com/wiki/Maine_(Edgerunners)). The jobs require someone who can do things no one else in Night City can do at his current augmentation level. Every time David escalates and survives, the crew's bar rises with him. They are not villains. [The relationship between enablers and addicts rarely involves malice](https://bridgingthegaps.com/blog/unpacking-the-cycle-trauma-and-addiction-connection) — it involves mutual benefit, love, and the inability to be the person who forces a stop.

**Tolerance → escalation → final dose.** The Cyber Skeleton sequence — David's absolute limit, the augmentation that literally begins dissolving his body — is the show's representation of the final spike. He knows what it is. He chooses it anyway, because the alternative is failure, and for an addict, failure is the only thing that competes with the substance for emotional weight. Adam Smasher [directly tells him](https://www.cbr.com/cyberpunk-edgerunners-ending-lucy-adam-smasher-netflix/) what he is: "You think you're special? Don't make me laugh." David's specialness was always a story he was telling himself. The Cyber Skeleton strips even that away, and what's left is a body collapsing under the weight of what it agreed to carry.

## Night City as the Structural Enabler

[Night City doesn't kill David.](https://sixmilepost.com/11277/entertainment/cyberpunk-edgerunners-is-a-tragic-warning-of-a-dystopic-future-and-unfulfilled-dreams/) This is what separates Edgerunners from the simpler version of the same story, where the city is a monster that consumes the innocent. Night City provides the conditions. It removes every alternative pathway, prices out every form of advancement that doesn't involve selling pieces of yourself, and then surrounds you with a culture that treats augmentation as identity. The corpo school that expelled David, the healthcare system that couldn't save his mother, the meritocracy that promised everything and delivered nothing — these aren't the cause of his death. They are the environment that made the chrome look necessary.

The show's commercial success after launch — [CD Projekt RED's best third quarter in company history](https://www.pcgamer.com/cyberpunk-edgerunners-helped-push-cd-projekt-to-the-best-third-quarter-in-our-entire-history/), a 70% revenue jump following the premiere — exists in a strange relationship to the addiction thesis. The anime worked as a gateway to the game by making people feel what it was like to be in Night City before they arrived. The emotional investment it generated was not in power fantasies but in grief. That is an unusual thing to market.

## The Contrast

Lucy's arc runs opposite to David's. She wants the Moon — [a place that corpo infrastructure hasn't yet fully colonized](https://leveluptalk.com/news/lucy-cyberpunk-edgerunners-character-analysis/), a destination that requires no chrome to appreciate, that exists outside the logic of Night City's rewards system entirely. She is the show's only character whose desire has a concrete, external, achievable endpoint that doesn't involve more of what Night City is selling.

She survives and reaches it. This is the show's one moment of redemption — not for Night City, which remains exactly what it is, but for the possibility of wanting something it cannot provide. [Lucy gets to the Moon](https://dmtalkies.com/cyberpunk-edgerunners-season-1-ending-explained-2022-netflix-series/) and watches a virtual simulation of David there, alone, in a place where augmentation doesn't matter and survival doesn't cost you your mind.

David wanted to be extraordinary within Night City's terms. Lucy wanted to leave. One of these is a story that Night City can accommodate; the other isn't. The show is not ambiguous about which one was right.

## What the Addiction Reading Adds

The prevailing read on Edgerunners — the tragedy of ambition, the price of power, the cruelty of the city — is not wrong. But it misses something the addiction reading captures: the question of agency. Addiction narratives are uncomfortable specifically because they complicate the distinction between choice and compulsion. David made choices. He also made them in the presence of a substance that progressively removed his capacity to choose differently.

[Rebecca watches him deteriorate and knows what's coming](https://www.dualshockers.com/cyberpunk-edgerunners-david-cyberpsychosis/). Lucy knows. The crew knows. Nobody stops him, partly because they can't, and partly because the logic of Night City makes stopping feel like a greater loss than continuing. This is what addiction looks like from the outside — the helplessness of people who love someone and cannot offer them anything that competes with the thing that's killing them.

*Edgerunners* doesn't ask you to sympathize with David's choices. It asks you to understand how a person arrives at them. [Creator Mike Pondsmith confirmed](https://80.lv/articles/they-nailed-it-cyberpunk-s-original-creator-approved-the-edgerunners-anime/) that Studio Trigger "nailed it" — and this is part of what they nailed: the interiority of someone who can see the edge clearly and cannot stop moving toward it.

Night City is the dealer. The chrome is the product. David Martinez is the customer who told himself he was different.

He was not different. That's the tragedy, and also the point.
