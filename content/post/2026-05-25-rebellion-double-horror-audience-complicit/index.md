*Walpurgisnacht: Rising* opens in Japanese theaters on August 28, 2026.[^1] Three months away. After multiple delays — originally slated for winter 2024, pushed to winter 2025, then February 2026, then August[^2] — the sequel to *Rebellion* is nearly here. Which means it's past time to look honestly at what *Rebellion* actually built.

The debate around *Rebellion* has always orbited the ending: Was Homura becoming the Devil a betrayal of the series? A commercial compromise that hijacked a planned graceful conclusion?[^3] These are real questions. But they've crowded out a more fundamental observation about the film. *Rebellion* is not primarily a sequel to *Madoka*. It's a horror film. And its horror works in two acts, with the second act depending entirely on what the first one does to you.

---

## The First Monster

The film opens in the familiar rhythms of magical girl comfort. Homura is shy and new again. The girls are fighting together against "Nightmares" — softer, consequence-free versions of Witches. Bebe, a familiar, is part of the team. Everything is slightly wrong in ways that feel like background noise until Homura starts pulling on the threads.

This is the classic architecture of paranoid horror. You have a protagonist who notices the unreality. You follow their investigation. The camera trusts them, their logic is sound, and their conclusion, when it comes, lands like a floor giving way: Homura herself is the monster. The labyrinth she's been investigating is her own Soul Gem. The fake Mitakihara is not something she woke up inside of — it's something she generated. She fell into despair over Madoka's erasure from the world and *became a Witch*, and the false paradise is the shape her grief took.[^4]

This is a clean horror reveal, and Shaft/Akiyuki Shinbo execute it with the formal precision that Gekidan Inu Curry — the design collective responsible for the Witch barrier visuals across the original series — helped build.[^5] The "tea party" scene that opens the film, added by Gekidan Inu Curry, is a perfect warm-up to the wrongness that follows: everything is pleasant in the way that things in dreams are pleasant, slightly too smooth, slightly too arranged.[^6]

When Homura discovers the truth, she resolves to die rather than be saved. She directs her familiars to kill every Incubator in the labyrinth and prepares to destroy herself. She will not allow her condition to expose Madoka to Kyubey's exploitation. This is Homura at her most pure: choosing obliteration over complicity in harm to Madoka. Horror sequence one is complete. The monster has been found, and the monster is the protagonist.

---

## The Rescue That Breaks Everything

Here is where *Rebellion* does something films rarely have the structural composure to do. It resolves its first horror cleanly.

Sayaka — who entered the false world as a full-memories guardian of the Law of Cycles — rallies the other girls. Nagisa (the magical girl identity of Charlotte, the Witch who killed Mami, given life and purpose by Gekidan Inu Curry in the film)[^7] joins the effort. They work together to dismantle the barrier imprisoning Homura's Soul Gem. Madoka arrives — Madoka, the Law of Cycles, the one who gave up her human existence so magical girls would no longer suffer[^8] — and reaches for Homura.

And the audience wants this. The film has spent an hour and forty minutes reminding us why we care about Homura. She's done everything right. She fought the Incubators, she protected Madoka, she chose self-destruction over surrender. She deserves to be saved. Madoka is there to cleanse her and bring her into the higher plane, and every piece of the film has pointed toward this as the correct outcome.

This is when *Rebellion* commits its actual crime.

---

## The Second Monster

Before Madoka can complete the cleansing, Homura grabs her.

What follows is one of the more genuinely disturbing sequences in modern anime — not because of its imagery (though the imagery is extraordinary) but because of its *logic*. Homura separates Madoka's divinity from her human form. She uses the power that has been building throughout the film — not despair, the film is explicit about this: *"what corrupted her Soul Gem was not a curse of despair, but a curse of love"*[^9] — and rewrites the cosmos. Everyone is returned to the lives they led before Madoka's sacrifice. Madoka is human again, in a world where she and Homura are schoolmates. And Homura is the Demon who governs this arrangement, the Arch-Enemy to Madoka's eventual recollection and re-emergence.

The film ends with Homura seated over Mitakihara, a badly-beaten Kyubey at her feet, watching. She has not been defeated. She has won.

*Key visual for Walpurgisnacht: Rising, the 2026 sequel. The sequel arrives August 28. All of this is its setup.*

The reason this second horror lands harder than the first is that the first horror prepared you for the rescue. You needed Homura to be saved. The film built that need over two acts. And the act that felt like salvation — Madoka arriving, the barrier breaking, the girls working together — was the mechanism Homura used. The rescue was real. And Homura chose not to be rescued.

This is not Homura making a mistake. It's Homura looking at what the Law of Cycles would mean for her — dissolution into the abstract, losing her individual self and her connection to a specific Madoka who knows her — and deciding that's worse than becoming something monstrous.[^10] The "curse of love" the film names is real. What corrupted her was not grief at Madoka's absence but grief at the *terms* of Madoka's presence in the Law of Cycles: infinite and everywhere and unreachable.

The second monster is worse than the first because the first monster didn't know what she was. She was a Witch, which in this world is what happens when a girl's despair outpaces her hope. That's a tragedy. The second monster knows exactly what she's doing, chose it in full consciousness over a clearly available alternative, and announced herself publicly.[^11] She stood over the city and told Kyubey what she'd done.

---

## The Incubators Didn't Understand the Problem

There's a subplot in *Rebellion* that gets less attention than the Homura arc but is structurally important. Kyubey's scheme throughout the film is to isolate Homura's Soul Gem, observe the Law of Cycles come into effect, and then use the data to eventually re-establish the old system where magical girls become Witches and generate harvestable energy.[^12] The Incubators are scientists. They ran an experiment.

What they didn't account for is the thing the film announces explicitly: Homura's corruption was love, not despair.

The Incubators' entire model of human psychology runs on hope/despair as a thermodynamic resource. They know how to farm grief. They know how to calibrate the system to maximize output before the collapse into Witchhood. This is a quantifiable, modelable process. Kyubey even says at one point that emotional states function like a gradient that can be measured.

Love — not the warm emotion but love as *motivating violence*, love as the willingness to remake the cosmos to keep a specific person reachable — is not in that model. It doesn't fit. Kyubey's plan assumed Homura would behave like a despairing girl. She didn't. She behaved like someone who decided Madoka's freedom from the Law of Cycles was less important than Madoka's availability. That's an affective category Kyubey has no framework for, which is why the experiment failed so catastrophically. The Incubators did not get their data. They got a Demon. They got to spend eternity bearing the weight of the world's curses as Homura's punishment for their interference.[^13]

The film is, among other things, arguing that purely rational optimization systems cannot anticipate what happens when their subject's corruption comes from love rather than despair. The Incubators ran the wrong experiment because they didn't know that failure mode existed.

---

## Why Shinbo's Correction Matters

A detail from the film's production notes: storyboarder Shinsaku Sasaki's original depiction of Homura was "much more negative," a characterization that Shinbo and the staff later corrected.[^14] The production moved toward a more sympathetic Homura.

This is significant. The film's entire horror mechanism depends on the audience wanting Homura saved. A more negative Homura — one the viewer can hold at arm's length, one whose transformation into the Devil feels like confirmation of something already suspected — doesn't implicate the audience. You saw it coming, you weren't rooting for her, you didn't get used.

The sympathetic Homura is the version of the character that makes the second horror function. When she grabs Madoka, the viewer is still mid-exhale from the relief of her rescue. That emotional lag, that split second where you're still processing the salvation that isn't coming, is the point where *Rebellion* does its damage. Shinbo's instinct to correct the storyboard was cinematically correct. The horror needed the audience on Homura's side.

---

## Three Months Out

Rebellion earned 2.08 billion yen at the Japanese box office, breaking the previous record for a late-night anime adaptation held by *K-On! The Movie*.[^15] Critics praised it. It won the Best Theatrical Film Award at the 19th Animation Kobe Awards.[^16] The ANN review called it "far more fascinating and entertaining than it had any reason to be."[^17]

And then the franchise went quiet for over a decade while Walpurgisnacht: Rising slowly materialized — announced in April 2021,[^18] delayed repeatedly, now confirmed for August 28, 2026.[^1] Gen Urobuchi revealed that he finished the WR script immediately after completing *Kamen Rider Gaim* and before beginning *Thunderbolt Fantasy*,[^19] which puts its composition in 2015 or thereabouts. He was writing the sequel to Rebellion's horror before anyone in the public knew a sequel existed.

What Walpurgisnacht: Rising has to do is answer the question Rebellion left open: what does it look like when the second monster governs? Homura won. Madoka is ordinary. Kyubey is broken at her feet. The sequel inherits a world remade by an act of love that was also an act of domination, and it has to take a position on whether that distinction matters.

The second monster is worse because we enabled it. What happens when she runs out of places to hide that fact is August's problem. But the structure is already there, in 2013, in 116 minutes, in a film that ran its horror twice and trusted you to feel both.
