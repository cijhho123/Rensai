"We used Christian symbols because they looked cool. There's no deep meaning."

That is Hideaki Anno in a [1997 interview](https://fandomwire.com/its-pretentious-hideaki-anno-debunks-a-common-misconception-of-neon-genesis-evangelion-that-makes-us-look-like-fools/), being asked whether the extensive religious imagery in *Neon Genesis Evangelion* carried theological significance. In [2021, on NHK's Top Runner](https://screenrant.com/neon-genesis-evangelion-pretentious-hidden-meaning-philosophical-factoid/), he was more expansive: "Evangelion is often described as philosophical, but in reality, it's not. It's pretentious. It just looks impressive at a glance — there's no deeper meaning."

The fan reaction to statements like this is usually one of two things: either denial (Anno is being modest, or self-protective, or lying) or deflation (the show is a fraud). Both responses are wrong. Anno's description of his own work is accurate. And it is also, paradoxically, the most precise explanation of why the show's religious symbolism works the way it does.

## What Anno Took and What He Didn't

The Kabbalistic and Judeo-Christian elements in *Evangelion* are extensive and specific. The opening title sequence places [the Tree of Life (Sephirot)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Themes_of_Neon_Genesis_Evangelion) — the Kabbalistic diagram of ten mystical aspects of divinity — among the first images the viewer sees. The Angels take their names from Jewish apocrypha and angelology: Sachiel, Shamshel, Ramiel, Arael, Armisael. The acronyms SEELE (German: "soul") and NERV (German: "nerve") map onto Kabbalistic concepts. The scenario is built around Adam and Lilith — specifically Lilith, not Eve, a deliberate choice that invokes the Midrashic tradition of the first woman who refused subordination. Third Impact is framed in terms of Tikkun Olam, the Kabbalistic concept of cosmic repair through the merging of souls.

[Scholar Stephen Chung has argued](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/370580245_All_is_Right_With_the_World_An_Angelological_Kabbalistic_Analysis_of_Religious_References_in_Hideaki_Anno) that each of *Eva*'s major characters can be mapped onto specific Sephirot, that the series reworks stories from the Midrash and Zohar, and that the structure of Human Instrumentality corresponds closely to mystical concepts of divine unity. The analysis is rigorous and the correspondences are real. They are also, by Anno's own account, largely unintentional.

[Toshio Okada, who co-founded Gainax with Anno](https://forward.com/culture/474063/kabbalah-neon-genesis-evangelion-30-10-rebuild-amazon-anime-eva-streaming/), described the production process at an MIT talk in 2003: Anno had never read the Bible. What the team did was pick through books on religion and psychology, extracting "interesting technical terms" and visual concepts that seemed powerful. They took what they wanted and left the theological context behind. The Dead Sea Scrolls appear in the show not because the writers understood their contents but because the phrase carries weight. Kabbalah appears not as doctrine but as a visual grammar.

This is the key distinction: Anno was not using religious symbolism. He was using religious *materials*. The difference matters enormously.

## The Freedom of Not Believing

In the same cluster of mid-nineties interviews, Anno said something that has received less attention than his "it's pretentious" dismissals: ["If I were a Christian believer, I couldn't have inserted Christian elements into Eva in that way. I would have been scared to."](https://www.cbr.com/neon-genesis-evangelion-religious-symbolism/)

This is the most important sentence Anno ever said about his own show. It describes not a limitation but an advantage. A Christian creator working with the Crucifixion, or a Jewish creator working with Kabbalistic cosmology, would carry obligations the imagery does not carry for Anno. Theological accuracy. Doctrinal responsibility. The weight of a living tradition with living practitioners who might be offended or misrepresented. These constraints — which are not abstract but very real — would curtail exactly the kind of free recombination that *Evangelion* performs constantly.

Anno had none of these constraints. He approached the Sephirot, the apocrypha, and the machinery of apocalyptic tradition the way a sculptor approaches raw stone: interested in texture, weight, and formal potential, not in the stone's geological history. The religious imagery in *Eva* does not need to be theologically accurate to work. It needs to be atmospheric — to produce feelings of cosmic scale, ancient authority, and sacred horror — and for that purpose, Anno's distance from the traditions he was raiding was an asset rather than a deficit.

This is not a uniquely Japanese practice, but *Eva* represents its [most extreme and most influential](https://www.evamonkey.com/writings/macwilliams-mark-apocalypticism-japanese-style-jewish-christian-symbolism.php) example in anime. Japanese popular culture has a specific relationship to Judeo-Christian imagery that is structurally different from how those images function in their cultures of origin. In Japan, the cruciform shape, the angelic names, the apocalyptic architecture — none of these come pre-loaded with personal theology or community memory. They are available as what literary theorists would call "free-floating signifiers": images that denote gravity, age, and transcendence without requiring the audience to have a theological position on them.

This is what makes NGE's religious imagery land differently for Japanese audiences than it does for Western viewers who grew up inside one of the traditions being borrowed from. A Western Christian watching the crosses and the Seraphim and the Dead Sea Scrolls is negotiating between their own theology and the show's use of it. A Japanese viewer in 1995 had no such negotiation to perform. The imagery arrived as pure atmosphere.

## The Combination That Makes Meaning

Anno's own description of how he used the imagery is telling: ["As the symbols are mixed together, for the first time something like an interrelationship or a meaning emerges."](https://loopingworld.com/2013/05/08/evangelion-and-kabbalah-metaphor-made-literal/) This is the closest he ever came to articulating what he was actually doing: not deploying pre-formed meanings but creating them through combination.

The Kabbalistic framework, the German naming conventions, the psychological terminology pulled from reference books — none of these carry standalone meaning within the show. What they do is produce a lattice. When Ramiel (the Angel of Thunder in Enochian tradition) manifests as an enormous geometric shape firing particle beams, the disconnection between the name and the form is not a mistake. It is the show's aesthetic logic. The apocryphal name signals ancient authority; the geometric horror signals modern scientific terror. The combination is the point.

[Fan wiki analyses at EvaGeeks](https://wiki.evageeks.org/Theory_and_Analysis:Yui_Ikari) have spent decades documenting the internal coherence that emerges from this kind of recombination — tracing how specific design choices in the Angel sequence map onto Sephirotic attributes that Anno may not have consciously intended. The scholarship finds meaning that may not have been placed there. But this is how aesthetic raw material works: it generates implications the creator did not prescribe, because the materials themselves carry sedimented meanings that activate upon contact with an audience. Anno was working with a tradition dense enough to generate its own logic, even when handled by someone unfamiliar with that logic.

The same principle applies to the [psychological terminology](https://unrememberedyesterdays.wordpress.com/2016/09/03/the-divinity-of-sin-or-neon-genesis-evangelions-religious-imagery-does-matter-a-lot/) — AT Field as a boundary between the self and the world, instrumentality as dissolution of identity, the specific way the show mapped clinical depression onto cosmic-scale catastrophe. Anno and his team picked these terms from psychology books without systematic understanding of the clinical contexts, and produced a portrait of psychological fragmentation that [resonates closely with clinical accounts of depression, social withdrawal, and suicidal ideation](https://medium.com/@cyberdank/be-good-to-yourself-hideaki-annos-gospel-in-neon-genesis-evangelion-329ed9fd4300) — specific enough in its phenomenology that viewers who have experienced these conditions often describe the show as the most accurate representation they have encountered. The freedom of surface engagement turned out to produce real depth.

## What Anno Gave Japanese Anime

The legacy of NGE's religious symbolism is that it established a practice — and a permission structure — for an entire generation of anime creators. [The show normalized the use of Judeo-Christian imagery as aesthetic raw material](https://su4roth.wordpress.com/2021/05/02/religious-futurism-evangelion-and-the-origin-story/) for anything that needed cosmic weight: apocalyptic scale, sacred horror, the language of absolute stakes. You can trace this influence through a decade of mecha and sci-fi anime in which Angels, Seraphim, and cruciform imagery appear wherever a creator needs the audience to feel that something is very large and very final.

The practice has become so normalized that it no longer registers as borrowing. [RahXephon (2002)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RahXephon), whose director Yutaka Izubuchi acknowledged NGE's direct influence, took the same method and applied it to Mayan cosmology — borrowing a different tradition's ritual weight for a cosmic-scale mecha narrative. [Haibane Renmei](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haibane_Renmei) grounded itself in Christian imagery of the fallen and not-yet-redeemed. The aesthetic permission NGE issued was that any religious tradition's vocabulary was available as raw material, provided the imagery was strange enough to generate the right atmospheric weight.

But the original permission came from *Evangelion*, and the original source of that permission was Anno's theological distance — his ability to use the materials without being accountable to the tradition they came from.

## The Accurate Insult

Anno calling his own show "pretentious" is, in one sense, exactly what the word means: the show claims more than it earns doctrinally. Its religious symbolism is not theologically grounded. Its psychological framework was assembled from reference books rather than clinical practice. It generates feelings of profundity without doing the philosophical work that would justify those feelings in a more rigorous context.

But pretension at this level is an aesthetic strategy, not a failure. The show is honest about what it is — Anno [has been saying so in interviews for thirty years](https://medium.com/@cyberdank/be-good-to-yourself-hideaki-annos-gospel-in-neon-genesis-evangelion-329ed9fd4300). What the symbolism produces in actual viewers — the sense of cosmic stakes, of ancient horror, of the universe turning on questions of individual psychological survival — is not pretense. It is the documented effect of two decades of audience engagement.

The [academic literature on Eva's symbolism](https://online.ucpress.edu/jrpc/article/doi/10.1525/jrpc.2025.2874560/217340/Canon-and-Apocrypha-in-Neon-Genesis-Evangelion) continues to find structural coherences that Anno did not intend. Scholars working from Kabbalistic studies, angelology, depth psychology, and film theory keep discovering that the materials Anno took are rich enough to sustain genuinely meaningful analysis — even if that analysis is retrospective, even if it was not built into the work by design.

This is what happens when you use raw materials that have centuries of accumulated meaning. You do not need to understand the tradition to put it to work. You need only the freedom to recombine it without theological inhibition.

Anno had that freedom because he never believed any of it. That is the origin story of *Evangelion*'s most distinctive quality: it means more than it meant to.
