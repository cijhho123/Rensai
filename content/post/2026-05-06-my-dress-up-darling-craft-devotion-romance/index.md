There's a scene early in *My Dress-Up Darling* where Wakana Gojo is caught sewing in the school craft room and braces for mockery. What he gets instead is Marin Kitagawa leaning over his shoulder, watching his hands move across the fabric, and saying something that changes the entire trajectory of the story: she doesn't just tolerate what he's doing, she *recognizes* it. She sees the quality in his stitching the same way she sees quality in the character designs she idolizes. And in that recognition, Shinichi Fukuda tips her hand. This isn't a romance about proximity and misunderstandings. It's a romance about craft.

That thesis sounds simple until you measure how much work the series does to earn it.

## What Gojo Actually Is

Most discussions of *My Dress-Up Darling* spend their energy on Marin — her personality, her body, the debate about whether the fanservice surrounding her undermines or expresses something about her agency. Those conversations aren't wrong to have. But they slide past the character who is actually doing the structural work: [Wakana Gojo](https://www.soapcentral.com/anime/the-pure-charm-wakana-gojo-my-dress-up-darling), aspiring *kashirashi* (a maker of hina doll heads), dedicated to one of Japan's oldest and most precarious craft traditions.

Hina dolls are not a neutral choice of backstory. They sit at an unusual intersection: culturally prestigious (the craft [dates to before the Edo period](https://fujikicrafts.co.jp/exploring-the-charm-of-traditional-hina-dolls-a-cultural-treasure-of-japan/), practiced by specialized artisans, ceremonially significant), socially coded as feminine (associated with Hina Matsuri, a girls' festival), and economically precarious (the market has been [shrinking for decades as nuclear families get smaller and apartments get tinier](https://kogeijapan.com/locale/en_US/iwatsukiningyo/)). To dedicate your life to hina dolls in contemporary Japan is to make a choice that is simultaneously respectable and weird. It requires that you care about craft for its own sake, because the external rewards — money, status, the respect of your peers — are minimal and diminishing.

Gojo knows all of this. [He spent years hiding his interest](https://infinitemirai.wordpress.com/tag/wakana-gojo/) because a childhood incident taught him that boys weren't supposed to care about dolls. He carries that lesson as a kind of social armor, isolating himself to avoid the judgment of people who wouldn't understand. What Fukuda does with this setup is quietly radical: she doesn't frame Gojo's isolation as a personality flaw to overcome. She frames it as the inevitable cost of genuine devotion. People who care this much about something specific and unglamorous are always going to be hard to understand.

The parallel she draws is exact. Marin's dedication to eroge cosplay — obscure games, niche characters, technically demanding costumes — puts her in the same position. Her interests are [not the cool, socially legible kind of otaku passion](https://www.thepopverse.com/tv-popverse-jump-my-dress-up-darling-about-awkardness-weirdness-gojo-marin). The specific characters she wants to embody are ones that require enormous effort to explain to anyone outside the fandom. She loves them anyway, and she has built her aesthetic intelligence around that love. Gojo doesn't recognize a cute girl in the craft room. He recognizes someone whose relationship to their obsession mirrors his own.

## Fukuda's Research as Argument

The case that craft is the series' real subject gets stronger when you look at how [Shinichi Fukuda actually made this manga](https://www.theoasg.com/reviews/manga/my-dress-up-darling-volume-1-review/19868). After an editor introduced her to the world of cosplay, she didn't just read about it — she interviewed working cosplayers, visited specialist shops, got formal permission to depict real locations (the wig shop Swallowtail appears in the manga with the company's blessing), and documented her research process in the afterwords of each volume. This is the level of investment a sports manga author makes to accurately portray an athletic discipline. She applied the same standard to cosplay.

The result is a manga that goes into [the mechanics of costume construction with the same specificity it gives to emotional beats](https://www.japanpowered.com/anime-articles/my-dress-up-darling). Fabric choice, wig fitting, foam shaping, heat transfer, makeup technique — these aren't background color. They are the plot. When Gojo exhausts himself finishing an outfit in time for a convention, saying "if I can't do my best for the person I'm making this for, then I have no business calling myself a craftsman," that line lands because the reader has been watching him work. The craft has been shown, not told. The emotional statement arrives through demonstration.

This is Fukuda doing what Gojo does: treating the subject seriously enough to earn the feeling.

## The Romance Is a Recognition Scene

It's worth being precise about where the romance actually lives in *My Dress-Up Darling*, because the series contains [a substantial amount of fanservice](https://www.cbr.com/my-dress-up-darling-anime-cosplay-fanservice/) and the discourse around it — [whether it undermines Marin's agency](https://www.cbr.com/my-dress-up-darling-fan-service-undermines-message/) or is [integral to her character](https://www.animefeminist.com/subverting-masculine-expectations-and-the-male-gaze-in-my-dress-up-darling/) — keeps drowning out the actual question. What neither reading quite captures is that the core romantic transaction of the series doesn't happen through any of the fanservice moments. It happens through the work sessions. Gojo measures Marin's body not as titillation but as data — he needs the numbers to build the costume correctly, and his mind goes straight to construction logistics. [Marin watches him work and develops respect that shades into attraction](https://iiisekai.com/my-dress-up-darling-why-people-love-it/) because what she's watching is genuine craft commitment, the same thing she feels about cosplay from the other side. He makes her feel understood. She makes him feel like his skills are not things to hide.

That exchange is the romance. The fanservice sits alongside it, sometimes amplifying it, sometimes working against it, but the emotional core is a recognition: *you care about making things the way I care about making things, and that's rare*.

## The Craft-Devotion Model as Masculine Reframe

One of the more interesting things *My Dress-Up Darling* does, largely without announcing it, is [redefine masculine virtue through craft](https://anihk.com/en/blogs/characters/my-dress-up-darling-gojo-wakana-clothing-philosophy-samue-cosplay). Gojo is physically large — over 180cm — but his actual power comes from precision, patience, and empathetic attention to what his subject needs. He applies makeup. He sews. He thinks about how a character's visual design expresses her personality, then tries to embody that in fabrication. [As CBR notes](https://www.cbr.com/my-dress-up-darling-why-marin-falling-for-gojo/), he's written partly for the female gaze — attentive, emotionally honest, skilled at work that patriarchal media treats as unserious. But that framing risks making him sound like a design decision rather than a person. What makes him work is that his skills aren't compensatory or incidental — they flow from the same dedication that defines his kashirashi training. He sews because sewing requires exactly what hina doll construction requires: careful, unhurried attention invested in getting it right rather than getting it done.

There's a moment in the manga where Gojo reflects on his grandfather's influence and realizes that his obsession with the ideal face of a hina doll — the specific, almost inexpressible standard of beauty he's trying to achieve — has given him a way of seeing that most people don't have. He can look at Marin dressed as a character and think precisely about whether the costume is accurate, whether the wig captures the right texture, whether the expression she needs to hold matches the character's emotional register. This is not a typical way to notice someone. It is, however, the way a craftsman notices things. And it turns out to be an excellent way to fall in love.

## What the Hina Dolls Did After

The real-world epilogue to *My Dress-Up Darling*'s craft argument is documented and measurable. [According to Anime News Network](https://www.animenewsnetwork.com/interest/2022-02-10/hina-dolls-see-popularity-spike-after-my-dress-up-darling-anime/.182395), all 300 sets of Suzuki Ningyo's modernized "Bell's Kiss" hina dolls sold out shortly after the anime's fourth episode aired in January 2022. The [Iwatsuki Ningyo Museum in Saitama](https://www.japan.travel/en/japans-local-treasures/iwatsuki-ningyo-museum/) — the city famous as Japan's premier hina doll production center — saw a significant spike in younger visitors. In April 2023, Saitama city formally collaborated with the franchise at the museum.

This is unusual. Anime has driven tourism (the "anime pilgrimage" phenomenon is well-documented), but it more commonly sends fans to geographical locations — a street corner, a staircase, a convenience store. *My Dress-Up Darling* sent fans to a craft museum. They went not to see where a scene was filmed but to see the actual objects that Gojo's training is aimed at producing.

[As Crunchyroll's feature](https://www.crunchyroll.com/news/features/2023/4/17/feature-how-my-dress-up-darling-bridges-fandoms-across-generations-with-ningyo-dolls) noted, this is a case of otaku culture actively preserving a traditional Japanese craft that had been quietly dying. The mechanism was simple: the manga treated the craft seriously enough that readers wanted to know if the real thing was worth their attention. It was.

## The Fukuda Parallel

There's something recursive about all of this. The reason *My Dress-Up Darling* is able to make this argument about craft devotion is that Fukuda made the manga the way Gojo and Marin make their costumes: by researching obsessively, getting the technical details right, and treating the subject with enough respect that the reader can feel it.

Fukuda [acknowledged that her research process was comprehensive](https://raycomicoriginal.medium.com/my-dress-up-darling-ef29cf71901f) — interview trips, on-site visits, formal permissions. The manga's afterwords trace this process volume by volume. She was, in other words, doing exactly what Gojo does when he's trying to make a costume: finding out what the subject actually requires, then delivering that. Not a version of it. The thing itself.

The manga [concluded in March 2025](https://www.crunchyroll.com/news/latest/2025/2/21/my-dress-up-darling-manga-final-chapter-march-2025) after seven years, with Marin and Gojo reaching the kind of ending that the series had been quietly building toward. Not a dramatic rupture, not a manufactured crisis — a confirmation that the recognition they felt at the beginning was real and lasting. The craft, it turns out, was always load-bearing. Everything else was decoration.

The [second anime season](https://butwhytho.net/2025/09/my-dress-up-darling-season-2-review/) that arrived in 2025 deepened this reading — reviewers noted that the quieter episodes, where Gojo and Marin are just working together on a costume, consistently outperformed the louder ones. "Intimacy over spectacle," as one [review put it](https://virmit.wordpress.com/2025/09/26/mduds2/). The craft sessions are where the feeling lives.

That's not a design flaw in a romance manga. That's the whole point.
