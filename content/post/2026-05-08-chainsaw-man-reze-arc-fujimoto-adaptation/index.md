Tatsuki Fujimoto's manga panels are distinctive in a way that's difficult to articulate without sounding like a backhanded compliment. They're ugly — deliberately, precisely ugly. His character faces smear at moments of emotional intensity. Proportions break when the story demands it. His line work is scratchy, dense in some places and startlingly sparse in others. When Denji cries, his face doesn't just express grief — it distorts into something almost abstract, the ink itself seeming to resist clean resolution. This is not an accident or a limitation. It is Fujimoto's argument: the world of Chainsaw Man is too unstable for polished lines.

Which creates a specific problem for any animation studio trying to adapt him. The things that make his art work are the things that animation traditionally eliminates: imprecision, distortion, the visible trace of an individual hand. When you smooth those away and give characters consistent proportions, well-crafted lighting, and beautiful backgrounds, you may have made something excellent. But you haven't made something that feels like Fujimoto.

[MAPPA gave two opposite answers to this problem](https://www.slashfilm.com/2016560/why-chainsaw-man-the-movie-reze-arcs-looks-different-show/), four years apart. Holding both answers next to each other tells you something important about how adaptation decisions actually get made.

## The First Answer: Animate Live-Action

When Ryu Nakayama directed Chainsaw Man Season 1 in 2022, he approached the adaptation with a specific, defensible philosophy. Fujimoto is a known cinephile — his panels are explicitly constructed with camera language, using close-ups, depth of field, and framing choices that read more like storyboards than traditional manga composition. Nakayama's instinct was to follow Fujimoto's inspiration rather than his execution: if Fujimoto thinks in film terms, make a film-style anime.

The result is a TV series that [Anime News Network's Kevin Cirugeda described as "animating live-action"](https://www.animenewsnetwork.com/feature/2022-11-18/the-chainsaw-man-anime-style-feels-off/.191720) — muted color palette, naturalistic lighting, grounded character movement, almost no conventional anime stylization. The opening episodes of Season 1 look like nothing else in the medium: composed carefully, taking their time, refusing the visual shorthand that anime uses by default.

It is beautiful. It is also, as a significant portion of the Japanese fanbase noticed immediately, wrong in a specific way. [The criticism that emerged](https://screenrant.com/chainsaw-man-anime-season-1-good-animation-controversy-op-ed/) is instructive: "too clean, too plain, too visually similar to other anime." The anime smoothed out Fujimoto's "delightfully ugly faces" and replaced them with soft, subtle expressions. The thick, scratchy ink lines became thin and precise. The controlled visual chaos that makes a Chainsaw Man panel physically uncomfortable to look at — in the best way — was replaced with cinematographic restraint.

Nakayama wasn't wrong about [Fujimoto's cinephile background](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tatsuki_Fujimoto). But he underweighted Fujimoto the cartoonist, the one who draws faces that collapse into abstraction when characters feel too much. [Blu-ray sales in Japan were reportedly poor](https://screenrant.com/chainsaw-man-reze-arc-character-design-changes-op-ed/), and the domestic critical reception was split — respected by animation professionals, alienating to manga readers who expected the specific textures of the source material.

## The Second Answer: Make It Look Like the Manga

By the time production began on the Reze Arc film, MAPPA had a new director — Tatsuya Yoshihara — and a new directive. [The production team created what has been described as an internal "design bible"](https://fandomwire.com/god-tier-animation-formula-chainsaw-man-reze-arc-made-a-conscious-effort-to-make-the-artstyle-just-like-the-manga/): a set of specific visual rules derived from studying Fujimoto's manga panels. The instructions included "remove eye highlights," "reduce shading," and "keep wrinkles natural." Makima's hair color was shifted to a more intense blood-red. Power's coloring became more vivid. The line art thickened. The action became more exaggerated.

The intent was explicit: make every frame look as if it came directly from Fujimoto's hand. Accept the chaotic, sketched, emotional, and messy qualities as features, not bugs. The film [received a 96% on Rotten Tomatoes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chainsaw_Man_%E2%80%93_The_Movie:_Reze_Arc), an A grade from ANN, and [$174 million worldwide at the box office](https://www.the-numbers.com/movie/Chainsaw-Man-The-Movie-Reze-Arc-(2025-Japan)), including a US opening weekend [of $17 million that topped the entire American box office](https://hypebeast.com/2025/10/chainsaw-man-the-movie-reze-arc-tops-u-s-box-office) — itself a remarkable fact given that [the US opening was twice the Japanese opening weekend gross](https://www.anitrendz.com/news/2025/10/26/chainsaw-reze-arc-passes-us100-million-worldwide-us-opening-2x-bigger-than-japanese-opening-gross).

The film looks different. [Reviews consistently noted](https://riot-us.com/2025/10/24/chainsaw-man-reze-arc-review/) that the animators showed evident joy in translating the manga's panels into movement, that the action sequences captured the disorienting energy of Fujimoto's fight choreography in ways the TV series didn't attempt. The color work — Fujimoto's manga is greyscale, which means color is an original interpretive choice in any adaptation — was praised for capturing the emotional tone of specific scenes rather than just filling in realistic hues.

## The Problem Neither Answer Fully Solves

Both approaches are principled. Both have legitimate arguments behind them. And both reveal, in different ways, the fundamental difficulty of adapting an artist whose visual language depends on imprecision.

Nakayama's live-action approach is a valid reading of Fujimoto's influences, but it mislocates the source of the manga's power. The Chainsaw Man manga works because Fujimoto's panels look unfinished — not unpolished, but volatile, as if the drawings might change if you looked away. The naturalistic restraint of Season 1 eliminated volatility in favor of craft, which is a trade-off worth examining: the result is technically superior animation that has none of the source material's physical anxiety.

Yoshihara's manga-faithful approach inverts the problem. By successfully reproducing something closer to Fujimoto's visual textures, the film achieves an energy Season 1 couldn't access. [The ANN review](https://www.animenewsnetwork.com/news/2025-10-26/chainsaw-man-the-movie-reze-arc-tops-u.s-weekend-box-office/.230351) praised its visuals, action sequences, and emotional impact in a way the TV series coverage didn't uniformly achieve. But the question of whether a design bible that says "reduce shading" and "remove eye highlights" actually produces Fujimoto's aesthetic — versus a cleaner approximation of it — is legitimately unanswered. Translating the static qualities of ink on paper into the temporal medium of film involves choices that no checklist can fully anticipate.

Look Back, the short film Studio Durian produced from Fujimoto's one-shot in 2024, took a third path entirely. [The AV Club praised its animation](https://www.avclub.com/look-back-review) for how director Kiyotaka Oshiyama channeled the source material differently: using psychological expressionism, letting the characters' emotional states shape the visual space rather than trying to replicate the manga's panel-by-panel aesthetic. Where Season 1 asked "how do we film this?" and the Reze Arc film asked "how do we draw this?", Look Back asked "what is this actually about?" — and built its visual grammar around that answer instead. The characters' movements are naturalistic and specific in ways that convey personality rather than art style; the emotional climaxes use spatial distortion (an impossibly large crowd of judging faces) that comes from Fujimoto's emotional logic rather than his visual technique. [The result](https://www.avclub.com/look-back-review), by nearly universal critical account, is [the most successful Fujimoto adaptation yet made](https://www.nippon.com/en/japan-topics/c030284/) — and the least reverential to his specific visual signature.

## What the Design Bible Actually Reveals

The specific detail that stays with me is the instruction to "remove eye highlights." This is a tiny thing. In conventional anime character design, eye highlights are standard — they convey life, warmth, presence. Removing them pushes characters toward the slightly uncanny, a flatness that registers as absence even when nothing is technically wrong. In Fujimoto's manga, this quality is pervasive: his characters look slightly hollow even when happy, which makes the moments when they crack open feel more devastating.

That MAPPA's design team identified this as a specific, nameable instruction reveals how seriously they engaged with the source material's logic. They weren't trying to make a movie that looks like Fujimoto's manga by instinct or approximation. They were studying it, extracting rules, building an aesthetic language from evidence. [The result is a film that outperformed every previous MAPPA theatrical release](https://www.koimoi.com/box-office/chainsaw-man-reze-arc-worldwide-box-office-mappas-latest-anime-feature-breaks-into-2025s-top-20-highest-grossing-films-list/) — creatively, critically, and commercially.

Whether that design bible fully captured Fujimoto, or only the most legible surface features of his style, is the article's unanswerable question. A design bible can document what an artist's pages look like. It cannot document why those choices land. The eye highlight removal works not because eyes without highlights look like Fujimoto's — they do — but because eyes without highlights feel subtly off in a way Fujimoto's entire world insists on being. The distinction between a visual rule and the aesthetic intelligence behind it is exactly what the next Chainsaw Man adaptation will have to navigate again. Probably also correctly, and also slightly wrong.

And this is what the two approaches ultimately reveal: there is no clean translation of an artist who made imprecision into principle. Every adaptation will make a bet about which Fujimoto they're serving — the filmmaker, the cartoonist, the emotional poet. The Reze Arc film placed its bet more successfully than Season 1. It is also, almost certainly, still only an approximation.
