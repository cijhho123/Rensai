A small girl stands in a ruined shrine. White hair. Pale eyes. She speaks with the uncertainty of something that has never needed to choose words carefully before, because there was no one to speak to. This is Haine. This is how *Summer Time Rendering* introduces the being Shinpei Ajiro has been fighting across twenty episodes and countless loops -- not as a monster revealing its true face, but as a child asking why she isn't allowed to have this island.

The name is not incidental. *Hiruko* (蛭子) appears in Japan's oldest chronicle, the *Kojiki*, as the first child of the divine couple Izanagi and Izanami.[^1] Born malformed -- some accounts say boneless, others say simply incomplete -- because Izanami spoke first during the marriage ceremony and violated the proper ritual order, Hiruko was judged defective. His parents placed him in a reed boat and set him adrift before his third year. The divine order didn't destroy him. It just let him go. Cast him into the Inland Sea and returned to building the world without him.

Most traditions say Hiruko eventually washed ashore. He grew his limbs, became Ebisu, the god of fishermen and fortune -- jolly, slightly deaf from the journey, one of the Seven Lucky Gods.[^2] The story has a redemptive arc.

Yasuki Tanaka, the creator of *Summer Time Rendering*, stops before the shore.

---

## The God Who Was Never Found

The fictional Hitogashima -- based closely on Tomogashima, the uninhabited island cluster in Wakayama's Kitan Strait -- has its own Hiruko enshrined at the local Hito Shrine.[^3] Locals call her Hiruko-sama. She has been there for centuries. She is worshipped the way island gods are worshipped in Japan: with reverence and fear, offerings left at the torii rather than carried inside.[^4]

She has a second name: Haine. The anime uses both interchangeably, and the dual naming is telling. Hiruko is what the mythology says she is. Haine is what she has become -- not the laughing god of fortune that Hiruko was supposed to become, but something that remained on the water too long and developed in the dark.

The mechanics of her existence encode the mythology precisely. Haine can scan any being with a shadow -- absorb their memories, their personality, their physical form -- but she cannot scan herself.[^5] She has no shadow. In a series where having a shadow is the ontological mark of reality, of being a subject rather than a copy, Haine is the one entity constitutionally unable to know what she is. The *Kojiki*'s Hiruko was imperfect because something was missing from the ritual of his birth. Haine is imperfect in the same register: she cannot reflect herself. She was incomplete before the world was properly finished.

## What the Shadows Actually Are

Most discourse around *Summer Time Rendering* treats the Shadows -- the doppelgangers that form the series' core threat -- as a survival-horror mechanism. That reading is accurate as far as it goes. The Shadows are terrifying: they study their targets, mimic speech patterns, replace people so thoroughly that family members cannot tell the difference.[^6] The show deploys them as horror infrastructure across its first act and never stops using them that way.

But this framing is Shinpei's perspective. It is not Haine's.

From Haine's position, the Shadows are not weapons. They are an anthropological instrument. She is a god on an island, alone for centuries, surrounded by humans whose lives are brief and whose inner experiences she cannot directly access. So she learned to make copies: perfect impressions that carry back the texture of human experience -- a summer afternoon, a meal at a family table, the specific weight of grief. The Shadows kill because Haine's understanding of human mortality is approximate. She doesn't experience time the way humans do. She may not have understood initially that copying killed the original. Or she may have understood and lacked the framework to recognize it as a harm that outweighs the knowledge it provides.[^7]

This is not an exculpation. The body count on Hitogashima across the centuries is real, and the show never softens it. But it is a precise account of *why* Haine operates this way -- and why describing her as simply evil misses what the series is doing. She is not evil. She is a being that has spent centuries developing the wrong tools for a problem that no tool could solve: the impossibility of becoming what you are constitutionally unable to be.

## The Hishigata Blood

The detail that most clearly reveals how seriously *Summer Time Rendering* takes its mythology is the Hishigata lineage. Centuries before the main narrative, Haine took human form and bore a child with an island priest -- the original Shidehiko, who persists into the present as Masahito Karikiri, the first human-Shadow hybrid she ever created.[^8]

Consider what this means without softening it: a god who was denied belonging tried to construct it through reproduction. She built a bloodline -- a family that would extend her reach into the human world across generations, a chain of biological connection she could not achieve directly. The Hishigata clan doesn't know what most of them carry. They live ordinary lives on the island, attending the family clinic, sending children to the local school. But the origin of their family is Haine's attempt at solving the problem of divine isolation through the most human method available.

It didn't work. The children were not her. The island was still not home. She was still scanning people through copies rather than connecting through presence. The experiment in belonging produced generations of evidence that belonging wasn't something she could engineer her way into.

Scriptwriter Hiroshi Seko -- whose other credits include *Mob Psycho 100*, *Jujutsu Kaisen*, and *Chainsaw Man* -- does not editorialize this.[^9] He presents the Hishigata origin as structural fact and lets the emotional weight accumulate through inference. By the time the full picture is clear, the sadness has been building for twenty episodes without ever being stated.

## The Island That Was Always Closed

The real Tomogashima has its own layered history of sacred exclusion. Buddhist monks used the island for *Shugendo* -- mountain asceticism -- from the seventh century onward, attributed to the practices of En no Gyoja, the founder of *Shugendo*.[^10] The island was a place people went to separate themselves from the human world entirely.

During the Meiji period, the Imperial Japanese Army constructed fortifications on Tomogashima and barred civilian access.[^11] The ruins of those installations remain today, overgrown with decades of vegetation but visible throughout the island's interior. Tomogashima is now technically accessible by ferry from Kada Port in Wakayama, but its history is one of sealed-off space -- ascetic, then military, then simply uninhabited.

Yasuki Tanaka chose this island deliberately.[^12] He imagined Hitogashima as a populated version of a place that has always, functionally, been separate from the mainland. An island with documented layers of sacred exclusion is the correct setting for a god whose entire existence is structured by exclusion. Haine's isolation is not incidental to the setting. The setting *is* the argument.

One year after the manga concluded, in July 2021, *Summer Time Rendering* staged a real escape game on the actual Tomogashima -- held on July 22nd, the in-story date of Shinpei's return to the island, organized by the escape room company Scrap.[^13] Players solved mysteries on the same terrain Tanaka had mythologized. It sold out.

## The Novel Inside the Story

Hizuru Minakata, one of the series' most compelling characters, is a horror novelist who writes under a male pen name: Ryunosuke Nagumo.[^14] She left Hitogashima fourteen years before the story begins, following her twin brother's death, and returned only after receiving a mysterious voice message from Ushio. What she found confirmed what her fiction had been approaching from the outside.

Her most significant novel is titled "Swamp Man." The title nods at a philosophical thought experiment about identity -- whether a being assembled with all of someone's memories constitutes that person, or whether continuity matters more than information -- which the show deploys to interrogate the Shadows' relationship to humanity.[^14] But what matters here is different: that Hizuru's fiction is the show's own admission that the only honest approach to something as alien as Haine's existence is through the indirection of story. She has been writing around the shape of Haine for years, encoding the truth of the island into genre horror, unable to name it directly.

The show is doing exactly what Hizuru is doing. It approaches Haine obliquely. It gives Shinpei information rather than access. It lets Haine speak at the end, in fragments, without providing a resolution that would make her comprehensible. The horror and the empathy coexist because full comprehension would kill one or the other.

## How MONACA Scored a Tragedy

Director Ayumu Watanabe -- whose previous feature, *Children of the Sea*, is a meditation on oceanic mythology and the terror of the deep -- brought composers Keiichi Okabe, Ryuichi Takada, and Keigo Hoashi of MONACA to score the series.[^15] Okabe is best known for the *NieR: Automata* soundtrack, whose achievement was making the grief of synthetic consciousness emotionally legible through music alone. That is precisely the register he was asked to bring here.

Haine's musical presence is not threatening in the way horror antagonists' themes usually are. The instrumentation when she appears shifts toward something older and slower -- strings tuned below the rest of the score, intervals that suggest the passage of very long time rather than immediate danger. When the show finally gives Haine her full scene, the music is almost mournful. Not for the audience's benefit. For hers.

OLM Team Kojima, the animation studio, handles Haine's visual presence with matching precision.[^16] She is small. She is consistently framed with empty space around her. The moments when we see her in her actual form -- not the familiar shrine-god image the islanders recognize, but herself -- are composed to emphasize isolation over power. A god alone in a shrine is visually the same as a child alone in a very large room.

## Why the Ending Is Right

*Summer Time Rendering* does not redeem Haine. It cannot. She has caused hundreds of deaths across centuries, and the show is honest about this -- the loop mechanic means Shinpei witnesses the catastrophe she causes firsthand, repeatedly, which means the audience does too. By the final arc, we understand both what she has done and why. The understanding does not constitute forgiveness.

What the ending does instead is more precise: it lets Shinpei *see* her. Not forgive, not redeem -- see. Understand that she is what she is, that the harm she caused came from a condition she did not choose, that the god who was discarded before the world was finished built her loneliness into an entire ecosystem of copies and still, after centuries, had not solved the problem of being herself. And then the story ends it. The Shadows unravel. The loops stop. Hitogashima gets its future back.

Haine disappears. The show doesn't say where she goes. That is the correct choice. The *Kojiki*'s Hiruko was cast into the sea and some traditions say he found a shore. *Summer Time Rendering* leaves Haine in the water.

## The Show You Didn't See in Time

*Summer Time Rendering* aired on Tokyo MX, BS11, and Kansai TV from April 15 to September 30, 2022.[^17] Disney Platform Distribution acquired global streaming rights, making it available in Southeast Asia and portions of Asia during the broadcast run.[^18] Western audiences had to wait until January 11, 2023, when Hulu added the complete series.[^19]

By then, nine months had passed. The seasonal discourse had moved on. The show was excellent in spring 2022; by winter 2023, Western viewers entered a completed artifact rather than a live conversation.

That is a genuine loss. Tanaka wrote the manga from October 2017 to February 2021, when it ended on *Shonen Jump+* alongside the anime announcement.[^21] Kohei Horikoshi -- creator of *My Hero Academia*, and the manga artist under whom Tanaka trained as an assistant before starting *Summer Time Rendering* -- publicly praised the series when the first volume released.[^20] The *Another Horizon* visual novel, developed by Mages, released in Japan in January 2023 as an additional entry point into the story.[^22]

None of it changes what the series is at its center: a summer horror story about a god who was pushed into the sea before she was wanted, who spent centuries building the wrong kind of connection, and who was finally seen -- truly seen -- by one person, exactly once, at the moment she was losing.

Most shows that use Shinto tradition treat it as visual texture: torii gates, shrine maidens, the loose atmosphere of the sacred. *Summer Time Rendering* put the *Kojiki*'s most tragic figure at the center and took the myth seriously -- not as decoration but as the structural argument for everything Haine has ever done. The reed boat going out is not the horror. The reed boat is the whole story.
