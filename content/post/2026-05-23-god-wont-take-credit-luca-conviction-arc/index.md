The Conviction arc ends in two simultaneous events. Griffith arrives at the base of the Tower of Albion in a blaze of white light, physically reborn, surrounded by tens of thousands of dead.[^1] The spirits of the original Band of the Hawk appear at his side. The narrative treats this as the arc's culminating image: the miracle Midland has been waiting for, delivered at a price the survivors do not know to count.

But somewhere in that same chaos, Luca makes sure Nina survives. Again. Even after Nina tried to have her burned. There is no light. No cosmic machinery. No Ghost-Band, no God Hand, no behelit. Just a woman who decided, again, not to abandon someone who probably deserves to be abandoned.

The story does not light that moment up. The manga does not cut to Luca's face on the final page. She does not get the arc's closing image. But she should, because what Luca does in the Conviction arc is the thing Berserk has been spending seventeen volumes building toward a question about and then refuses to answer with thunder: what does genuine goodness actually look like in this world?

The answer is not what Griffith is. The answer is what Luca is.

---

## The World She Lives In

The Conviction arc runs through volumes 14 through 21 of the Dark Horse edition.[^2] It is set in the immediate aftermath of the Eclipse: Midland is a country in collapse. The hundred-years' war the Band of the Hawk helped win has been replaced by pestilence, invasion, and the creeping supernatural dread of an astral layer thinning under the weight of the God Hand's operations. Refugees flood toward St. Albion, a fortified city built around the Tower of Conviction, seeking protection from the Holy See.

The arc is Berserk at its most sustained in a single location. It is not a dungeon run or a battle sequence. It is a pressure cooker, a siege, a population under extraordinary duress, organized by a religious institution with its own specific doctrine about who deserves protection and what the cost of heresy is.

![Berserk Volume 42, the first new compiled volume since Studio Gaga and Kouji Mori resumed the manga after Miura's death in 2021.](https://cdn.animenewsnetwork.com/thumbnails/max400x400/cms/news.7/214572/berserk42.jpg)

This is the environment Miura builds before introducing Luca. By the time she appears, we understand precisely what she is operating inside: a closed, desperate, institutionally violent world where goodness requires theological justification or it does not count. The Holy See classifies people. Those outside the classification receive Mozgus. Those inside receive protection conditional on continued compliance. The refugees at the base of the Tower live entirely outside any classification except desperate.

Miura began serializing Berserk in Hakusensha's Monthly Animal House in 1989, with a prototype one-shot published in Monthly Comicomi a year earlier.[^3] By the time the Conviction arc was running, he had spent the better part of a decade constructing a world structured by the Idea of Evil: the theological engine underneath Berserk's cosmology, the unnamed force that the God Hand serves, which Miura's controversial Chapter 83 reveals as having been summoned by the collective unconscious of humanity's accumulated despair.[^4] The world is designed by suffering. Its supernatural architecture runs on it. Every apostle was made in a moment of human extremity, every sacrifice feeds the machine.

This is the context in which Luca operates.

## Three Frameworks, None of Them Hers

**Guts** arrives at Albion looking for Casca. That is the entirety of his motivation. He is not there to save refugees, challenge the Holy See, or perform any heroic function. The refugees who begin treating him as a demon-slaying protector are projecting a narrative onto him that he does not seek and would prefer to be rid of. His violence is purposive and specific: find Casca, survive the night, eliminate any apostle in the way. The people he saves incidentally are incidental.

This is not heroism. It is a man clinging to the last thread of his own life with both hands. He tolerates Isidro because Isidro is occasionally useful. He tolerates Farnese and Jerome because asking them to leave would take energy he does not have. The Conviction arc gives Guts exactly one thing he is fighting for, and it is a woman traumatized past the point of recognizing him. His care is singular, purposive, and structurally incapable of extending past its object.

**Mozgus** is the arc's most formally interesting villain, and Miura was deliberate about what he represents. The Berserk Official Guidebook confirms that Mozgus was directly inspired by Bernardo Gui, the Grand Inquisitor of Toulouse who appears in Umberto Eco's *The Name of the Rose* (1980).[^5][^6] In Eco's novel, Gui is the text's closest thing to a pure institutional antagonist: a man whose faith is absolute, whose commitment to rooting out heresy is total, and who is terrifying precisely because he is not venal or secretly corrupt. He believes. His cruelty is an expression of sincere devotion.

Miura imports this into Mozgus with exacting precision. Mozgus is not hypocritical. The self-mortification, the stone floor he beats his face against in penitence, the scripture he recites while his subordinates practice their instruments of torture: none of it is performance. The arc goes out of its way to establish that his faith is genuine, that his suffering is real, that his care for the doctrine is not a mask over something more recognizable as evil.

This makes him the arc's most disturbing figure, and also its clearest illustration of what conditional goodness looks like at scale. Mozgus can care deeply for the institution. He can care for the saved souls within its definition. He cannot care for a refugee-prostitute who falls outside it. His goodness has clear edges. It burns at the edges.

**Farnese** begins the Conviction arc as genuine antagonist: commander of the Holy Chain Knights, driven by institutional loyalty and unprocessed trauma that Miura encodes into her behavior with uncomfortable precision. The self-flagellation, the authority she derives from religious position, the difficulty she has separating discipline from cruelty: these are established as pathological before they are presented as pitiable. What redeems Farnese across the subsequent arcs is not a better theology but human connection. Connection to Guts. To Casca. To Schierke. She leaves doctrine behind not because she finds a stronger doctrine, but because she finds specific people she is unwilling to let die.

Her transformation has a precondition: she has to encounter the right people first. Farnese's care is conditional on relationship. It expands as her relationships expand.

Luca does not have a precondition.

## The Woman the Story Doesn't Frame

Luca runs a group of prostitutes in the refugee camp at St. Albion. She has no supernatural power, no destiny, no Brand of Sacrifice, no behelit, no army. She has a community of women she has organized to survive in conditions that are actively trying to kill them, and she protects that community the way someone protects a family: not because they chose her, but because she chose them.

![Berserk's Conviction arc takes place in and around the Tower of Albion, a medieval fortress-church that becomes the site of a second Eclipse-scale sacrifice.](https://static.wikia.nocookie.net/berserk/images/5/5e/Tower_of_Conviction.jpg/revision/latest/scale-to-width-down/400)

Nina is her hardest case. Nina is cowardly, self-serving, willing to implicate others in heretic rituals to save herself, willing to betray Luca's trust repeatedly in circumstances where that betrayal carries mortal stakes. Nina is also the character the Berserk fandom developed one of its stranger relationships with: r/Ninahate is a real subreddit, with the explicit purpose of cataloguing and amplifying hatred for her.[^7] The visceral disgust Nina generates in readers is telling. She is not evil in the way apostles are evil. She is just weak, frightened, and willing to do genuinely terrible things when frightened. She is the person in the extreme situation who fails to be heroic, repeatedly, and in ways that have real consequences for people who were trying to help her.

Luca keeps choosing her anyway.

When the manga dramatizes the implicit tension around this choice, Luca does not give a speech about the inherent worth of all people or produce a philosophy of unconditional care. She just keeps doing it. The manga's most pointed observation about her is precisely this: she has no ideology to point to. She does not frame her behavior as sacrifice, does not treat it as heroism, does not explain it in terms that would make it legible to the arc's institutional frameworks. She is not performing virtue for any audience, divine or human. She does not appear to be performing it for herself either. She just keeps showing up for people who need something, and one of those people is Nina, and that does not change the arithmetic.

This structural difference from the other three figures is the arc's central move. Guts' care is singular and purposive. Mozgus' care is institutional and conditional. Farnese's care is developing through encounter and conditioned by relationship. Luca's care is unconditional and, crucially, un-narrated. The manga never provides the interiority to explain it. We never get the scene where Luca thinks to herself that Nina deserves another chance. We only get the behavior, and the behavior is consistent regardless of what Nina does.

## What the God Hand Cannot Use

Berserk's cosmological premise, spelled out in the suppressed Chapter 83 and embedded throughout the story's architecture, is that the Idea of Evil emerged from human despair.[^8] The God Hand are its instruments. The apostle system runs on human beings in moments of extremity choosing self-transformation over loss. Every sacrifice, every behelit activation, every miraculous resurrection like the one that ends the Conviction arc: all of it is suffering converted into cosmic power, human pain metabolized into the story's supernatural engine.

The Conviction arc ends with Griffith's resurrection requiring the deaths of tens of thousands at Albion — refugees, soldiers, everyone bearing the Brand of Sacrifice who happened to be there. The narrative does not flinch from the scale of this. It also does not pretend that the resulting miracle is purely good: Griffith is reborn into physical form by a mass sacrifice that mirrors the original Eclipse structurally — a collective of humans consumed to elevate one transcendent figure — except the victims this time never chose him, never followed him, never knew what he was.[^9] The story calls it a miracle and lets the image do its work.

Luca's care generates none of this. It produces no mythology, no collective faith, no sacrifice the system can run on. Nobody burns in her name. Nobody ascends. The God Hand cannot use what Luca does because what Luca does has no narrative around it. It does not represent anything larger than itself. It does not feed the machine.

This is, the Conviction arc argues quietly, the one thing the world of Berserk cannot metabolize: care that has no story around it. Guts' grief is spectacular and the manga builds chapters around it. Griffith's ambition is operatic and reshapes reality. Mozgus' faith produces a grotesque kind of power. Even Nina's cowardice feeds the narrative economy of sacrifice and betrayal. Luca just keeps showing up.

## Why the Fandom Misses It

The Conviction arc is regularly described as underrated within Berserk's fan community.[^10] Part of this is the Golden Age's overwhelming gravitational pull: the Eclipse is Berserk's most discussed event, and the decade of story preceding it tends to be treated as the manga's essential body. Everything after reads as aftermath. The Conviction arc, which is neither a flashback to the Band of the Hawk nor a forward movement toward Griffith's final defeat, occupies uncomfortable middle ground.

But part of it is structural. The arc's real argument is made by the character who least demands attention. Luca is not the protagonist. She is not the most powerful person in the arc, not the most visually spectacular, not the most emotionally available. She shows up, does what she does, and does not particularly invite analysis. The fandom argument that tends to occur around the arc is about Nina, about whether she deserved Luca's protection, about whether her cowardice is forgivable or contemptible. That argument is, in its own way, exactly the question the arc is raising.

Because Luca's answer to that question is: it doesn't matter whether Nina deserves it. That's the whole point.

Berserk has 70 million copies in circulation worldwide as of August 2025, including 9 million in English.[^11] Studio Gaga and Kouji Mori have been continuing the manga since June 2022, following Miura's death from acute aortic dissection on May 6, 2021.[^12][^13] Volume 43 shipped in Japan in August 2025, the continuation pressing forward into the Fantasia arc that Miura was constructing near the end of his life.[^14]

The manga is ongoing. The Conviction arc is finished. And its most honest argument is still the quietest one: the God Hand can resurrect itself in white light at the base of a tower of corpses. Luca can make sure Nina survives the night. One of those things has a story. The other is what goodness actually looks like when stripped of one.
