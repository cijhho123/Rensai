When Kow Otani talks about the Shadow of the Colossus score, the word he reaches for isn't triumphant. It isn't thrilling. He calls it "more in common with a prayer or a requiem." He describes it as music that "feels like you might have saved someone you love, but you couldn't save everyone you loved." This is not the language of a composer describing a victory soundtrack. It is the language of someone writing music for grief.

Seven years after Shadow of the Colossus, Otani composed the score for Another — P.A. Works' 2012 horror anime about a class haunted by a dead student who keeps returning among the living. [The two works appear to have nothing in common](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kow_Otani): one is a PlayStation action-adventure, the other a twelve-episode anime adapted from Yukito Ayatsuji's light novel. But they share something deeper than a composer credit. They share a moral problem — and the same musical philosophy for inhabiting it.

## The Colossus Problem

Shadow of the Colossus asks you to kill sixteen massive creatures to resurrect a dead girl. The colossi are peaceful until provoked. They do not attack unless threatened. The game never confirms that what you're doing is right. [Otani's compositional approach](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/audio/the-art-of-shadow-of-the-colossus-5-6-music), as analyzed by Game Developer, reflects this: "Kow Otani compositions respond to a constant desire to blur things and blend feelings; nothing is black or white, good or bad, sad or cheerful."

The battle sequences are scored in three phases that track emotional states rather than narrative beats. [The ost.co analysis of the score](https://www.ost.co/996-shadow-of-the-colossus.html) describes these as the realization of what must be done, the guilt of doing it, and the grim determination to follow through anyway. You can hear this in "Revived Power" — the main combat theme — which opens with unsettling dissonance before the orchestra builds toward something that sounds almost like resolve, but never like triumph. After the colossus falls, "Demise of the Ritual" plays: haunting choral vocals and poignant strings that don't celebrate the kill. The music treats the death as a loss even when the player won. It never does anything else.

This is Otani's stated philosophy. In the [PlayStation Blog interview about the 2018 remake](https://blog.playstation.com/2018/01/25/shadow-of-the-colossus-composer-interview-iam8bit-2-lp-vinyl-revealed/), he confirms that the score was built as "a prayer or requiem" — and that "[Sony's video interview with him] talks about the music's sense of decay, and how art doesn't always have to be positive." That last clause is the key to everything Otani does. Art doesn't always have to be positive. The score can accompany harm without celebrating it. Music can sustain moral ambiguity without resolving it.

## The Same Problem in Classroom 3-3

The premise of Another is structurally similar in a way that's easy to miss. Class 3-3 at Yomiyama North Middle School is haunted by a curse: one student each year is a returned dead, a presence that death has allowed to re-enter the living world without knowing they've crossed back. The curse kills everyone around the "Another" until the students identify and eliminate the intruder. They must kill someone who doesn't know they're dead, someone who experiences themselves as entirely normal, in order to stop the dying.

This is, again, the problem of necessary harm. The Another is not malevolent. They are not a monster. [The curse operates through institutional belonging](https://www.animenewsnetwork.com/news/2011-08-27/anime-another-with-noizi-ito-p.a-works-debuts-in-january), not through any particular malice on the Another's part — the horror is collective, systemic, activated by enrollment. The students who hunt the Another are not heroes vanquishing an evil. They are a class committing what amounts to a mercy killing against someone who cannot comprehend why it's happening. The moral structure is identical to Wander's colossi hunts. The colossi don't know they need to die. The Another doesn't know they're already dead.

Otani's score for Another tracks this. [The CBR analysis of the series' stylistic elements](https://www.cbr.com/another-stylistic-elements-make-best-horror-anime/) notes that the music ranges from "soft music-box melodies to orchestral arrangements" — and that the silence between them creates its own atmosphere of "breathless anticipation." The key observation is the music-box register. Music boxes are delicate, mechanical, slightly out of place. They suggest childhood and fragility and things that should be innocent but aren't. In the context of a horror anime, they produce a specific kind of dread: the sense of something small and fragile placed in front of something inevitable.

## Beautiful and Innocent and Dark

The Another soundtrack, [released on July 27, 2012](https://vgmdb.net/album/33080) by Kadokawa Anime, contains only 13 tracks. That restraint is itself significant. The [general critical description of the album](http://elmundodelscore.blogspot.com/2021/02/another-soundtrack.html) calls it "dark and mysterious work, but also beautiful and delicate, almost innocent at times, with these two styles combining and contrasting throughout." The darker elements use "ambient and chaotic melodies that convey a sense of anguish." The lighter ones are nearly pastoral.

This is the same architecture as the Shadow of the Colossus score: serenity and dread in close proximity, neither one dissolving the other. In SotC, the prologue begins with melancholy before opening toward discovery. In Another, ["Mei and Kouichi's Solitude" (Track 07)](https://another.fandom.com/wiki/Another_OST) plays during Mei Misaki's conversations about her past — calm, soothing, tender — as if the score is refusing to let the horror consume the character's humanity even in a show built on systematic death. This sits in the same album as "Cursed Tradition" and "Omen of Death," and the contrast is not accidental. Otani gives the tragedy space even inside the terror.

[The My Shiny Toy Robots review](https://www.myshinytoyrobots.com/2012/04/anime-review-47-another.html) of Another notes that "the background music usually comprises eerie wind tones at subtle and mysterious scenes, and a creepy unrelenting orchestra that plays when the suspense and action kicks in." The contrast is deliberate. Otani is calibrating the score to a moral register, not just a tension register. When the camera lingers on a character before a death, the music doesn't just build dread — it preserves something about the person being watched. It mourns them before the fact. That is what a requiem does.

## What a Requiem Actually Does

The technical definition of a requiem is a Mass for the repose of the souls of the dead. Kow Otani is not writing liturgical music, but the structural purpose is the same: the requiem does not judge the dead. It commends them to something beyond the present. It does not ask whether the dying deserved to die. It is, by design, non-judgmental about the fact of death. It honors the loss without litigating the cause.

This is exactly what Otani's Another score does for each death in the series. [The AnimeRants review](https://animerants.net/2019/10/15/anime-review-another/) observes that the music is "really good and fits the show really well, making it feel very eerie and tense" — but the more precise observation is that the score holds both the tension and the weight of each life being lost. [Another's deaths are often criticized as gratuitous](https://www.cbr.com/another-stylistic-elements-make-best-horror-anime/), as Final Destination-style spectacles. What separates them from pure exploitation is partly Otani's score, which keeps asking the audience to feel something beyond shock.

In Shadow of the Colossus, the epilogue music contains "slow passages with extended pauses suggesting the last moments of an agonizing living being," [according to the Game Developer analysis](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/audio/the-art-of-shadow-of-the-colossus-5-6-music). The colossus you killed was an agonizing living being. The music makes you live in that fact. Otani does not cut away. He holds the grief open and asks you to sit in it.

## The Outlier and the Philosophy

[P.A. Works is primarily a studio of warmth](https://www.animenewsnetwork.com/encyclopedia/company.php?id=4769), and Another is the only horror series in their catalog — which is part of why the score's moral weight lands so hard. There's no house style for comfort here. The Another is eliminated, but the class committed a murder, and the score doesn't let them — or the audience — forget that.

[Kow Otani's career spans anime, film, and game composition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kow_Otani). [His influences include Stravinsky, Bartók, Led Zeppelin, and Björk](https://www.resetera.com/threads/shadow-of-the-colossus-ps4-kow-otani-interview.18847/) — a combination that explains the eclecticism that lets him move between action scoring and chamber music within a single project. The [TV Tropes entry on his music](https://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/Music/KowOtani) notes his ability to shift registers within a single project, from "epic action" to "intimate character themes" — which describes exactly what the Another OST does across its 13 tracks. He built a score that moves between terror and tenderness because the story required both to exist simultaneously. His understanding of SotC was that the game's drama fit the music because both were built around the same moral question, not because the music was written to match beats.

Another presented that question in a different form. A class of schoolchildren. A curse. A person who doesn't know they've already died. An elimination that must happen anyway. Otani wrote music for that in the same way he wrote music for the colossus hunts: without judgment, without celebration, without catharsis. A prayer for what you cannot spare.

Art doesn't always have to be positive. Sometimes the most honest score is the one that holds the loss open after the credits roll.
