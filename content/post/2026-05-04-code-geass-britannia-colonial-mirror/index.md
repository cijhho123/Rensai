Japan was never colonized. This is not a minor historical detail — it is one of the defining facts of modern Japanese national identity. While every other major Asian nation experienced direct European or American occupation, Japan industrialized on its own terms, forced open its own ports in a negotiated if asymmetrical process, and within fifty years became an imperial power itself. The absence of colonial subjugation is foundational to how Japan understands itself.

So why is the most beloved anti-colonial narrative in modern Japanese anime a story about Japan being colonized?

*Code Geass: Lelouch of the Rebellion* aired on MBS from [October 2006](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Code_Geass) and became one of the defining anime of its decade. Its premise — Japan, renamed "Area 11," occupied and stripped of its identity by the Holy Britannian Empire, with a masked rebel leading the resistance — hit something deep in its audience. The show ran 50 episodes across two seasons, launched a film trilogy, and remains in [MAL's all-time top rankings](https://myanimelist.net/anime/1575/Code_Geass__Hangyaku_no_Lelouch) nearly two decades later. Its core fantasy, of fighting back against a system that erases who you are, clearly worked.

What's less often discussed is the specific shape of that fantasy — and what it looks like when you hold it up against actual history.

## Britannia Is Not a Political Statement

Director Gorō Taniguchi has [stated in interviews](https://fullfrontal.moe/goro-taniguchi-interview/) that he is not particularly interested in politics and that Britannia's primary function was to give Lelouch something large enough to fight. His concern was narrative infrastructure: he needed a credible totalitarian system, global in scale, capable of occupying Japan and generating seven years of resistance. The alternate history setup — in which the British Empire won the American Revolution, evolved into a global superpower, and eventually conquered Japan — is worldbuilding in service of character, not allegory. In [a 2008 interview translated by fan archivists](https://codetrainwreck.tumblr.com/post/179230716932/goro-taniguchis-roman-album-interview), Taniguchi focused his commentary on production pressures and character arc decisions; the geopolitical framing barely came up. He built an empire. He moved on to the story.

Ichirō Ōkouchi, who wrote the scripts, has [described the show's origins](https://codegeass.fandom.com/wiki/Ichir%C5%8D_%C5%8Ckouchi) as emerging from a desire to tell the story of a protagonist who gains power to destroy an oppressive system — the empire came second, as the system Lelouch needed to destroy. The initial concept involved "two young boys" at a military academy; the geopolitical backdrop crystallized around them.

This is an important admission: the creators built an empire without particularly intending what that empire would mean. And yet the specific choices they made — about what Britannia does to Japan, how it does it, what it takes away — are not arbitrary. They follow a recognizable logic. The question is where they learned that logic.

## The Mirror Mechanics

Look at what Britannia actually does to Japan in *Code Geass*, stripped of the mecha framing. It conquers the country militarily using technologically superior forces. It dismantles the Japanese government and installs a colonial administration. It renames the territory — Japan becomes "Area 11" — and renames the people: Japanese citizens who refuse to assimilate are called "Elevens," a dehumanizing numerical designation that replaces their national identity. Those who collaborate with Britannia can become "Honorary Britannians," gaining access to resources and social advancement unavailable to those who resist. Those who do not live in marginalized settlements, cut off from full participation in Britannian society. Resistance movements form, fighting not just for political sovereignty but for the right to call themselves Japanese.

Now compare this to the mechanics of Japanese colonial rule in Korea, which Japan administered from [1910 to 1945](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Korea_under_Japanese_rule). Japan dismantled the Korean government. It renamed the territory a protectorate, then formally annexed it. It implemented *[sōshi-kaimei](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/S%C5%8Dshi-kaimei)* — a policy enacted from 1940 onward that pressured Koreans to adopt Japanese surnames and given names. [As the Pacific Atrocities Education project documents](https://www.pacificatrocities.org/blog/what-does-colonization-look-like-the-case-of-soshi-kaimei-in-colonial-korea), those who refused found themselves locked out of mail delivery, ration cards, and virtually every mechanism of civic life, erasing the family names they had carried for generations. Korean cultural expressions were suppressed; the Japanese language was imposed as the official medium of education and government. Koreans who fully assimilated into Japanese identity could achieve mobility within the colonial system. Those who refused were locked out. Resistance movements formed, fighting for the right to exist as Korean.

The structural match is not a coincidence in the sense that Ōkouchi was secretly writing an allegory about Korea. It is a coincidence in a more interesting sense: the show's creators drew on the general template of colonial occupation, and that template, as it exists in the archive of twentieth-century history, was substantially authored by Japan. They built the empire, put Japan under it, and didn't notice they were describing what Japan had done.

## What the Fantasy Is Actually For

This inversion has a specific psychological function. Japan's relationship to its own imperial history is [notoriously unresolved](https://apjjf.org/park-yuha/2923/article). Unlike Germany, Japan never underwent a structured national reckoning with what it did in Asia — no Japanese equivalent of Nuremberg, no equivalent of Germany's constitutional renunciation of nationalism, no equivalent of the public pedagogy around the Holocaust that reshaped German civic identity. Japan's war memory is fragmented, contested, and — among large portions of the political establishment — actively minimized or denied. The 2001 [Japanese history textbook controversy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japanese_history_textbook_controversies) is emblematic: the Fusosha-published *New History Textbook*, approved by the Ministry of Education over the protests of Japanese historians and educators, emphasized Japan's "modernizing" role in Korea while minimizing colonial brutality. It sparked protests in Seoul and Beijing. It was adopted in under one percent of Japanese schools — and yet the government approved it, which is its own kind of statement. Code Geass began production five years after that controversy. What gets omitted from official memory tends to resurface elsewhere.

*Code Geass* lets Japanese audiences feel colonization from the inside. The humiliation of being called an "Eleven" rather than Japanese. The indignity of watching Britannians occupy the reconstructed parts of cities while the displaced population lives in shantytowns. The impossible position of the "Honorary Britannian" — collaborating for survival while betraying your community. The moral weight of taking up arms against a system that won't yield to any other logic. These feelings are rendered with real craft. They land.

What they're simulating is something Japan never experienced as a nation — but something Japan inflicted on others. The fantasy of resisting Britannia is, at some level, a fantasy of being the Korean resistance fighter, the Taiwanese independence activist, the Chinese partisan. It is a fantasy made possible by the actual history being absent from public memory. If Japan had genuinely processed what it did in Asia, it is harder to imagine this inversion working without friction.

The [analyst writing for *When Anime Past Meets Present*](https://animehistory.wordpress.com/2008/06/10/is-code-geass-arguing-for-militaristic-nationalism-or-against-it/) argues that *Code Geass* is anti-nationalist precisely because it punishes characters who express ethnic chauvinism — the bigoted Britannians die, the show advocates something like universalist humanism. This reading is not wrong, but it focuses on the show's explicit messaging while the structural work happens one level below: at the level of which position the audience is invited to inhabit, which history they're asked to feel.

## The Show Knows This, Partially

*Code Geass* is not entirely unaware of its own complications. Lelouch vi Britannia — the rebel, the liberator — is himself a Britannian prince. His claim to lead the Japanese resistance is structurally paradoxical: he fights for Japan while remaining, by blood and training, the thing he's fighting against. This is not incidental. It's baked into the show's title. [The Animanachronism essay from 2008](https://animanachronism.wordpress.com/2008/04/26/colonial-code-geass-and-narnian-nerve-gas/) noted that the show "uses a stylized British Empire fantasy as Japan's Narnia" — a space where Japan can imaginatively inhabit positions unavailable to it in actual history, "safely" because the frame is so stylized.

The show also refuses to let the Black Knights off the hook. The resistance movement is riven by factionalism, opportunism, and violence against civilians. Lelouch's absolute obedience power — the Geass — means the liberation movement is literally built on coercion. Episode after episode complicates the moral calculus of anti-colonial violence. By the time of the series finale, the moral landscape is thorough wreckage; nobody's hands are clean.

But complicating the ethics of resistance is not the same as acknowledging whose template you're borrowing. *Code Geass* is sophisticated about the moral costs of revolution. It is less sophisticated — or simply incurious — about the historical origins of the structures it's depicting. The show runs [50 episodes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Code_Geass) and never once asks why Japan, of all nations, feels the occupation scenario with such visceral resonance. It doesn't need to. The resonance is doing its work at a level below the narrative's explicit concerns.

## The Borrowed Wound

There's a version of this analysis that becomes a simple indictment: *Code Geass* is Japanese nationalist wish-fulfillment dressed as anti-imperialism. That reading exists, and there's something to it. [Jin Johnson's essay](https://medium.com/@jlj009/code-geass-lelouch-of-the-rebellion-and-the-accountability-of-japan-f4ac07a4d8a7) makes the case directly, arguing the show is "a story told from the perspective of an oppressed Japan, when historically Japan has been the colonizer," and that this fantasy is only possible because Japan never genuinely reckoned with its own conduct.

But the simple indictment misses what makes this interesting. *Code Geass* is not propaganda. It's a show that reached for the most emotionally available template for occupation and resistance — the one that exists throughout twentieth-century history — and built something compelling from it. The fact that Japan wrote that template, rather than living it, created a specific resonance that the creators didn't fully engineer. They built an empire for Lelouch to fight. The audience felt something real about what that empire was doing.

[Taniguchi wanted to appeal to everyone](https://fullfrontal.moe/goro-taniguchi-interview/) — to make a show that wasn't just for hardcore mecha fans, that would reach beyond the usual otaku demographics. He brought in [CLAMP to design characters](https://codegeass.fandom.com/wiki/CLAMP) that would draw female audiences as well as male ones, chose a protagonist whose power was psychological rather than physical, built a world whose real drama was political intrigue rather than hardware. He succeeded spectacularly. But the reason the colonial premise hits as hard as it does is not reducible to craft. It's that the show found a historical wound that Japan carries without fully acknowledging — and transformed it into the most satisfying kind of fiction: a story where the oppressed fight back and win.

The wound is borrowed. That doesn't make the art less real. It does make it worth examining.
