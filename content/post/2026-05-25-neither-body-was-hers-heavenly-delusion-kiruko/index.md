Every time Kiruko gets a moment of quiet, the camera in Masakazu Ishiguro's *Tengoku Daimakyou* (*Heavenly Delusion*) holds her face just a beat too long. She is looking at something the frame doesn't show. It is a very deliberate technique — Ishiguro trained himself on Katsuhiro Otomo's obsessive attention to character mannerism — and what it communicates is that Kiruko is always, underneath the competence and the care she shows Maru, doing a kind of interior math that the reader never gets full access to.[^1]

The math isn't hard to guess at on the surface. Inside the body of an older sister named Kiriko is the brain of her younger brother, Haruki Takehaya. The resulting person goes by Kiruko. She presents feminine, fights like someone who was trained in a body that is now gone, and navigates a post-apocalyptic Japan where nobody much cares about her ontological situation because everyone is too busy not getting eaten by Hiruko, the kaiju-adjacent horrors that have made the outside world a lethal mess. And trailing behind her is a boy named Maru who is developing feelings for her, complicated by the fact that he doesn't know what she is — biologically, existentially, historically.

When Kiruko fans online encounter this setup, they usually reach for the same word: allegory. Specifically, trans allegory. The wrong body, the authentic self persisting despite the physical container, the romance that complicates when the truth comes out. All of it maps. The reading isn't wrong. It's just insufficient — and the author himself has told us why.

---

![Kiruko and Maru in the forsaken world outside — the series was praised for its dystopian atmosphere and the relationship between its leads. &copy;石黒正数・講談社／天国大魔境製作委員会](https://cdn.animenewsnetwork.com/thumbnails/max600x600/cms/newtype/202577/te_01.jpg)

In September 2023, *Anime News Network* published an interview with Ishiguro where he was asked directly about gender expression and Kiruko. His answer is worth quoting carefully: "Kiruko represents not a binary of 'male' or 'female' but rather a gradation of spiritual sexuality."[^2]

A gradation of *spiritual* sexuality. Not biological. Not even psychological in the clinical sense. Spiritual. That word choice is doing real work.

In a separate interview with Kodansha, Ishiguro elaborated further. He said he "wanted to tell the story of a girl's body that contains the brain of her younger brother." He specifically and forcefully rejected the comedic alternative: he "opposed the idea of a man turning into a woman, leading to jokes about cleavage and lacking a penis." Instead, he chose a brain transplant — hard science fiction, not magic. He wanted "to create a more-realistic world to show what might happen after a brain transplant."[^3]

Most tellingly: Kiruko is, per Ishiguro's own framing, "a new person born out of the combination of two bodies and yet a separate existence from them."[^4]

Not Haruki-in-Kiriko's-body. Not Kiriko-possessed-by-Haruki. A new synthesis. A third entity.

That is not what a trans allegory looks like. A trans allegory says: the authentic self was always there, the body was wrong, resolution means alignment. What Ishiguro is describing is ontologically different: there was Kiriko, there was Haruki, and then a procedure created someone who is neither but composed of both. The question is not "which self is real?" The question is "is the result of this synthesis a *continuous* self at all?"

Note also the specific inversion at play: the person with male origins inhabits a female body and presents female. This is not the configuration most trans allegory discussions anticipate. It doesn't map to a standard framework in any direction. The series seems almost engineered to refuse those maps.

---

This is a posthuman question. Not a trans question.

The Critical Posthumanities volume edited by Sengupta and Boruah names it directly: Maru and Kiruko are "posthuman characters" in the technical sense — beings whose relationship to the biological substrate of identity has been disrupted or exceeded.[^5] Maru recovers from wounds at speeds that violate normal human biology. He can destroy Hiruko with a touch. His jacket reads "We are neither machines nor game pieces" in one of the series' more pointed visual cues. Kiruko inhabits a body that menstruates — that detail is handled with remarkable frankness for a seinen manga — but the menstruation isn't natural. It is triggered by contact with Hiruko venom filtered through Maru's lips in episode 8. Her body's biology is both her own and not her own, simultaneously inherited and alienated.

The Takahara Academy, the walled compound where the "heaven" storyline unfolds, is the philosophical counterpart. Director Hirotaka Mori described it precisely: though the outside world is officially called Hell and the academy is called Heaven, "heaven was actually closer to hell between the two."[^6] The academy is a thought experiment in a world where "sexual elements are abolished," where children are raised by robots with no parents, where Tokio — a girl who can gestate Hiruko — is studied and managed as a biological resource. The academy's horror is institutional. It is the horror of a system that has decided what its children *are* rather than letting them discover it.

Both storylines, inside and outside, are doing the same thing from different directions: stripping away the social and biological scaffolding that people usually use to answer the question "what am I?" and asking what's left.

Maru's romantic feelings for Kiruko are where this question becomes most acute, and where the series reveals its real claim.

Most manga that introduce a "wrong body" scenario treat the romance as a problem to be resolved: once the protagonist finds out the truth, they either reject the love interest (usually comedically, often cruelly) or demonstrate growth by accepting them anyway. The arc is about the protagonist's journey toward tolerance. The series uses its love interest as a test of the protagonist's virtue.

*Heavenly Delusion* does something stranger and harder. Ishiguro described Maru's feelings developing "without knowing his bodyguard is a man inhabiting a girl's body," then called the resulting romantic situation "transsexual sci-fi," and — crucially — described the "potential for Maru's feelings to persist even after learning the truth."[^7]

Persist. Not survive as an act of heroic acceptance. Persist as in: the feelings don't change because the identity question doesn't change them. Maru falls for whatever Kiruko *is*, and whatever Kiruko is doesn't resolve neatly into either of the categories the series has scrambled.

This is not a tolerance arc. It is a philosophical one. The series is asking whether love can attach itself to an identity that isn't reducible to the biological facts about the body it inhabits — and then it's asking whether *identity itself* can survive that kind of substrate disruption.

The answer Ishiguro appears to be building toward is: yes, but it becomes a new thing. The love that persists is not the same love that began. Kiruko is not the same person who went into the surgery. The synthesis has changed the terms.

---

The mythological grounding matters here. When asked about the "corrupted" familial relationships in the series — Kiriko and Haruki's sibling relationship collapsed into a new shared body, the children of the academy raised by robots with no parents at all, the Hiruko that a woman believes is her dead son — Ishiguro said it directly: "If I put it bluntly, that was because the story was inspired by mythology. Kinship in mythology is in disarray. Kojiki (Japanese mythology), which Heavenly Delusion is based on, also has such elements."[^8]

The *Kojiki*, Japan's oldest chronicle, opens with a cosmogony in which the categories of creation haven't settled yet. The boundary between kami and human, between the living and the dead, between male and female, is porous and contested. Izanami gives birth to fire and dies of it. Izanagi goes to retrieve her from the land of the dead and leaves having ritually purified himself back into a separate existence. The primordial incest, the divided siblings, the birth of gods from pollution — none of it maps to stable modern kinship categories. Ishiguro is reaching back to a Japanese framework that predates the stable self precisely because he wants to write a story in which the stable self is the fiction.

Kiruko is not a symbol of trans identity. She is a character built on the oldest Japanese mythological logic — that the self is a site, not a substance; a process, not a fixed point.

---

None of this means the trans reading is wrong. Kiruko's experience of inhabiting a body that doesn't match her history resonates with real-world gender experience in ways that are genuine and not accidental. Ishiguro's refusal of the "cleavage jokes" version of the premise is a refusal to treat the body mismatch as comedy, which is what decades of anime body-swap plots have done. That refusal is meaningful. Trans readers finding themselves in this story are finding something real.

But the series is better and more precise than "trans allegory" captures. An allegory maps one thing onto another. Kiruko doesn't map. She is a thought experiment about the conditions of identity — a question about whether personhood is something you inherit biologically, construct socially, or synthesize from the materials of loss and survival.

Maru and Kiruko are searching for Heaven. The irony is that Ishiguro's argument seems to be that the concept of Heaven — a stable, permanent, comfortable selfhood — is the delusion the title is gesturing at. Both of them are new people made from damage. The journey doesn't end when they find the destination. It ends when they stop needing the destination to confirm who they are.

That question — "what is 'human'? what is 'human-like'?"[^9] — is the one Ishiguro says he embedded in every chapter. Kiruko is not the answer. She's the shape of the question.
