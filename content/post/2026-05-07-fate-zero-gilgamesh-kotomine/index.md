There is a scene in Fate/Zero Season 2 that is easy to misread. Kotomine Kirei has just saved Kariya Matou — a man who despises him, who has every reason to want him dead — and he cannot explain why. He catalogues the moment afterward with genuine confusion: he felt something when Kariya suffered. Not guilt. Not compassion. Something warmer than either. It takes Gilgamesh to name it.

"You felt joy," the King of Heroes tells him, without ceremony. "What is wrong with that?"

That exchange is the axis around which all of Fate/Zero Season 2 rotates. Not the Grail's corruption. Not Kiritsugu's breakdown. Not Saber's loss of faith. Those are the consequences. The *cause* is this: [Kotomine Kirei](https://typemoon.fandom.com/wiki/Kirei_Kotomine) spends the whole of the Fourth Holy Grail War looking for someone who will tell him the truth about himself, and the only one who obliges is the most cynical entity in the entire franchise.

![Kirei begins the war an ascetic priest; he ends it a man who finally knows what he wants — and that coherence is the show's most disturbing achievement](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/2/21/Fate-Zero-Ep25-Gilgamesh.png/640px-Fate-Zero-Ep25-Gilgamesh.png)

## The Problem of Kirei

To understand why Gilgamesh matters so much, you have to understand what Kirei actually is at the start of Season 2 — and more precisely, what he is not.

He is not a man who has chosen evil. He is a man who cannot locate any stable moral center at all. As the [Type-Moon wiki characterizes him](https://typemoon.fandom.com/wiki/Kirei_Kotomine), Kirei "cannot feel joy from the same things as others." He has tried piety. He has tried duty. He has tried love — or something approximating it. He served the Church. He had a wife. He has a daughter. None of it resonates. He has been performing goodness for his entire adult life, and the performance has produced nothing: no warmth, no satisfaction, no sense of meaning.

What he does feel, obscurely and shamefully, is something like pleasure when others suffer. Not ordinary cruelty — he does not go out of his way to inflict pain. But when it happens in front of him, something clicks. The affect that should come from prayer or duty or family arrives instead at the sight of agony. He finds this monstrous. He has spent years trying to root it out through more rigorous piety. The Church's framework, his father's teaching, his own professional training — all of it says his instinct is corrupt, that he is corrupt.

The tragedy Urobuchi constructs is not that Kirei is evil. It's that Kirei has been told he is evil by everyone in his life, has internalized that judgment, and has therefore spent decades in a prison built from self-denial. As [one analysis observes](https://edgyanimeteenblog.wordpress.com/2018/11/07/the-contradiction-of-kotomine-kirei-fate-zero/), Kirei is fundamentally a man in contradiction: he tries to do what is right while being constitutionally oriented toward what is, by any standard moral framework, wrong.

[Gen Urobuchi said in an interview with ANN](https://www.animenewsnetwork.com/interview/2011-10-21/interview-fate-zero-screenwriter-gen-urobuchi) that he wrote Kirei as a character who "has sincerely struggled with conflicts" and whose resolution would inform his role in Fate/stay night. That word — sincerely — matters. Kirei is not performing his crisis. He is genuinely lost. And in the structure of Fate/Zero, that lostness is what makes him susceptible to the one being who has no interest in lying to him.

## Gilgamesh Does Not Corrupt Kirei. He Diagnoses Him.

This is the crucial distinction the show handles with precision.

Every other significant relationship in Kirei's life has been structured around management. His father tried to guide him toward grace. Tokiomi Tohsaka used him as a tool. The Church gave him rules to follow. Even Kiritsugu, who is Kirei's mirror in the show's formal symmetry, engages with him primarily as an enemy — a man whose internal logic he cannot fully grasp. Nobody looks at Kirei and says: *here is what you actually are*.

[Gilgamesh](https://typemoon.fandom.com/wiki/Gilgamesh), who was alive before recorded history, who has watched human civilization construct every moral system it has ever devised and found them all laughable, has zero investment in Kirei being something he is not. He is not trying to save Kirei, exploit him, or rehabilitate him. He is, in the most disinterested way imaginable, curious about him.

The King of Heroes finds humans tedious precisely because they are predictable. Their desires — love, glory, belonging, survival — bore him. Kirei is anomalous. He desires something that cannot be easily named, something that his own culture calls sin. Gilgamesh recognizes in Kirei a genuinely unusual human: a man whose authentic self is at war with his performative self, and who has fought that war so long he can no longer identify which is which.

What Gilgamesh offers is not permission to be evil. It is permission to be honest. "You were born twisted," he tells Kirei, in effect. "That is simply what you are. The suffering you feel is not punishment for sin — it is the cost of pretending otherwise."

This is, by any conventional moral reckoning, monstrous counsel. But the show does not let you dismiss it as simple villainy. Because Gilgamesh is *right*. Not about the value of sadism — but about the diagnosis. Kirei is not going to find peace in the Church. He is not going to become the man his father wanted. The self-denial is producing nothing except a more dangerous version of the thing being denied.

## The Mirroring Structure

Fate/Zero is built on doubles. Urobuchi has described his intent to construct characters around "conflicting ideologies" that emphasize their differences by pairing them against someone whose worldview is nearly inverse — and [critical analyses of the show](https://geekorner.wordpress.com/2016/02/22/fatezero-flawed-characters-urobuchis-style-and-series-composition/) consistently note this structural symmetry as one of its central design principles. Kiritsugu and Kirei are the most-discussed pair: two men who have abandoned conventional morality in pursuit of something they believe is larger than themselves, who arrive at opposite conclusions through opposite means.

But the show's *second* doubling structure is Gilgamesh and Kirei, and it is less often discussed precisely because it is more uncomfortable. What Gilgamesh represents is not nihilism in the abstract — it is nihilism successfully inhabited. He has resolved the question that is destroying Kirei. He decided long ago that the world's moral frameworks are projections, that authenticity matters more than goodness, that the only honest relationship with existence is one based on what you actually want rather than what you are supposed to want.

He finds this liberating. He is, in the deep sense, at peace. Not kind, not good, not redeemable — but at peace.

Kirei looks at Gilgamesh and sees the completion of an argument he has been making to himself for thirty years without having the courage to finish it. What would he be if he stopped fighting his own nature? Gilgamesh shows him. And [the show](https://gamerant.com/fate-zero-ending-explained/) is careful to make this vision genuinely seductive rather than easily dismissible. The King of Heroes carries himself with a coherence and certainty that none of the "good" characters in Fate/Zero possess. Iskander dies without knowing if his dream was real. Saber reaches the end having lost everything she wanted to protect. Kiritsugu commits genocide for nothing. Against all of that, Gilgamesh's position — desire nothing you cannot take, regret nothing, depend on no one — looks not like horror but like *wisdom*.

![Gilgamesh's nihilism is not a pose — it is a resolved position, and that resolution is what makes him Kirei's most legible mirror](https://cdn.myanimelist.net/images/anime/1887/117644l.jpg)

## Revelation by Accumulation

What Gilgamesh does for Kotomine in Season 2 is not a single moment of revelation but a slow accumulation. He does not tell Kirei what to do. He asks him questions. He observes. He reflects back what he sees without the reflex of condemnation.

The Kariya incident is the hinge. Kirei saves a dying man who hates him, watches him crawl off toward vengeance and further suffering, and feels something he cannot categorize. [As the Star Crossed Anime review of Season 2 notes](https://starcrossedanime.com/fatezero-season-2-review-86100/), it is one of the season's most disquieting character beats precisely because Kirei has no framework to process it — the Church never gave him language for this. Gilgamesh does not judge this reaction. He names it, the way a good therapist names a feeling — except the feeling in question is pleasure derived from watching someone suffer, and the therapist is a five-thousand-year-old divine tyrant.

The effect is the same. Naming a feeling validates it. Validation, however perverse the source, is the thing Kirei has been denied his entire life. He has been told his feelings are wrong so long that the telling has become louder than the feelings themselves. Gilgamesh simply removes that noise.

[Community analyses of Season 2](https://theanimehubcom.wordpress.com/2016/08/21/fatezero-analysis-part-1-psychopathy-utilitarianism-and-kirei-kotomine/comment-page-1/) often focus on Kirei's "villain reveal" as if it were a transformation — as if Gilgamesh made him something he was not. But the show is precise about this. Kirei does not change. He stops pretending. The distinction is everything. A man who becomes evil is potentially redeemable: there was a prior self to return to. A man who discovers his authentic self is evil has nowhere to go back to. The self he was performing was the fiction.

## What This Costs the Story

The Gilgamesh-Kotomine relationship is one of the most sophisticated pieces of character work in Fate/Zero because it refuses to be comfortable about itself.

The easy version of this story would make Gilgamesh a tempter, Kirei a fallen man, and the whole arc a cautionary tale about demonic influence. Instead, the show treats Gilgamesh's counsel as *accurate*. He is not deceiving Kirei. He is not manipulating him. He is telling him the truth. And the result — a Kotomine who is finally whole, finally coherent, finally capable of acting with real conviction — is unambiguously more dangerous than the conflicted, self-denying Kirei who began the series.

[Urobuchi's design](https://geekorner.wordpress.com/2016/02/22/fatezero-flawed-characters-urobuchis-style-and-series-composition/) is to give us a villain whose origin story is, at its core, a story about self-acceptance. This is deeply transgressive narrative territory. We are trained, by nearly every story we consume, to believe that self-knowledge leads to growth, that honesty about one's nature leads to integration, that the resolution of internal conflict is the marker of positive character development. Kirei gets all of that. His arc is complete. He is, by the internal logic of the character journey, transformed.

The transformation is into a sadist who will go on to shape the events of Fate/stay night. The resolution is into someone who finds genuine joy in precisely the suffering of others that his entire life had told him he should suppress.

There is a counter-reading worth addressing directly: that Gilgamesh is not honest but strategic, that he cultivates Kirei's sadism because a chaos-aligned Kotomine is more entertaining and more useful than a conflicted priest. [Fan discussions of the show](https://forums.spacebattles.com/threads/why-do-people-praise-fate-zero-the-anime-so-much.927009/) return to this reading periodically, and it is not wrong — Gilgamesh is not selfless. But it does not undermine the thesis. Whether his interest in Kirei is curiosity or calculation, the counsel he gives is accurate. The truth remains true regardless of the speaker's motive. What makes Gilgamesh's role genuinely disturbing is precisely that even if he is playing a game, the game still works — because the only move available to Kirei was the one Gilgamesh recommended.

Kirei is one of anime's most compelling antagonists, and the reason is finally this: he is a villain built on the logic of self-help. He did the work. He confronted his shadow. He found his authentic self. The authentic self is a catastrophe.

## The Season's Real Argument

Fate/Zero Season 2 is a tragedy about the corruption of every idealism on offer — Kiritsugu's utilitarianism, Iskander's heroism, Saber's chivalry. But underneath all of that is something more unsettling: the story of a man who solves his psychological problem and becomes humanity's problem as a result.

Gilgamesh is, in the strange arithmetic of the show, Kirei's greatest benefactor. He is the only character in the series who engages with Kirei honestly — not as a tool, not as a project, not as a threat to be neutralized. He sees Kirei clearly, names what he sees, and then steps back and lets Kirei decide what to do with the information.

The horror is that this is exactly what genuine understanding looks like. Not the generous kind that leads to healing. The accurate kind that leads to self-possession. Gilgamesh gives Kirei himself. It turns out Kirei was the worst possible person to receive that gift.

As the [final episodes of Season 2 unfold](https://www.animenewsnetwork.com/review/fate-zero/episodes-24-25/.162002), Kirei moves through the carnage with a coherence he never had before — he knows what he wants, he knows why he wants it, and he has stopped apologizing for either. [The ANN episodic review](https://anime.astronerdboy.com/2012/06/fatezero-25-finale.html) captured this shift as the show's most unsettling conclusion: not the Grail's fire, not Kiritsugu's collapse, but Kotomine walking away whole. The King of Heroes created the most effective villain in the Fate franchise not through corruption or conspiracy but through something far more dangerous: a single conversation conducted in complete honesty.

That is Fate/Zero's most quietly devastating move. In a series full of characters who destroy themselves by chasing the wrong answers, Kirei Kotomine finds exactly the right answer for exactly the wrong man. And the person who gave it to him did so out of pure, disinterested recognition — the closest thing Gilgamesh has ever come to a kind act.

The kindness ended the world.
