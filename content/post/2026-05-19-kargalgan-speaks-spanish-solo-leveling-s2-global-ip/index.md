In February 2025, Crunchyroll announced that Grammy-nominated Colombian reggaeton star J Balvin would be voicing a character named Kargalgan in the English and Spanish dubs of *Solo Leveling Season 2 -Arise from the Shadow-*.[^1] The announcement landed with the gravity of a marketing press release, which is exactly what it was. Kargalgan is not a significant character. He is a mid-tier antagonist, one of the Monarchs, who appears for a handful of episodes near the season's climax. He is not the kind of role that typically generates celebrity casting news.

But Crunchyroll didn't cast J Balvin because Kargalgan is a great role. They cast him because Latin America is a great market. And that calculation — visible, deliberate, unembarrassed — is the clearest window into what *Solo Leveling Season 2* actually is: not just an anime, but the first specimen of a post-national IP franchise engineered to colonize every continent simultaneously, using anime as its delivery mechanism.

## The Paper Trail of Deliberate Globalization

Start pulling at the production decisions for *Solo Leveling Season 2* and the pattern becomes impossible to ignore.

The opening theme, "ReawakeR," is performed by LiSA — Japan's premier anime song artist, known for *Demon Slayer*'s "Gurenge" — featuring Felix from Stray Kids, the South Korean boy band with one of the largest global fanbases in K-pop.[^2] This is not an accidental collaboration. It is a Japan-Korea crossover designed to activate both markets at once, blending the anime-adjacent credibility of LiSA with the international reach of K-pop. The ending theme, "UN-APEX," is by TK from Ling tosite sigure — a purely Japanese artistic choice that gives the show its more introspective, home-market flavor.[^3] You can see the yin-yang logic: OP for the world, ED for Japan.

The dub roster continues the geography lesson. English and Latin Spanish were released simultaneously on Crunchyroll.[^4] French, Italian, and Polish dubs followed — the French version produced by Khobalt, the Italian by VSI Rome, with the Polish version broadcast on Polsat Games.[^5] Indian viewers got Hindi dubs, with Rajesh Shukla voicing Sung Jinwoo.[^6] The 2026 Crunchyroll Anime Awards list a Best Voice Artist Performance (Hindi) category nomination for Sung Jinwoo's Hindi voice actor specifically — Solo Leveling is one of the shows with enough regional dub infrastructure to appear in that category at all.[^7]

The show also ranked #1 in Amazon Prime Japan's TV Show category and placed in the top ten on Netflix in eleven countries simultaneously.[^8] These numbers, combined with the breadth of regional production investment, make one thing plain: the architects of this franchise were not making an anime and hoping it would travel. They were mapping the globe and sending insertion teams.

## What It Actually Is

Strip away the marketing apparatus and *Solo Leveling Season 2* is a remarkably confident power fantasy anime. The source material — a Korean manhwa (webtoon) by Chugong, illustrated by the late DUBU of REDICE Studio, published on Kakao Page and WEBTOON — concluded in December 2021, meaning A-1 Pictures is adapting a completed work with full knowledge of where Sung Jinwoo ends up.[^9] That structural certainty shows in how the season handles its protagonist: there is no pretense of danger for him.

This is where critics divide sharply. ANN's James Beckett called Jinwoo "the most somniferous, robotic, and two-dimensional kind of anime protagonist" — a character who "exists on some level just to validate his confidence and lack of effort."[^10] Jeremy Tauber, writing in the same preview guide for the season premiere, acknowledged the spectacle but found it "smoke and mirrors" without "emotion or drama to latch onto."[^10] Both are diagnosing a real structural limitation: *Solo Leveling* became a power fantasy so complete that its protagonist lost the capacity to be threatened.

But the season's best episode reveals the show's actual emotional engine. Episode 24, written by Noboru Kimura and directed with exceptional control, uses the Jeju Island Raid arc to accomplish something subtle. Sung Jinwoo arrives on Jeju to find Korean hunters being slaughtered by ants. He is so overpowered that he literally clears the battlefield before the opening credits. What makes the episode work is what he does next: he discovers that Cha Hae-in is dying from ant venom that his healing potions cannot cure, and he proceeds to spend multiple irreplaceable resources — including one of his three all-illness-curing elixirs — trying to save a woman he has met exactly three times.[^11]

ANN's Richard Eisenbeis reads this as the series' most sophisticated storytelling: a show-don't-tell account of love at first sight, where Jinwoo's irrational expenditure of precious resources makes visible an attraction he doesn't consciously recognize.[^11] The argument lands. Jinwoo is, for once, operating at a loss — emotionally, materially — and it humanizes him in a way no dungeon boss ever could. It is the one moment in the season where the power fantasy has stakes because someone Jinwoo cares about is beyond the reach of his power.

Hiroyuki Sawano's score remains essential to all of it. Sawano, whose compositional fingerprints are all over *Attack on Titan*, *Promare*, and *Mobile Suit Gundam Unicorn*, brings his signature orchestral maximalism to the fights and the quieter character moments alike.[^12] The 2026 Crunchyroll Anime Awards nominated *Solo Leveling Season 2* for Best Score alongside formidable competition: kensuke ushio for *DAN DA DAN Season 2* and Yuki Kajiura/Go Shiina for *Demon Slayer: Infinity Castle*.[^13] That Sawano made that list against those names is not a small thing.

## The Category Anxiety

Here is the telling detail: the first season of *Solo Leveling* won Anime of the Year at the Crunchyroll Anime Awards 2025.[^14] It beat, presumably, everything else airing that year. Season 2 is better-produced, more emotionally coherent, and achieved comparable cultural saturation. And yet it was not nominated for Anime of the Year 2026. It sits in Best Continuing Series, Best Action, Best Animation, Best Opening, Best Ending, Best Score, and Best Main Character — but not in the top category, which went to *DAN DA DAN*, *Gachiakuta*, *My Hero Academia FINAL SEASON*, *Takopi's Original Sin*, *The Apothecary Diaries*, and *The Summer Hikaru Died*.[^13]

You can read this as voters simply preferring artier, more narratively demanding work in the main category. That is fair. But there is another reading: *Solo Leveling Season 2* occupies an uncomfortable category position. It is not really a Japanese anime in the traditional creative sense — the source material is Korean, the IP infrastructure is managed by REDICE Studio and D&C Media, the global rollout strategy is driven by Crunchyroll and Kakao, and the franchise's future content (the sequel manhwa *Solo Leveling: Ragnarok*, the confirmed live-action Korean adaptation) is being produced outside Japan.[^15][^16] A-1 Pictures is the animation studio, and a superb one, but it is a service provider in a franchise ecosystem it does not control.

This is not a criticism. It is a structural observation about what *Solo Leveling* is — and what it signals about the future of the medium.

## The Korean Manhwa Pipeline

*Solo Leveling* did not arrive in a vacuum. The Korean webtoon industry — dominated by Kakao and NAVER — has spent the last decade building international readership through platforms like WEBTOON and Tapas, which operate in English as primary markets. The content was always written for a global reader. The manhwa format, scrolled vertically on a phone, was designed for how the world actually reads digital content in 2018. The animation follows the readership.

What makes *Solo Leveling Season 2* different from previous Korean-origin adaptations is the scale of the localization investment. This is not a show that got subtitled and uploaded to Crunchyroll hoping someone would watch. It received a J Balvin campaign targeted at Latin American market penetration, an Indian dub built large enough to earn awards recognition, and a simultaneous multi-language rollout that most Japanese-origin anime do not achieve. It also generated an ice musical in Seoul — featuring Triple S idol Chae-yeon Kim and the South Korean national figure skating team — that was effectively live entertainment IP cross-promotion on par with what Disney does with its theatrical releases.[^8]

The comparison to Disney's playbook is not hyperbolic. This is franchise management, not anime production. The content is an anime because it was animated in Japan by a Japanese studio. The franchise is something else: a Korean-origin global entertainment product that uses anime production values as its calling card.

Franchise manager-brain is not inherently bad for the creative output. *Attack on Titan*, which Sawano also scored, was managed with similar meticulousness. But *Attack on Titan* was fundamentally a Japanese creative project — Isayama's vision, realized by Wit and then MAPPA, with the global audience a consequence of the work's quality rather than a target built into the production architecture from day one. *Solo Leveling Season 2* inverts that relationship. The global target is the primary variable; the creative choices serve it.

---

## What Comes Next and Why It Matters

The trajectory is already drawn. *Solo Leveling: Ragnarok* — the sequel manhwa, featuring protagonist Jin-woo Sung's son Sung Suho — ran its second season on Kakao Page before artist JIN had to enlist for mandatory military service.[^17] A season 3 will follow. A live-action Korean adaptation has been confirmed by Kakao Entertainment.[^16] The solo leveling IP has sufficient cultural infrastructure to exist as a multi-format franchise for years without the anime needing to continue.

And that is precisely the point. *Solo Leveling Season 2* is not primarily a chapter in an ongoing anime narrative. It is a proof of concept for the Korean manhwa-to-global-IP pipeline: take a completed webtoon with international digital readership, contract a prestigious Japanese animation studio, deploy global marketing across every regional audience simultaneously, generate ancillary merchandise, live events, dubs in a dozen languages, and a sequel property before the dust settles. It works. Season 2 hit #1 in Japan on Amazon Prime and top ten in eleven countries on Netflix.[^8]

J Balvin voicing Kargalgan is not a curiosity or a gimmick. It is a flag planted in a territory. And if *Solo Leveling Season 2* is the template — and every commercial signal suggests it is — then the anime industry is entering an era where the creative origination, the animation production, the distribution infrastructure, and the ancillary franchise development all belong to different national industries, coordinated by global streaming capital. The word "anime" will increasingly describe a production method rather than a creative origin.

That is not catastrophic. But it is new. And *Solo Leveling Season 2*, for all its spectacular animation and Sawano bombast and Jinwoo's emotionally complicated expenditure of elixirs on a woman he barely knows, is the clearest current evidence that it is already happening.
