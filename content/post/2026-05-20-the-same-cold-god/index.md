Ryo Asuka sits on a beach at the end of the world. The sky is red. The sea is red. At his feet are the scattered pieces of Akira Fudo — Devilman, the only person Ryo has ever loved, torn apart by the demon army Ryo himself commanded. And Ryo — who is Satan, who has spent ten thousand years maneuvering toward this moment, who began the series insisting love does not exist — Ryo cries.

Now consider Lelouch vi Britannia in the finale of *Code Geass*. He has spent two seasons building himself into the world's greatest monster so that the world can be unified against him. The Zero Requiem comes together exactly as planned. Suzaku, disguised as Zero, runs a sword through Lelouch on live television. In his last moments, Lelouch holds Nunnally, who finally understands what he was trying to do. Recognition arrives. Love is acknowledged. He dies — and the world changes.

Both endings were written by the same person.

## "The Code Geass Guy"

[Anime News Network's Mike Toole](https://www.animenewsnetwork.com/review/devilman-crybaby/episodes-1-10/.126524), reviewing *Devilman Crybaby* in January 2018, tipped his hat to Ichiro Okouchi, "the *Code Geass* guy," for "telling another story about a teenager suddenly imbued with formidable powers, who tries so hard to turn away from savagery to defend the weak and reject his meaner cohort in the name of bettering mankind, but who screws it all up along the way anyhow." Toole was pointing at Akira. He was right that the connection exists — but he was looking at the wrong character.

[According to his credits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ichir%C5%8D_%C5%8Ckouchi), Okouchi served as original story creator, series composer, and screenplay writer for both seasons of *Code Geass: Lelouch of the Rebellion* (2006-2008) and as series composer and screenplay writer for *Devilman Crybaby* (2018). These are the two works he is most identified with, and they share a specific character type that Okouchi clearly finds compelling: the brilliant, cold strategist who treats all human relationships as instrumental except for exactly one — one person, one attachment, held genuinely outside the system of calculation.

The type is precise. Lelouch vi Britannia is a chess genius who [builds a resistance army, engineers his own assassination, and uses supernatural mind control](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lelouch_Lamperouge) to manipulate thousands of people — all while maintaining that his goal is a world where his crippled younger sister Nunnally can live happily. His other relationships are real but always secondary. Nunnally is the one thing he cannot instrument.

Ryo Asuka is an academic prodigy who studies demonic possession, recruits his childhood friend into a dangerous experiment, and ultimately engineers the apocalypse — all while insisting, repeatedly, that love is a weakness invented by creatures too feeble to accept reality. The insistence is the tell. As Chris Person noted in [a Kotaku conversation shortly after *Crybaby* premiered](https://kotaku.com/what-we-loved-about-devilman-crybaby-1821909262): "The entire series begins with Ryo saying that love doesn't exist and because love doesn't exist his actions are justified, only to realize that he's killed the only person he loves."

Same bones. Same architecture. Same person held apart from the machinery.

## What the Universe Does With Recognition

The question Okouchi asks of both characters is identical: when does the strategist finally acknowledge that the one person they love is what the whole apparatus was actually about — and what does the universe do with that acknowledgment?

In *Code Geass*, the universe responds. The Zero Requiem requires that Lelouch's death be understood as sacrifice, that Nunnally witness it and grasp its purpose, and that the world be better afterward. [Okouchi stated explicitly in interviews](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lelouch_Lamperouge) — including a series of anniversary tweets collected by the *Code Geass* staff — that Lelouch's sins require death as payment: "So his end can't be one that forgives him his sins. At least he is aware of his sins and pays for them with his death. This is mine and Taniguchi-director's sense of ethics in our works." The framing is moral rather than nihilistic. There is a ledger. The universe keeps score. Sacrifice buys something.

[The production notes for *Code Geass*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Code_Geass) describe the show's central conflict as originating from "two characters with different values" — a structural core that is fundamentally optimistic about causation. Things happen because characters make choices with consequences. The Knightmare battles, the Geass mechanics, the political intrigue — all of it serves a narrative logic where cleverness, however corrupt, can remake the world. [Lelouch was voted Japan's most popular male anime character](https://myanimelist.net/anime/1575/Code_Geass__Hangyaku_no_Lelouch) three years running in Animage's Grand Prix — in part because audiences understood, at some level, that his sacrifice was purchased. The world responded.

*Devilman Crybaby* works under entirely different physics.

[The show](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Devilman_Crybaby), directed by Masaaki Yuasa at [Science SARU](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Science_SARU) and premiering on Netflix in January 2018, adapts [Go Nagai's original 1972 manga](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Devilman) — a story in which the antagonist is not a human empire but God. Specifically: a God who resets creation every time Satan's rebellion (always sparked by love for a human) comes close to succeeding. [Heather Alexandra, writing for Kotaku](https://kotaku.com/what-we-loved-about-devilman-crybaby-1821909262), put it plainly: "Everything that happens is part of a big eternal cycle where Satan is punished and loses the only person he loves over and over again." Chris Person's response — "Maybe next time love will win" — is exactly the hope the show is constructed to deny.

![In Devilman Crybaby, even Satan sheds tears — and it changes nothing](https://static0.polygonimages.com/wordpress/wp-content/uploads/chorus/uploads/chorus_asset/file/10075261/Screen_Shot_2018_01_22_at_9.25.26_AM.png)

[Polygon's review of the finale](https://www.polygon.com/2018/1/22/16909692/devilman-crybaby-ending-after-credits-scene) called Satan's tears "bold and beautiful" — finding in them "something that makes such a nihilistic ending feel not so totally hopeless after all." But this reads it backwards. Ryo's tears are not evidence of hope; they are the mechanism of his punishment. God engineered this outcome. The timing is deliberate. Ryo recognizes that he loves Akira at the exact moment when recognition can change nothing. That is not an accident in the cosmology of *Crybaby* — it is the point. The universe waited for Ryo to learn what love is before taking Akira away. The cruelty is precise.

This is the cosmological difference that separates the two endings. Lelouch's sacrifice works because *Code Geass*'s universe is structured to let sacrifice work. Ryo's grief achieves nothing because *Crybaby*'s universe is designed by a malicious God specifically to ensure that love, when it arrives, arrives one moment too late.

## Why the More Free Adaptation Got the Harsher Cosmology

What makes this worth lingering on is the production context. *Code Geass* was a broadcast Sunrise production, working inside the commercial constraints of late-night network television. *Devilman Crybaby* was a [Netflix production](https://myanimelist.net/anime/35120/Devilman__Crybaby) with, as [Toole noted in the ANN review](https://www.animenewsnetwork.com/review/devilman-crybaby/episodes-1-10/.126524), significant creative freedom from its production partners — Aniplex and Dynamic Planning Inc. as the sole listed partners, with the streaming platform enabling a level of content and structural integrity that broadcast television had historically denied the *Devilman* property.

Yuasa and Okouchi had more room, in 2018, than Okouchi had had in 2006. They used that freedom to make ten episodes that end with the world burned down and love acknowledged only as its ashes are swept away.

That is a choice. [Polygon called the full series "a masterpiece"](https://www.polygon.com/2018/1/8/16862868/devilman-crybaby-season-1-review-netflix). The craft is not in question. But it matters that Okouchi, given maximum creative latitude, chose the universe where God runs the clock and the clock is set against love. Code Geass says: a sufficiently clever person can engineer a better world, and it will cost everything. Crybaby says: there is no amount of cleverness that outmaneuvers a God whose cruelty is specifically targeted at the thing you love.

These are not the same pessimism. One is cynical idealism — means corrupt, end achievable. The other is something harder: the acknowledgment that the outcome is not determined by what you are willing to pay, but by what kind of universe you happen to inhabit.

## The Question Okouchi Keeps Writing

Okouchi's [Wikipedia entry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ichir%C5%8D_%C5%8Ckouchi) describes his consistent interest in "the emotional gray areas of his characters" — protagonists who cannot be simply read, whose motives are inseparable from their methods. Lelouch and Ryo both qualify. Both are morally illegible from the outside — brilliant, cold, seemingly indifferent to the suffering their plans require. The gray area in each case is what they are protecting and whether they know it.

What Okouchi keeps returning to is the question of whether a person who has built their entire identity around not needing love can be undone by it. Both Lelouch and Ryo answer yes. The follow-up question — whether being undone by it changes anything — is where the two stories diverge completely.

Lelouch gets a universe that says: yes, love recognized in time, expressed through sacrifice, buys a better world. Ryo gets a universe that says: love recognized, but you are inside God's reset cycle, and the recognition is the punishment, not the redemption.

Twelve years of Okouchi's career separates those two answers. Whatever changed between them, it isn't the character. It is what he imagined stood above the character, watching.
