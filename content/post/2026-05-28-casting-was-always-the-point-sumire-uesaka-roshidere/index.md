In her first year of high school, [Sumire Uesaka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sumire_Uesaka) heard the State Anthem of the Soviet Union and couldn't let it go. Not as a novelty, not as irony — as genuine fascination. She enrolled in the Department of Foreign Studies at Sophia University and spent four years majoring in Russian, graduating in 2014. By November 2013, before she had even graduated, she traveled to Russia as a guest of J-Fest, the Japanese culture festival held in Moscow. She was already a working voice actress, already building a career, and she was spending her free time at a Russian cultural festival in Moscow studying a language she had no professional reason to learn.

That detail matters because seven years later, [a light novel called *Alya Sometimes Hides Her Feelings in Russian*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alya_Sometimes_Hides_Her_Feelings_in_Russian) would become one of the most commercially successful romcoms of the 2020s, and the anime adaptation would cast Uesaka as its protagonist: a half-Russian girl who hides her genuine feelings by whispering them in a language she assumes the boy next to her cannot understand.

The casting isn't a coincidence. It's the completion of an argument that had been building across her entire career. And understanding *why* Uesaka was always going to voice Alya says something precise about what *Roshidere* is actually doing — and why it works.

---

## Nonna, Anastasia, and a Decade of Preparation

Before Alya, Uesaka had already carved out a specific niche in anime: the Russian-coded character who carries cultural weight beyond decoration. In 2012, she voiced [Nonna in *Girls und Panzer*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Girls_und_Panzer) — the stoic, deadpan Soviet-themed commander of the Pravda High School team, the character who leads her schoolmates in singing "Katyusha" and who embodies the aesthetic of Soviet military culture with complete commitment. In 2015, she began voicing [Anastasia in *The Idolmaster Cinderella Girls*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Idolmaster_Cinderella_Girls) — a half-Russian idol character, born to a Russian father and Japanese mother, who sings in Japanese while her blonde hair and blue eyes mark her as culturally legible as "foreign" to the Japanese industry she's trying to break into.

Two characters. Both half-outside of Japan. Both navigating a cultural identity that isn't fully Japanese, both performing Japaneseness while carrying something from another place. Neither of these castings was random. Uesaka had already become the seiyuu you called when you needed a character who authentically inhabits the space between Russian and Japanese cultural identity — not because she was performing it, but because she had genuinely lived inside Russian culture and could bring that specificity to the work.

When *Roshidere* arrived and needed a voice for a half-Russian girl attending a Japanese school, hiding her feelings in a language she treats as a private channel, the question wasn't who should voice her. The question was whether anyone else realistically could.

---

## The Character Who Mirrors the Seiyuu

Alisa Mikhailovna Kujou — "Alya" to her classmates — is the inverse of her own premise. She's the most visible person in any room she enters: half-Russian, strikingly beautiful, effortlessly excellent at everything she applies herself to, fluent in two languages, immaculately composed in Japanese social environments. And underneath that composed surface, she uses Russian as a private channel — a place where she can say *I like you* to Masachika Kuze's face, secure in the assumption that he doesn't understand her.

The joke, of course, is that Masachika learned Russian as a child to speak to a girl from his past, and understands every word she says. The premise is built on the gap between what Alya reveals in Russian and what she conceals in Japanese.

Uesaka's biography runs that same gap in the other direction. She is a Japanese woman who became genuinely fluent in Russian culture — not conversational Russian, but the emotional interiority of it, the specific aesthetic of Soviet-era music and culture that turned a fifteen-year-old into a devoted student. When she voices Alya's Russian lines, she isn't approximating foreignness. She's speaking from a place of actual accumulated knowledge, the same bicultural fluency the character is built around, but pointed in the opposite direction. Alya is a Russian-heritage girl performing perfect Japanese cultural belonging. Uesaka is a Japanese woman who genuinely absorbed Russian cultural feeling. They're mirror images of the same premise.

That mirroring isn't incidental. It's what gives Alya's Russian lines weight beyond their function as a romantic mechanic. When Uesaka delivers a line in Russian, the character's sincerity isn't manufactured — it's underwritten by the seiyuu's own relationship to the language. The performance is real in a way that pure technical training cannot replicate.

## Twelve Episodes, Twelve Songs She Shouldn't Know This Well

The ending theme structure of *Roshidere*'s first anime season deserves its own examination, because it forms the other half of the argument.

[Each of the twelve episodes ends with a different song](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Alya_Sometimes_Hides_Her_Feelings_in_Russian_episodes) — all covers of pre-existing Japanese tracks, all performed by Uesaka in character as Alya. The list runs the length of Japanese popular music history: ["Gakuen Tengoku" from 1970](https://www.animenewsnetwork.com/news/2024-07-03/alya-sometimes-hides-her-feelings-in-russian-anime-creditless-theme-song-videos-reveal-1st-ending/.212781), "Omoide ga Ippai" from 1983, ["Love Story wa Totsuzen ni"](https://animecorner.me/roshidere-reveals-new-ending-video-for-episode-7-with-another-song-by-alya/) (Koji Tamaki's 1991 ballad, ubiquitous to the point of cultural shorthand), [Spitz's "Cherry" from 1996](https://animecorner.me/alya-covers-che-rr-y-for-roshidere-episode-8-ending/), MONGOL800's "Chiisana Koi no Uta" from 2001, ["Hare Hare Yukai" from the *Haruhi Suzumiya* anime](https://animecorner.me/roshidere-episode-4-ending-video-features-haruhi-suzumiya-song-hare-hare-yukai-covered-by-alya/) in 2006, ["World Is Mine"](https://animecorner.me/roshidere-reveals-episode-9-ending-song-with-another-song-by-alya/) from ryo/supercell's 2008 Vocaloid era, Ikimonogakari's "Kimagure Romantic." A decade-spanning playlist that constitutes, in aggregate, a map of what a Japanese person absorbs simply by growing up inside Japanese culture.

The implicit argument is that Alya knows these songs because she has fully absorbed Japanese culture at the level where pop music becomes involuntary memory. She doesn't just speak Japanese — she carries Japanese music in her body the way a native would. The Russian lines are her private channel; the Japanese song covers are proof of her public assimilation. Both are hidden truths about the character. The Russian shows who she is underneath. The Japanese songs show how completely she has made herself at home.

That Uesaka performs both sides — the Russian confessions and the Japanese pop covers — is the production's most quietly correct decision. She is the only seiyuu working today who has actually occupied both positions: a performer who belongs completely to Japanese culture while having genuinely internalized Russian cultural feeling. The premise required a voice that had done the actual cultural work Alya's character implies. Uesaka had been doing that work since she was fourteen.

## The isekai That Didn't Happen, and Why the Grounding Mattered

The light novel's origin adds context that sharpens the casting's significance. Author SunSunSun originally conceived the premise as an isekai. The central mechanic — a character using a foreign language as a private emotional channel, unaware that her audience understands — was the core, but it was initially wrapped in genre scaffolding. That scaffolding was discarded.

The decision to set the story in a real Japanese high school, with a real half-Russian transfer student, was the author choosing to let the premise stand on its own weight rather than use genre conventions as load-bearing structure. It also made the casting stakes different. An isekai allows a certain abstraction — the Russian heritage can be exotic decoration without requiring authentic grounding. A real-world setting demands that the cultural specificity be earned. Alya's Russian has to feel like something real, not like a signifier.

Uesaka earns it. That's what a decade of genuine Russian cultural fascination produces in a performance: the difference between an accent and an orientation. [The light novel series sold five million copies before the anime even finished its first season](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alya_Sometimes_Hides_Her_Feelings_in_Russian) — the first romantic comedy in Kadokawa Sneaker Bunko's history to reach that milestone. Doga Kobo's adaptation received the Slice of Life Anime of the Year at the 2024 Anime Trending Awards, with Yuki Suou named Supporting Girl of the Year. These are franchise numbers, and the anime's reception was a significant part of how the franchise built that momentum.

The critical conversation around the anime focused heavily on the voice performance — on the quality of the Russian delivery, on the specificity of what Uesaka brought to a character whose entire premise depends on the audience believing in her dual linguistic interiority. That response wasn't accidental.

## What Happens When Biography Arrives at Character

There is a particular thing that sometimes happens in casting — rarer than it gets credited — where the right person doesn't just perform a role but has already been living adjacent to it for years before the role existed. The character arrives, and the biography answers it.

Uesaka's path to Alya runs from a high school classroom where a teenager hears the Soviet anthem and can't explain why it moves her, through four years of Russian study at Sophia University, through a trip to Moscow as a festival guest in 2013, through Nonna in 2012 and Anastasia from 2015, through a decade of anime's designated Russian-adjacent seiyuu — and arrives at a character who hides her love in Russian because it's the only language in which she can be fully honest.

[The second season of *Roshidere* has been delayed from its originally announced 2026 window to 2027](https://www.animenewsnetwork.com/news/2026-02-12/alya-sometimes-hides-her-feelings-in-russian-2nd-season-anime-delayed-to-2027/.234081), [confirmed on the day the first season finale aired](https://www.animenewsnetwork.com/news/2024-09-18/alya-sometimes-hides-her-feelings-in-russian-anime-gets-2nd-season/.215710), the production team citing a desire to further improve quality. It will arrive with the same cast. When Uesaka voices Alya's next Russian confession, it will be with the same biography — the same Soviet anthem moment, the same Sophia University Russian department, the same decades of accumulated cultural fluency.

That's not production trivia. That's what the role requires. And the casting team knew, before the anime began, that they had already found the only person who was genuinely prepared to deliver it.
