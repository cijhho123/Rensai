The original Steins;Gate contains one of the great sustained acts of love in anime: Okabe Rintaro dying, over and over, across hundreds of time loops, to save Mayuri Shiina from a death the universe keeps insisting on. He loses his mind doing it. He watches her die in every way possible — a train, a car, a fall, a gun — and keeps leaping back because the alternative is accepting it. That obsession is what defines the first series' thesis: a love so absolute it refuses to take the hint.

Steins;Gate 0 inverts this, and almost nobody talks about it.

In S;G 0, [Mayuri saves Okabe](https://hero.fandom.com/wiki/Mayuri_Shiina). Not in the supporting-character sense of "being there for him." She is the actual mechanical architect of the Steins;Gate worldline — the person who devises the plan, travels through time to execute it, and delivers the message that pulls a destroyed man back into action. The show is officially Okabe's story, about his depression and recovery. But Okabe's recovery is evidence of Mayuri's success. Without her, the true ending doesn't exist.

That inversion is the show's most important design choice, and it's been systematically underread by a fandom that still thinks of Mayuri as the cute girl Okabe keeps dying to protect.

## What the Original Established

The original Steins;Gate made Mayuri a particular kind of character deliberately. She is, on the surface, [everything the "childhood friend" archetype promises](https://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/Characters/SteinsGate): gentle, slightly vacant, making elaborate cosplays, calling Okabe "Okarin," radiating sweetness with no apparent edge. The show builds its emotional stakes around her death precisely because she reads as so innocent, so replaceable-by-plot, so structurally marked for sacrifice.

But the original also seeds something else. Mayuri observes people with extraordinary precision. She notices things about emotional states that Okabe's manic genius misses. She figures out, before almost anyone else, that something is catastrophically wrong with Okabe during his time-looping period — not because she can theorize it, but because she's been paying attention to him for years. She is not smart in the way Kurisu is smart. She is attuned in a way that none of the geniuses in the Lab ever become.

S;G 0 takes that seed and grows it into an entire arc.

## The Worthlessness Complex

The Okabe of S;G 0 is not the eccentric self-styled mad scientist of the original. [He is broken](https://animeposts.org/posts/okabe-rintarou-s-psychological-breakdown-in-steins-gate-0/). He has stopped using the Hououin Kyouma persona. He has enrolled in university and is performing normalcy with the mechanical compliance of someone who no longer believes in anything but doesn't know how to stop moving. Kurisu is dead and he chose that outcome, and the weight of it has turned him into someone entirely different.

Mayuri watches this and experiences a crisis of her own, and [one review accurately describes her arc as "the show's most powerful moments"](https://www.myshinytoyrobots.com/2019/04/steinsgate-0-review.html) — though it is also the least analyzed. She discovers that she is alive because Kurisu died. That the original Okabe, across hundreds of loops, concluded that Mayuri's life was worth Kurisu's. She doesn't experience this as gratitude. She experiences it as evidence that she is worth less — that a lesser person won the comparison and the better one paid the price. The worthlessness isn't irrational. It has specific logical content: Kurisu was brilliant, contributed to science, changed the world. Mayuri makes costumes and says "tutturu" and loves her grandmother's grave. The universe chose wrong.

This mirrors Maho Hiyajo's arc in a fascinating way. [Maho spent her career in Kurisu's shadow](https://anifiction.wordpress.com/2017/03/04/character-appreciation-maho-hiyajo/), the second genius to a first genius, constantly measuring her worth against someone she couldn't match. In S;G 0, both women who loved Kurisu — one as colleague, one as the person whose survival cost Kurisu her life — are engaged in the same work: trying to understand whether they have earned the right to keep going. S;G 0 is, among other things, a story about women who feel insufficient in the presence of a dead woman's legend.

What [the show argues back at both of them](https://www.kirikiribasara.com/2025/12/10/steinsgate-0-ten-years-later/) is the same argument, delivered through different mechanics: the thing they can do that Kurisu couldn't is the thing that actually matters here.

## What She Actually Does

The specifics of Mayuri's role in the true ending deserve more attention than they typically receive. When she learns what Okabe sacrificed and why, she doesn't accept it as the settled price of survival. She devises a plan. With Suzuha, she uses the time machine — the tool built for Okabe's genius-level timeline navigation — not for scientific intervention but for emotional intervention. [Operation Arc-Light](https://steins-gate.fandom.com/wiki/Operation_Arc-Light) is, mechanically, a time travel mission whose entire purpose is to deliver a slap and a message.

The slap is not a joke. It is the specific physical act required to snap Okabe's self-pity into something operational. And the message that accompanies it — Mayuri speaking from the future, from a Mayuri who has already watched Okabe fall apart and understood that he needs permission more than instruction — is the actual trigger for the Steins;Gate worldline. The famous D-Mail video that the original Okabe sends to his 2010 self exists because Mayuri told this Okabe that it was possible. His heroism downstream depends entirely on her intervention upstream.

[The original series has Okabe sacrifice everything for Mayuri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steins;Gate). S;G 0 has Mayuri travel through time to save Okabe not by giving him information he lacks but by giving him something to live for. These are not equivalent acts, and S;G 0 is fully aware they're not equivalent. The show is arguing that the second kind of saving is harder, in some ways, because it requires understanding another person at the level Mayuri understands Okabe — not as a genius problem to be solved but as a human being whose despair has a specific shape she has been studying her entire life.

## The Confession That Doesn't Ask for Anything

There is a scene near the end of S;G 0 where Mayuri confesses her love for Okabe. She does it directly. She says she loves him more than Kurisu loves him, as a factual claim, and then she immediately tells him she wants him to be happy with Kurisu.

This is an extraordinary piece of writing. Most love confessions in anime are strategic — they're declarations that want a response, that ask the other person to decide. Mayuri's confession asks nothing. It's not a move in a game. She says it because she wants him to know, and she releases him from any obligation to respond appropriately, because the only thing she actually wants is for him to be saved.

[The contrast with how the original framed her](https://hero.fandom.com/wiki/Mayuri_Shiina) — as the person being saved, the innocent who exists to be protected — is deliberate and precise. The original Okabe loved Mayuri so completely that he died hundreds of times to protect her from the consequence of plot logic. The S;G 0 Mayuri loves Okabe so completely that she exposes her own heart without asking for anything in return. These are equally absolute loves. The show refuses to rank them. What it does is show that the second kind — the love that saves by releasing rather than by gripping — is the love that works here. Okabe's love for Kurisu requires resolution. Mayuri's love for Okabe requires nothing from him at all.

## The Reappraisal

[The critical consensus on Steins;Gate 0](https://www.cbr.com/steins-gate-or-steins-gate-0/) — shared by [most dedicated reviews](https://thekonnetwork.com/steins-gate-0-review/) — tends to frame the show as "Okabe's story of recovery from loss" — and that's not wrong, exactly, it's just a description of the surface structure. Underneath, the show is organized around the question of what makes someone worth saving, and worth calling a hero. Kurisu answers that with genius: she changed the world, she contributed something irreplaceable, she earned her place in the story. Okabe answers it with sacrifice: he gave everything, repeatedly, because the person he loved mattered more than his own survival. Both of these are the kinds of heroism that anime — and most storytelling — is comfortable with.

Mayuri's answer is different. She is present. She pays attention. She loves someone specific, not abstractly, and that specific love means she knows what he needs better than he does. In the end, that knowledge is what the universe needed. Not genius. Not sacrifice. Attentiveness.

[The fandom tends to underrate Mayuri](https://discover.hubpages.com/entertainment/Anime-Reviews-SteinsGate-0) because she doesn't read as a conventional hero. She doesn't have a skill set that translates to traditional heroism. But S;G 0 is quietly making the argument that her kind of intelligence — the kind that knows a person fully and acts on that knowledge without asking for credit — is the kind that changes timelines when all the genius and sacrifice in the world has run out of options.

She was always the hero. The show just took a second series to let her prove it.
