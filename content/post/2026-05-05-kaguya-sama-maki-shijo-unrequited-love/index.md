Kaguya-sama: Love is War is a manga about people who are terrified to confess their feelings. Its premise — that love is a war and the first to confess loses — is also its central psychological observation: that these characters have built elaborate cognitive architectures around the simple fear of being rejected. The entire comedy machine runs on that fear. Neither Kaguya nor Shirogane can just say the thing, so they build strategies, misread signals, engineer situations, and declare moral victories based on who made the other blush harder.

Maki Shijo has no game to play. She loves someone who doesn't love her back. No strategy fixes this.

She appears as a supporting character, [a distant Shinomiya relation](https://kaguyasama-wa-kokurasetai.fandom.com/wiki/Maki_Shijo) who comes to the student council room to complain and cry. She is in love with Tsubasa Tanuma. Tsubasa has been dating her best friend Nagisa Kashiwagi since before Maki entered the story. Her love is seven years old, and the manga's final chapters reveal that Kaguya knew about it the whole time and watched Maki choose to stay close to the couple anyway. The question Maki's presence poses to the series is the question the series usually answers through comedy: what do you do with a love that has nowhere to go?

## The Geometry of Impossible Love

The relationship between [Kashiwagi, Tsubasa, and Maki](https://kaguyasama-wa-kokurasetai.fandom.com/wiki/Nagisa_Kashiwagi/Relationships) is one of the manga's cruelest structural jokes. Kashiwagi and Tsubasa are the series' designated beta couple — [they actually date, they actually communicate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kaguya-sama:_Love_Is_War), their relationship progresses in concrete, physical, realistic ways while the main couple is still arguing about who moved one centimeter closer to the other at lunch. They come to the student council room for relationship advice while Kaguya and Shirogane are still refusing to admit they like each other. They are the answer to the question "what does a real relationship look like in this universe?" — messier, more direct, more grown-up than the elegant cerebral warfare of the student council room.

And Maki loves the person at the center of it.

She has been watching them from inside their social circle for seven years. She didn't confess before Kashiwagi got involved because she was against confessing — [the same ideological position as Kaguya](https://www.animenewsnetwork.com/encyclopedia/manga.php?id=20344), though for Maki it's less strategic philosophy than the more honest truth: she knew, somewhere, that the outcome would be this. She stayed anyway. She comes to the student council room not to scheme but to process, to have somewhere to put the feeling because the feeling won't go anywhere else.

![Maki Shijo, one of the manga's most emotionally complex supporting characters](https://images.alphacoders.com/133/1337697.jpg)

## India and the Limits of Enlightenment

At some point, [Maki goes to India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Kaguya-sama:_Love_Is_War_chapters). This is played as a joke, and it is funny — the absurdity of seeking Buddhist enlightenment specifically to stop loving someone is exactly the kind of comic escalation the manga specializes in. But the joke is also the honest thing: she has tried every rational approach to the problem (accept it, distance herself, fill the space with something else) and none of them have worked, so she is now attempting to transcend the problem altogether by achieving a state of spiritual detachment from earthly attachment.

It doesn't work. She comes back still in love.

This is the manga's clearest statement about what Maki represents: you cannot think your way out of love. You cannot strategize it away. The elaborate chess games Kaguya and Shirogane play across 280 chapters function, among other things, as a distraction from the core vulnerability — actually being in love — and the comedy derives from all the clever maneuvering around the thing neither of them will just name. Maki named the thing. She told Tsubasa, inside herself, seven years ago. It didn't help. The naming made it real, and the real thing has nowhere to go.

## The Club for Unwinnable Situations

There's a recurring image in the manga of Maki sitting with Ishigami and Shirogane, the three of them commiserating about love situations that can't be resolved through cleverness. One of the recurring comedy setups has Maki arriving at the student council room specifically to process the latest episode of watching Kashiwagi and Tsubasa be happy together — and finding Ishigami and Shirogane already there, having arrived with their own impossible situations. [Ishigami has his own history of love that cost him something](https://kaguyasama-wa-kokurasetai.fandom.com/wiki/Yu_Ishigami) — his willingness to take the fall for someone who used him, the years of social isolation that followed, the way he carries that history in his posture. Shirogane, before Kaguya's feelings become clear, knows he's competing from below — [that the gap between a commoner scholarship student and the Shinomiya heir is not closable by strategy alone](https://bookwormsandbluestockings.wordpress.com/2022/10/18/kaguya-sama-love-is-war-but-i-like-the-supporting-character-arcs-more/).

What this trio shares is an honest relationship to the part of love that isn't about winning. They commiserate not to scheme but to witness each other — here is another person who knows what it's like to carry something heavy with nowhere to put it down. The student council room, in those moments, stops being a war room and becomes something more like a refuge.

The comedy of these scenes depends on the gap between the dignity of the feeling and the absurdity of the container. [Three teenagers sitting in a school room treating unrequited love with the seriousness of a military debrief](https://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/Characters/KaguyaSamaLoveIsWarSecondYears) is inherently funny. But it's also, under the comedy, the truest thing in the series: love doesn't come with a strategy guide, and the people who know that are the ones who've been where the map ends.

## The Ending That Wasn't

[Chapters 273 and 274](https://kaguyasama-wa-kokurasetai.fandom.com/wiki/Chapter_273) are Maki's conclusion. Nagisa overhears Maki telling Miyuki and Ishigami the truth: that she loves Tsubasa, has loved him for years, wants them to keep it secret. Kaguya reveals she has known for the entire time of their acquaintance. And then Nagisa and Tsubasa break up.

The story offers Maki the thing she wanted. Tsubasa is available. Her best friend, now aware of the situation, might even step aside.

Maki tells Nagisa to reconcile with him.

![Nagisa Kashiwagi and Maki Shijo, a friendship defined by what goes unsaid between them](https://images.alphacoders.com/133/1337698.jpg)

This is the most adult moment in a series that is full of sophisticated people. The logic of the Kaguya-sama universe rewards emotional bravery — Kaguya and Shirogane get each other because they eventually find the courage to be honest. The logic says: if you want something, figure out how to get it. Maki has the chance and she doesn't take it. She chose her friendship with Nagisa over the possibility of getting what she's wanted for seven years.

[The chapter ends with Maki crying again in the student council room](https://kaguyasama-wa-kokurasetai.fandom.com/wiki/Chapter_274) — but differently. This is not the cry of someone trapped in a situation she can't resolve. This is the cry of someone who made a choice, and lives with what the choice costs.

## What the Series Admits

Every major romantic arc in *Kaguya-sama* is ultimately a story about two people who get each other. Kaguya and Shirogane get each other. Ishigami and Iino get each other. Fujiwara, who doesn't play the game, drifts through the story as its benevolent disruptor and winds up fine. Even Hayasaka, whose arc is about escaping the Shinomiya orbit, finds a version of freedom.

Maki doesn't get what she wanted. She is the exception the series preserves specifically to prove that it understands what it's usually comedically eliding: that love isn't always a game, that not all games are winnable, and that the person who loses well deserves as much attention as the people who figured out how to win.

[The narrator's running commentary](https://myanimelist.net/anime/37521/Kaguya-sama_wa_Kokurasetai__Tensai-tachi_no_Renai_Zunousen) — declaring winners and losers at the end of each chapter-vignette — never assigns Maki a round. [Akasaka has said he thinks of each chapter in terms of the emotion it delivers first](https://www.animenewsnetwork.com/encyclopedia/manga.php?id=20344), and Maki's chapters consistently deliver something the narrator has no word for. She's not in the war. She's outside it, watching, occasionally sitting with the people inside it who are also, in different ways, outside it. Her arc is the series' acknowledgment that the game has losers, and that losing with grace is its own kind of victory — the kind the narrator can't declare and the scoreboard can't record.

She ends the manga still in love with someone who will never love her back. She also ends it having chosen her friend's happiness over her own desire, which is the exact thing that the elaborate scheming of the main romance is designed to avoid ever having to do. Both choices are honest. The series respects both of them.

Kaguya and Shirogane win the war. Maki Shijo, in the only way available to her, wins something else.
