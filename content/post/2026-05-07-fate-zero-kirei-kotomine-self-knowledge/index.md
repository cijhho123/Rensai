The final episode of Fate/Zero ends with Kirei Kotomine standing over a burning city, laughing. Not the calculating smirk of a manipulator satisfied with his scheming, not the cold triumph of a tactician who played the board better than his opponent — but genuine, uncontrolled laughter. Joy. The first real joy we have seen from him across the entire series.

That laugh is the thesis of Season 2.

Every other significant character spends the second cour destroying themselves. Kiritsugu obliterates his ideals at the precise moment they are fulfilled. Rider marches into an impossible charge knowing he will die without his dream realized. Saber watches everything she staked her kingship on collapse. Kariya disintegrates under his own curse. The Grail corrupts. Urobuchi constructs an elaborate machine for producing tragic failure across the board — and then plants Kirei in the middle of it, the one figure who walks out of the wreckage not broken but illuminated.

This is not an accident. It is a deeply uncomfortable argument about what self-knowledge actually costs.

## The Architecture of Emptiness

Kirei Kotomine, as established in Season 1 and confirmed by his role in Season 2, is [a fundamentally hollow individual](https://fateuniverse.fandom.com/wiki/Kirei_Kotomine) — not hollow in the sense of depth missing, but hollow in the clinical sense. He does not feel what others feel. He has spent his life doing all the correct things: becoming a church executor, mastering combat, marrying, raising a child. He is, by every external measure, an accomplished and devoted man. And none of it produces anything in him.

What Kirei does feel — and what he has spent decades trying not to — is [schadenfreude](https://theanimehubcom.wordpress.com/2016/08/21/fatezero-analysis-part-1-psychopathy-utilitarianism-and-kirei-kotomine/comment-page-1/). The suffering of others registers in him as the only genuine pleasure he can access. He knows this. He finds it monstrous. And rather than examining why, he buries it — because the only moral framework he has, the one handed to him by his father and the Church, has no room for a person like him. [As one analysis puts it](https://edgyanimeteenblog.wordpress.com/2018/11/07/the-contradiction-of-kotomine-kirei-fate-zero/), acknowledging his nature would negate his ability to remain moral, since his morality relied entirely on blind faith in God's judgment.

So he stays hollow. A perfect executor. A devotedly empty man.

![Kotomine Kirei in Fate/Zero](https://static0.gamerantimages.com/wordpress/wp-content/uploads/2022/08/kirei-kotomine.jpg)

What makes Season 2 the real story of Kirei Kotomine is that it gives him, for the first time, someone who refuses to let him keep the lie going.

## Gilgamesh as the Only Honest Mirror

The relationship between Gilgamesh and Kirei is one of the most structurally precise things Fate/Zero does. Everyone else in the show tells Kirei who he should be. [His father Risei](https://fateuniverse.fandom.com/wiki/Risei_Kotomine) was proud of the perfect heir he had raised — diligent, moral, combat-hardened — and failed to understand his son in any meaningful sense. Risei's devoutness and lack of genuine interest in Kirei's inner world left his son's psychological reality suppressed even from himself, his murderous tendencies buried under a lifestyle designed to prevent them from surfacing. The Church gave Kirei a framework that defined his nature as sin. His participation in the Holy Grail War was itself an attempt to find some direction — to have a goal that would finally make the emptiness productive.

Gilgamesh does something nobody else does: he interrogates Kirei's desire without judgment or agenda. The early Season 2 scene where Gilgamesh intrudes on Kirei's office, pours himself wine, and asks flatly what Kirei would wish of the Holy Grail — and then refuses to accept the answer that he has no wishes — is the axis the show turns on. [When Kirei says](https://www.animenewsnetwork.com/review/fate-zero/episodes-16-17/.161807) that pursuing his own pleasure would stain his soul, Gilgamesh's response is not an ethical argument but an empirical one: he tells Kirei he needs to learn what pleasure actually is. Not what it should be. What it is, for him, specifically.

[He recognizes](https://villains.fandom.com/wiki/Kirei_Kotomine) that he is the only figure who has ever truly known Kirei — that where Risei saw a promising heir, Gilgamesh sees the thing underneath. And that Kirei's fascination with Matou Kariya's suffering is not a curiosity to be managed but a desire to be named. When Kirei watches Kariya degrade across the War's second half, Gilgamesh's question is simple: what does that make you feel? Not "why is that wrong?" or "how do you stop it?" Just: what is this?

This is why their partnership works and why every other relationship in Kirei's life has failed. [As one analysis of his character notes](https://theanimehubcom.wordpress.com/2016/08/21/fatezero-analysis-part-1-psychopathy-utilitarianism-and-kirei-kotomine/comment-page-1/), Gilgamesh was the only guidance figure who ever really knew Kirei, and it is obvious that the King of Heroes was more in tune with Kirei's deepest desires than Risei ever was. Faced with someone who will not let him deflect, Kirei has to actually look at himself — possibly for the first time.

The show is careful about pacing this discovery. Kirei's self-discovery across Season 2 is gradual and uncomfortable, not a dramatic conversion. He resists the conclusions Gilgamesh is pushing him toward. The tension in his scenes — the way he keeps returning to Kariya, keeps probing Kiritsugu, keeps asking what kind of man he actually is — reads less like villainy forming and more like a man terrified of the answer to a question he cannot stop asking.

## The Mirror Fight

The duel between Kiritsugu and Kirei in the final episodes is formally constructed as a confrontation between two parallel men. [The show explicitly positions Kirei as Kiritsugu's "kindred spirit"](https://gamerant.com/fate-zero-kirei-vs-kiritsugu-best-fight/) — someone who, like Kiritsugu, operates through extreme pragmatism and a willingness to abandon conventional morality in service of some deeper goal. Both have sacrificed everything their nominal identities required of them. Both have done horrific things. Both are, in the classical sense, broken.

The difference — the crucial, structurally load-bearing difference — is direction.

[Kiritsugu fights against his nature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fate/Zero). His utilitarian calculus is a cage he built to contain the part of himself that would rather protect the specific people he loves than sacrifice them for the abstract greater good. He knows this tension and tries to resolve it through philosophy. He loses — not to Kirei but to the logic of his own system, which the corrupted Grail completes for him without hesitation. When Kiritsugu's wish is inverted and fulfilled as mass death, it is not a perversion of his idealism. It is the destination his idealism was always heading toward.

Kirei, by the end, is going in the opposite direction. He has been allowing the lie to surface for one episode at a time across Season 2, and by the time the two men face each other — [in a duel of almost theatrical minimalism](http://wordofsean.blogspot.com/2018/09/battle-breakdown-kiritsugu-emiya-vs.html), two hits, no dialogue, no Servants — Kirei fights as a man who knows exactly what he is. His resolution is not philosophical. It is psychological. He does not need the Grail to complete his wish because he has already granted it himself: he now knows what he wants.

![Kiritsugu Emiya vs. Kirei Kotomine](https://static0.gamerantimages.com/wordpress/wp-content/uploads/2022/08/kiritsugu-vs-kotomine-featured.jpg)

The fight is two hits because it does not need more. It is not a combat between equals — it is a demonstration of what clarity gives you that ideology cannot. Kiritsugu is fighting for something abstract. Kirei is fighting, for the first time, as himself.

## What the Laugh Means

When Kirei dies — when Kiritsugu's Origin bullet stops his heart — and then gets revived by the Grail's corrupted prana, the show gives us something it has otherwise denied: a scene of genuine self-fulfillment. The [corrupted Grail pours into Kirei](https://animehunch.com/fate-zero-ending-explaination/), the city burns around him, and he laughs. Not strategically. Not in triumph over Kiritsugu. Just: joy. The kind of undiluted, unmanaged emotional response that this character has been incapable of his entire life. [Contemporary reviews noted](https://starcrossedanime.com/fate-zero-25/) that the episode gives Kirei considerably more screen time than expected in the finale, and that it reshapes the retrospective experience of the whole series — the suggestion that he is the show's true lead rather than one among an ensemble.

This is the show's most uncomfortable moment because it refuses to make Kirei's joy monstrous in the conventional way. The burning city is monstrous. The hundreds dying are monstrous. But Kirei's laughter is — and this is the problem — *genuine*. It is the first authentic thing we have ever seen from him. And Urobuchi, to his credit, does not undercut it. He does not have Kirei recoil in horror at what he has become. The scene holds.

[The contrast with Kiritsugu in the same episode is devastating](https://www.uk-anime.net/anime/Fate%28slash%29Zero_-_Part_2.html). Kiritsugu is sobbing. Having killed the Grail's vessel with his own hands — having, by his own utilitarian logic, made the correct choice — he stands in the rubble of his idealism and weeps. He was completely sincere. He believed completely. He sacrificed everything. And it destroyed him.

Kirei was sincere about nothing for most of his life, and that suppressed sincerity was killing him slowly. When he finally becomes sincere — about what he is, about what brings him joy, about the desire he has spent decades denying — the show rewards him with the only form of victory it has left to offer: survival, and something that looks disturbingly like peace.

## The Thesis the Show Will Not Admit

The reading I have laid out is uncomfortable in a specific way: it makes Kirei the argument Fate/Zero is actually making, and nobody wants that to be true.

The conventional reading of Fate/Zero Season 2 is that it is a tragedy about idealism — a series of brilliant people with genuine convictions who all get destroyed by the gap between their ideals and reality. That reading is correct, as far as it goes. But it is incomplete because it ignores the counterweight.

Kirei's arc is not a tragedy. It is [structured like a dark bildungsroman](https://shindeiie.wordpress.com/2012/06/06/fatezero-episode-22-the-kotomine-kirei-problem/) — a character who has been psychologically stunted finally achieves the self-knowledge that unlocks his development. The fact that his development is toward monstrosity is Urobuchi's point: the machinery of self-discovery is value-neutral. It will take you where your actual desires lead. For Kiritsugu, self-knowledge leads to grief — his actual desire, he learns too late, was never world peace but the specific faces of the people he loves. For Kirei, self-knowledge leads to joy — his actual desire, learned just in time, is exactly what he always feared it was.

[As the broader series analysis makes clear](https://theanimehubcom.wordpress.com/2015/11/21/fatezero-series-review/), Kirei is not a simple villain but the story's most complete case study in the nature-versus-nurture dynamic the show is examining throughout. His father's failure to address his psychological reality did not create his sadism; it just ensured it would emerge at the worst possible scale. You cannot suppress a desire to nothing. You can only defer the bill. [The geekorner analysis of Fate/Zero's character writing](https://geekorner.wordpress.com/2016/02/22/fatezero-flawed-characters-urobuchis-style-and-series-composition/) makes the pointed observation that Kirei and Gilgamesh look as if they are having fun, and are greater than life — a quality that makes Kirei paradoxically more readable than Kiritsugu, because his desire expresses itself as spectacle while Kiritsugu's self-suppression reads as withholding.

Season 2 is the bill coming due — for everyone. Most characters pay it in ruin. Kirei pays it in revelation.

## The Continuity Problem

There is one final thing the ending forces us to confront: Kirei survives.

He survives because the corrupted Grail grants him the artificial heart that replaces the one Kiritsugu stopped. He survives into [Fate/stay night](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fate/Zero), where he becomes the supervising priest of the Fifth Holy Grail War — still operating, still dangerous, carrying the clarity he found in the ashes of the Fourth War forward into the franchise's sequel. He is revived by the very evil the show has been building toward and carries it inside him as a literal organ.

This is not just plot continuity. It is the show completing its argument. The man who stopped lying to himself is still here. The man who could not stop lying to himself is gone — dead before Fate/stay night begins, having spent his final years watching his adopted son develop while grieving an unreachable dream. Kiritsugu's ending is tragic, beautiful, and utterly final. Kirei's ending is open. Purposeful. Pointed.

[Urobuchi has said](https://www.animenewsnetwork.com/interview/2014-09-17/gen-urobuchi/.78542) that Fate/Zero restored his joy in writing — that the project let him write tragedy as craft rather than as burden. There is an irony in that. He wrote the story of a man discovering what actually makes him happy while himself rediscovering what makes him happy. The means were monstrous. The result was not.

Kirei's laugh, in other words, is not just a character moment. It is a structural signature: the sound of desire finally unmasked, of the performance dropping, of a self that was suppressed for decades finally emerging into exactly the kind of world it was built for.

That the world is on fire is not incidental. For Kirei Kotomine, it is the only appropriate setting.
