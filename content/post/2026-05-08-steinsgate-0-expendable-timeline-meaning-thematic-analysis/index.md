You already know this world ends.

Not in the disaster-movie sense — not a countdown to apocalypse that the characters might prevent. In Steins;Gate 0, the world ends in the specific sense that you, as a viewer who has seen the original series, know that its entire timeline will be overwritten. The beta worldline — where Kurisu is dead, World War III eventually kills billions, and the Future Gadget Lab operates under the shadow of a grief that never resolves — is not the worldline that produces the happy ending. That's a different branch. You are watching the one that gets erased.

[The Kiri Kiri Basara analysis of why SG0 is often misread](https://www.kirikiribasara.com/2025/09/22/why-steinsgate-0-is-often-misunderstood/) makes the structural argument precisely: this is not a side story or a prequel in the conventional sense. It is the story of the final iteration of Okabe — the specific one who records the video D-Mail that the original Steins;Gate shows at its opening. The events are causally necessary to the happy ending. They are also being erased by it. The characters in SG0 are building the message in a bottle that will reach the self who never lived through what they lived through.

The question this creates — whether meaning requires permanence, whether a life and its bonds can matter inside a timeline the genre has already condemned — is more philosophically serious than anything the original Steins;Gate asked. The original's question was whether one person can bear the cost of saving everyone. SG0's question is whether "saving everyone" actually saves the people in the room.

## What SG0 Actually Is

[According to the Science Adventure Series Wiki](https://scienceadventure.wiki.gg/wiki/Steins;Gate_0_(anime)), the anime was not a direct route adaptation from the visual novel but a new linear worldline constructed by writer Naotaka Hayashi combining elements from all six VN routes. Director Kenichi Kawamura (same studio, White Fox, with series composer Jukki Hanada) chose linear structure, constructing a single anime-exclusive worldline rather than adapting any one VN route directly. [The VN comparison by PsycheGames](https://psychegames.wordpress.com/2020/03/13/steins-gate-0-animation-vs-game-warning-confusing/) notes that SG0's anime worldline sits alongside the VN's routes rather than matching any of them — a companion rather than a translation.

This context matters because it explains some of SG0's perceived weaknesses. Kagari Shiina — a Kurisu lookalike who becomes central to the anime's geopolitical thriller plot — was criticized by [Star Crossed Anime's full series review](https://starcrossedanime.com/steinsgate-0-anime-review-46-100/) as having essentially no interior life. This is accurate for the anime, and it creates an unintentional irony: the character who physically resembles Kurisu is less present as a person than Amadeus, the AI constructed from Kurisu's memories. Kagari has the body; Amadeus has the interiority. That inversion is the Amadeus problem stated in visual terms — but the anime never develops Kagari enough to make it intentional. [The same review scores the anime 46/100](https://starcrossedanime.com/steinsgate-0-anime-review-46-100/) specifically because the adaptation stripped several characters' arcs for linearity. Maho Hiyajo lost the most.

[The Sweetsandgold review at 7/10](https://sweetsandgold.wordpress.com/2018/11/12/steinsgate-0-review-english/) frames SG0 as a "flawed masterpiece" necessary to complete the franchise experience — a framing closer to the truth than the harsher takes. The flaws are adaptation flaws. The masterpiece part is the architecture.

## Amadeus and the Ship of Theseus She Solved Wrong

Amadeus — the AI constructed from Kurisu Makise's memories up to March 2010 — exists as a Ship of Theseus problem that the show eventually refuses to answer philosophically. [As the Amadeus System wiki documents](https://steins-gate.fandom.com/wiki/Amadeus_System), both the anime and the characters within it debate explicitly whether she is Kurisu. The anime's answer, delivered through Maho and Okabe, is that they've been using emotional attachment to the deceased to project Kurisu onto an entity that is its own being. The VN routes are more ambiguous, some strongly implying a continuity of consciousness. [A 2019 ANN interest piece](https://www.animenewsnetwork.com/interest/2019-09-13/steins-gate-0-amadeus-ai-is-100-percent-real/.151072) noted that real AI researchers were already building systems comparable to Amadeus, making the question of digital personhood and grief no longer purely speculative.

What makes Amadeus essential to SG0's argument is how she resolves her own existential crisis. Her final speech before deletion: "Since the original me died, I've often asked myself: 'What exactly is my existence?'... But if it's within my power to save the original Kurisu... if that can be the reason I exist... don't you think that's more logical... and even romantic?" She does not answer whether she is Kurisu. She identifies a purpose that makes the question irrelevant. The Ship of Theseus problem dissolves when you stop asking who you are and start asking what you do with whatever you are.

[Star Crossed Anime's episode 22 review](https://starcrossedanime.com/steinsgate-0-22-rinascimento-of-projection-project-amadeus/) calls Amadeus's deletion the series' emotional apex, noting it works precisely because she has no "survival guarantee" imported from the original series. Unlike Okabe, Mayuri, and Kurisu, whose ultimate survival is known before SG0 begins, Amadeus's fate is genuinely uncertain. The scene lands the way it does because she is the character who is fully inside the doomed timeline — no version of her survives into the Steins Gate worldline. She is the most completely expendable character in the show, and she chooses what to do with that expendability. Meaning without permanence, as a deliberate act.

## The Salieri Who Didn't Turn Destructive

Maho Hiyajo is the character SG0 adds to the franchise rather than borrowing from the original, and she is substantially underrated among anime-only viewers because the adaptation compressed her resolution. [The Beneath the Tangles analysis of the Salieri parallel](https://beneaththetangles.com/2018/06/25/we-cant-all-be-makise-kurisu-or-wolfgang-amadeus-mozart/) draws this precisely: Maho built the Amadeus project — named after Mozart — as a monument to her own inadequacy. She is the neuroscientist who was a prodigy until Kurisu arrived, after which she became the excellent person standing next to the extraordinary one.

What the Salieri comparison misses, and what [the Anifiction character appreciation piece](https://anifiction.wordpress.com/2017/03/04/character-appreciation-maho-hiyajo/) notes explicitly, is that Maho never turns her inadequacy outward. She doesn't punish Kurisu. She loves her — platonically, deeply, as a person who saw her clearly and still found her worth admiring. The inferiority complex is real, but it coexists with genuine affection rather than collapsing into resentment. This is the realistic version of Salieri: not murderous envy but the quieter grief of someone who can see the distance between herself and genius and still chooses to work in its shadow.

The VN's "Twin Automata" route — [almost entirely absent from the anime per WebNovel community comparisons](https://psychegames.wordpress.com/2020/03/13/steins-gate-0-animation-vs-game-warning-confusing/) — is where this resolves. Maho chooses to delete Amadeus rather than let the system be weaponized. In doing so she receives what reads, across the text's ambiguity, as a final message from Kurisu herself. She leaves Japan determined to build the time machine with her own hands, fulfilling a promise she made with Kurisu. The ending the anime truncates is not a triumph of genius: it is the ending where Maho decides she doesn't need to be Kurisu, that her own person is sufficient to continue Kurisu's work. [The Walking Under the Sky Tumblr analysis of her ending](https://atopearth.tumblr.com/post/187686587069/steinsgate-0-part-2-maho-ending) describes this as "one of the most quietly satisfying arcs in the franchise."

Anime viewers often rank her below franchise regulars because they got the setup without the payoff. [The Science Adventure Series Wiki entry on Maho](https://scienceadventure.wiki.gg/wiki/Hiyajo_Maho) situates her as one of the SG0-original characters with the most complete arc in the source material — precisely the arc the anime compressed most severely. In the VN, Maho's route is the clearest argument SG0 makes about identity independent of comparison. The anime gave her the inferiority complex without the resolution. VN readers know what they lost.

## The Sacrifice That Doesn't Get Credited

Mayuri Shiina makes a choice in SG0 that her original-series role never allowed. [The TV Tropes tear-jerker documentation](https://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/TearJerker/SteinsGate) and [the Operation Arc-Light wiki](https://steins-gate.fandom.com/wiki/Operation_Arc-Light) capture the beat: when Okabe tries to keep Mayuri in ignorance about the timeline's stakes, she independently seeks out the truth, learns it, and decides on her own to accompany Suzuha on a one-way trip back to 2010 to persuade her past self to push Okabe toward saving Kurisu. She is not rescued. She is not kept safe. She acts — with full knowledge of what it costs her — in order to repay a debt she feels to the Kurisu who died to preserve a version of Mayuri she would never meet.

The Steins Gate worldline doesn't credit this. [The Game Rant timeline explainer](https://gamerant.com/-steins-gate-0-timeline/) traces how Okabe's success in the original worldline overwrites the SG0 timeline. The Mayuri who chose to sacrifice herself in Operation Arc-Light does not exist in the continuity that ends happily. The Mayuri who laughs and eats pudding at the end of the original never made that choice — and could not, because that choice belonged to a person whose entire formative experience was shaped by a timeline that no longer exists. This is why Mayuri's SG0 arc rarely appears in franchise "best moments" discussions: the happy ending's existence renders its context invisible. The triumph absorbs the sacrifice.

[The ANN feature on untangling Steins;Gate before SG0 aired](https://www.animenewsnetwork.com/feature/2018-04-11/untangling-steins-gate-before-steins-gate-0/.129544) prepared viewers for complexity, but most discourse still treats the beta worldline as a "failure state" to be corrected. The franchise's block-universe premise — [all worldlines coexist, none are destroyed, only the "active" branch changes](https://steins-gate.fandom.com/wiki/Timeline) — is the narrative's philosophical answer. In theory, the beta worldline persists; nothing is destroyed. In practice, every character who cared about SG0's version of Mayuri is watching the wrong branch. The consolation is real and it doesn't console. That gap is the emotional residue the show leaves behind: you can accept the block-universe answer and still feel the weight of what the active branch never witnessed.

## Does Meaning Require Permanence?

SG0 asks this question through three different lenses simultaneously: Amadeus (an AI who chooses purpose over identity), Maho (a person who finds self-worth without needing to exceed her predecessor), and Mayuri (a character who acts heroically in a timeline that will not remember her doing so). None of these are the same question, but they converge on the same answer.

Amadeus's answer: no. She makes her choice knowing it eliminates her and dissolves into a worldline that her "original" will never inhabit. The romantic logic she invokes is not about survival but about action — what you do is real even if the entity that did it is eventually overwritten.

Maho's answer (in the VN, compressed in the anime): no. She builds toward a future that may produce Kurisu's time machine, which will enable the victory that erases the timeline she's working in. Her work is causally necessary to an outcome she will not witness. She does it anyway.

Mayuri's answer: no. The choice she makes in Operation Arc-Light produces a version of history that will not contain the person who chose it.

The original Steins;Gate is about one person's capacity to bear the cost of saving everyone. Steins;Gate 0 is about what the people in the room do when they understand that saving everyone doesn't save the room. The franchise's block-universe consolation — that no worldline is destroyed, only deactivated — is the show's polite answer to a question it has already made you feel the full weight of.

The beta worldline doesn't win. But it builds the message that the winning worldline finds. The work was real. The choice was real. The meaning was real. Whether that's enough is the question the ending leaves you to answer on your own.
