The first thing Togame says to Yasuri Shichika when they meet is a lie.

Not in content — she is, functionally, a Shogunate strategist seeking help collecting twelve legendary blades — but in form. She introduces herself with a title, a mission, and an aggressive verbal confidence that fills the room before she has finished her opening sentence. She talks too much, too fast, too elaborately. She is performing, and she is performing for someone who has, for his entire life, never had to meet anyone. Shichika grew up on an isolated island with one sister and the ghost of his father's reputation. He has no reference point for eccentricity. He cannot tell she is hiding.

That is, of course, the point.

**Katanagatari** premiered in January 2010, adapted from NisiOisiN's light novel series — written and released by Kodansha Box at the extraordinary pace of one volume per month throughout all of 2007.[^1] White Fox matched that cadence with a release format almost unprecedented for TV anime: one fifty-minute episode per month, from January to December 2010, each with its own unique ending theme.[^2] This was not serialization in the weekly sense. It was something closer to monthly events — a pace that required audiences to sit with each chapter, each sword, each death for thirty days before the next one arrived.[^3]

That structure matters. Viewers who experienced Katanagatari as it aired developed, without entirely realizing it, a relationship with Togame's performance. Not with Togame — with her performance. The distinction is the whole show.

## The "Cheerio!" as Armor

Togame's recurring catchphrase — "Cheerio!" (チェリオ!) — appears in episode one and never fully leaves.[^4] In context it lands as comic relief: an inexplicable bit of verbal eccentricity from a character already defined by her motormouth strategizing and theatrical self-regard. NisiOisiN gave it to her as part of a larger ecosystem of idiosyncratic verbal tics — the insistence on being called "Togame the Schemer," the demand to have every tactical development "written down in her strategy notes," the habit of narrating her own feelings as if reporting field intelligence.[^5]

Read as characterization, these tics are sharp and funny. Read as psychology, they are the signature of someone who cannot afford silence. Every "Cheerio!" is a reset button. Togame has spent her life since watching her father Hida Takahito be killed in a failed rebellion constructing elaborate defenses against vulnerability.[^6] She has been betrayed by everyone she's hired for money or fame. She chose Shichika because she decided love was the only commitment proof against theft — and then she chose a man who literally cannot choose his own feelings, a man who treats love as a strategic variable because he has no other framework for it.[^7] The setup is so calculated it reads as parody. That is also exactly the point.

"Cheerio!" is performed invulnerability. It says: I am in control. I set the terms of this interaction. Nothing you do can hurt me because I named it before you did it. For a character who grew up knowing the shogunate might kill her for who her father was, and who spent decades working inside the very apparatus that destroyed her family, performed invulnerability is not a quirk. It is survival.

## The Wall Between Us

This creates a structural paradox that critics have noticed without quite resolving. The Anime News Network review of the first volume calls the series' dialogue "an endless stream" that can be "infuriating" at its worst and "too much fun to let go" at its best.[^8] The review of the second volume — Carl Kimlinger's more searching assessment — identifies the deeper problem: "Characters talk about events, about schemes, about people and places and feelings and relationships and trauma and the flow of history... Everything the characters think or feel is mediated by words, never experienced directly. We are separated from them by walls of words."[^9]

Kimlinger frames this as a flaw. He argues the finale "should have eviscerated us... We should have cried and cheered and torn holes in our armrests... The end of Katanagatari is good, but had it allowed us in closer, it would have been great."

He is wrong, and the error is instructive.

Katanagatari is not trying to eviscerate you. What it is doing — what it pulls off completely in episodes eleven and twelve — is placing you in the exact emotional position Shichika occupies for the entire series. Shichika grew up deliberately isolated, trained to think of himself as a weapon rather than a person, incapable of reading social situations, unable to distinguish affection from strategy because no one ever showed him the difference.[^10] He meets Togame and cannot read her. He accepts the mission because she commands it. He is kept at arm's length by the same wall of words the audience is kept at arm's length by. He is, in the most structural sense, Togame's audience — and he is treated exactly as we are treated.

The wall of words is not incidental to Katanagatari's form. It is the form expressing the content. We cannot get close to these characters because one of them has spent her entire adult life making sure no one can get close to her. "Cheerio!" is the sign above the gate.

## What Togame Was Actually Doing

Here is what the show has embedded for eleven episodes, delivered so deftly beneath the comedy that most viewers only see it whole in retrospect.

Togame sought out Shichika knowing he was the son of Mutsue Yasuri — the man who killed her father during the rebellion.[^11] Her stated goal is the sword collection. Her real goal is revenge. Her plan, never spoken aloud, was to use Shichika for the mission and then arrange for him to be killed — a clean completion of the cycle. She hired the son of her father's murderer with the intention of destroying him once he was no longer useful.

She fell in love instead.

The catastrophe of this is not dramatic in the usual sense. The show does not underline it. It does not have Togame deliver a monologue about her growing feelings or an agonized diary entry about the mission she cannot complete. It has her keep saying "Cheerio!" and demanding things be written in her strategy notes, and talking at Shichika about the next sword while, beneath all that talk, something is changing that she will not give words to because giving it words would make it real and real things can be taken away.

## Episode Eleven: When She Stops Talking

Emonzaemon shoots her in the final moments of "Gilt, the Venomous."[^12] Togame's true identity — as Hida Takahito's daughter, as someone the Shogunate has been watching — is exposed, and she is eliminated. She has served her purpose. The machine she worked inside has no further use for her.

She dies in Shichika's arms.

She does not say "Cheerio!"

Not once. Not as irony, not as farewell, not as habit. The eleven-episode verbal tic that had become as expected as punctuation simply is not there. And in its absence the entire character falls open.

What Togame says instead: that she had planned, from the beginning, to have Shichika killed once the swords were collected. That she had hired him with love precisely because love was meant to be disposable — a reliable chain that could be cut when the time came. That somewhere between the first episode and the eleventh she lost the ability to treat him as a means and did not have the courage to tell him. That she had maintained the fiction that her feelings were strategy and her strategy was feelings for so long she could not separate them anymore. And her actual final words: "Was it okay that I fell for you too?"

No "Cheerio!" No strategy notes. No verbal armor. A question asked without a catchphrase to close it, in a voice that had finally run out of performance.

Voice actress Yukari Tamura — a performer associated with high-energy, theatrical deliveries, known for roles requiring distinctive stylized affect[^13] — plays this sequence in a register unheard from her in the preceding ten episodes. The studied eccentricity is gone. What remains is someone who has run out of words and is speaking in the gap.

## The Argument About Binge-Watching

The ongoing fan dispute about Katanagatari centers on whether the ending lands. Polygon named it one of the best anime of the 2010s, highlighting White Fox's "understated, almost flattened out style" and the uniqueness of Shichika's swordless fighting.[^14] The ANN reviewers awarded it a B+ overall but argued the finale underperformed its potential. Reddit discussions split reliably between "this destroyed me" and "I could see it coming from episode one."[^15]

Both reactions are understandable. The people who were destroyed are right. The people who saw it coming are also right. What they disagree about is whether that visibility is a flaw.

It is not. Katanagatari shows you the entire shape of Togame's tragedy from episode one if you know where to look — which means the emotional wallop of episode eleven is not a surprise but a confirmation. You always knew she was going to die. The show always knew you knew. What it was building was not suspense but weight: each episode adding more evidence of the mask, so that its final removal would mean something. Twelve months of "Cheerio!" purchasing thirty seconds of silence.

## What White Fox Understood About NisiOisiN

White Fox's visual approach — extensively documented and debated since 2010 — matches this perfectly. Kimlinger's Vol. 2 review nails it: the characters are "abstractions of abstractions," design conventions stripped to their most basic components.[^16] Princess Hitei (whose name in kanji reads, appropriately, as "denial/negation") is so archetypal she functions almost as a Brechtian figure, a deliberate concept in character form.[^17] Togame is drawn as a stylized performance of a strategist — theatrical hair, theatrical costume, theatrical face.

When genuine feeling ruptures through that stylization, it ruptures through design too. Composer Taku Iwasaki's score — which blends hip-hop vocals, traditional chant, and stabs of avant-garde classicism in a calculated collision of historical and modern[^18] — does something similar: it keeps most of the series in a pleasant stylistic register and then, at the finale, shifts without warning into territory the preceding episodes had not prepared you for.

The whole apparatus — art, music, dialogue density, episode format — was Togame's armor wearing White Fox's face. It performed confidence. It performed control. It said: this is a series that knows exactly what it's doing, that is in full command of its effects, that is playing a long game and is not nervous about it.

And then it stopped performing.

"Was it okay that I fell for you too?"

The show had spent eleven months teaching you who Togame was by showing you who she pretended to be. It needed that long. A "Cheerio!" lands differently after you've heard forty of them. The silence after the forty-first is only as loud as the forty that preceded it.
