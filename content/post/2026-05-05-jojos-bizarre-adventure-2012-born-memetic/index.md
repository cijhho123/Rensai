On [January 27, 2016](https://knowyourmeme.com/memes/yes-roundabout-to-be-continued), a Vine user named Danksmash posted a clip combining a moment from another show with a freeze frame, an arrow sliding across the screen, and the opening notes of "Roundabout" by Yes. The clip was the first widely documented iteration of what would become the "To Be Continued" meme — borrowed wholesale from the closing format that [David Production had used in the 2012 JoJo's Bizarre Adventure anime](https://jojowiki.com/JoJo%27s_Bizarre_Adventure:_The_Animation) to punctuate its most extreme cliffhangers. Within weeks, the format was everywhere. Within months, [it had accumulated millions of loops](https://www.cbr.com/best-jojo-bizarre-adventure-to-be-continued-memes/), spawned infinite variations, and pulled a generation of Western viewers back to a show that had been quietly available on Crunchyroll for years.

The standard read of this moment is that JoJo's Bizarre Adventure "became a meme" in 2016. That reading is wrong, or at least backwards. JoJo didn't become a meme. JoJo was *always* a meme. The 2016 moment just gave internet culture the vocabulary to articulate what [Hirohiko Araki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hirohiko_Araki) had been building since 1987: a work structurally coded for instantaneous recognition, incongruous juxtaposition, and maximum theatrical impact. The 2012 anime, by committing fully to that register rather than softening it, became the delivery mechanism that finally gave the internet a language for what JoJo's had always been doing.

## The Structural Logic of a Meme

A meme works through three moves: a striking image that needs no context to register, delivery that is disproportionate to content, and a format repeatable enough to generate variations. JoJo's Bizarre Adventure executes all three with unusual precision, and not by accident.

Start with the images. Araki has described his approach to character poses in an interview: ["Manga drawings are boring if the characters are just standing still, but if you twist the character's waist a bit, you can create an extraordinary fantasy feeling."](https://redjacket.gg/anime/different-influences-in-jojos-bizzare-adventure/) His solution was to import posing logic from high fashion photography — specifically the work of illustrators like [Tony Viramontes and Antonio Lopez](https://jojowiki.com/Reference_Gallery), whose models struck contorted, unconventional poses that prioritized visual drama over anatomical plausibility. Applied to men built like [Michelangelo's sculptures](https://www.animenewsnetwork.com/feature/2019-07-31/celebrating-the-art-and-fashion-of-jojo-bizarre-adventure/.149560) — which Araki also studied for the ideal of exaggerated human form — these poses create images that are immediately dissonant. The [CBR piece on JoJo's art style evolution](https://www.cbr.com/how-jojos-bizarre-adventure-art-style-evolved/) notes that "Araki's evolving art — highly stylized anatomy, dramatic poses, and couture-inspired clothing — stands apart from mainstream manga/anime aesthetics." That's the point. The dissonance is the point.

The iconic "JoJo poses" aren't just [striking in isolation](https://www.cbr.com/jojos-bizarre-adventure-fashion-important-references-explained/); they invite imitation precisely because they look like they were designed to be performed. When fans recreate them — which they have been doing for years, both online and [at conventions worldwide](https://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/Memes/JoJosBizarreAdventure) — they're not parodying JoJo. They're participating in a logic that's native to the source material. You can't separate the pose from its memetic potential because Araki built the pose to *be* a pose. It's not a character in a situation — it's a symbol extracted from context, immediately legible, endlessly reproducible.

## Delivery Over Content

The second meme criterion — delivery disproportionate to content — is where JoJo is most nakedly itself. Take "KONO DIO DA!" ("It was me, Dio!"). The [line itself is simple](https://villains.fandom.com/wiki/Dio_Brando_(JoJo%27s_Bizarre_Adventure)): Dio reveals that he was behind a specific piece of treachery. The delivery is apocalyptic. The framing is as though the revelation of Dio's involvement is the most significant event in the known universe. Araki [designed Dio to embody the "attractiveness of evil,"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dio_Brando) to be beautiful and charismatic and maximally theatrical — but the theatricality is applied to content that is, objectively, somewhat silly. The gap between register and content is what makes it funny without being a joke.

This is not a bug in Araki's writing. He [modeled Dio's characterization partly on FBI profiling of serial killers](https://screenrant.com/jojo-anime-dio-brando-villain-hidden-backstory-jjba-explainer/) — the way genuine sociopaths use charm and presentation as tools. Dio's melodrama is *his* melodrama; he's performing for an in-universe audience, and Araki is letting us see the performance from the outside. The result is a character who reads as campy precisely because he's earnest, not in spite of it. That's meme logic: humor arising from genuine commitment, not winking irony.

The [piece from A Piece of Anime](https://apieceofanime.com/2017/09/02/the-memetic-magic-of-jojos-bizarre-adventure/) identified this dynamic accurately: JoJo's humor "doesn't need context because someone licking a cherry like that would be goofy and random no matter what." The comedy is in the delivery — "melodramatic delivery combined with the ultra serious framing of a pretty silly line." But the delivery IS the content. Strip the delivery and there's no joke, no image, no meme. You can't mock JoJo's delivery because the delivery is the point. The memes work because they're accurate.

## The Incongruity Machine

Araki's creative sources are already an act of incongruous assemblage. [Renaissance sculpture meets 1980s fashion photography meets Western rock music meets horror novel structure meets shōnen battle manga conventions](https://www.thecollector.com/what-inspired-hirohiko-araki-manga-artist/). The [Vogue+ interview](https://jojo-news.com/2022/02/28/vogue-an-interview-with-hirohiko-araki-in-china/) quotes Araki: "VOGUE is a fashion magazine chock-full of avant-garde and artistic photos. Every time I read it, I get inspired to draw." He listened to Western music while writing — [the series title itself is a Beatles reference](https://screenrant.com/jojos-bizarre-adventure-title-beatles-reference/), "JoJo" from the opening of "Get Back." Characters are named after David Bowie albums, Rolling Stones songs, Led Zeppelin tracks. Stands named after songs that are massive in the West — "Killer Queen," "Sticky Fingers" — are things the Western audience already knew, encountering them in a completely unexpected context.

This is incongruity as creative method. When you build from sources that already don't belong together, the result is a work that is perpetually surprising, that generates unexpected combinations at every level. In internet culture terms: every panel is a potential crossover meme, because every panel already *is* a crossover. Jonathan Joestar's muscular Victorian gentleman physique is itself a crossover. The Pillar Men — [ancient humanoids built like idealized gods who serve as the antagonists of Battle Tendency](https://jojowiki.com/Pillar_Men) — are described by fans as "the Aztec Gods of Fitness," which is funny because it's accurate. That's JoJo: specificity so extreme it becomes absurd.

## Why 2016 and Not 1987

The question that needs answering: if JoJo was always meme-native, why did the explosion happen in 2016 and not before? Two reasons, one obvious and one structural.

The obvious one: Vine. The [format that allowed video clips to circulate and accumulate](https://digitalcultures.net/memes/jojo-to-be-continued/) in 2015-2016 didn't exist before. The "To Be Continued" meme is fundamentally a video format — the freeze frame, the sting of Roundabout's opening, the arrow sliding in — and that format only became socially viral with the rise of Vine and then YouTube remixes. Even if you'd tried to explain the joke in text or a static image, it wouldn't have worked. The format needed the infrastructure.

The structural reason is more interesting: the 2012 anime committed to a register that the [previous 1993 OVA adaptation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/JoJo%27s_Bizarre_Adventure_(OVA)) never could. That OVA skipped directly to Part 3 (Stardust Crusaders), bypassing Phantom Blood and Battle Tendency entirely — which means it bypassed Jonathan Joestar, Joseph Joestar, the Pillar Men, Dio's origin, and virtually every moment that became foundational meme material. There was nothing to go viral because the source of the virality hadn't been adapted yet. David Production's 2012 series started from the beginning, and its [twenty-six episodes](https://jojowiki.com/JoJo%27s_Bizarre_Adventure:_The_Animation) leaned fully into the onomatopoeia, the color shifts, the theatrical monologues, the freeze frames. The result was an anime that behaved exactly like the manga: proud of its own absurdism, refusing to wink at the audience about it. [Crunchyroll streamed the series internationally](https://jojowiki.com/Crunchyroll), and when Western audiences who had grown up with internet culture encountered something genuinely committed to this register, they didn't know what to do with it at first — and then, in 2016, they suddenly did.

The meme gave them a handle on the experience. When the Vine format met JoJo's freeze frames, the reaction wasn't "this is how you make fun of JoJo." It was "this is exactly what watching JoJo feels like." The meme spread because it was accurate. [Multiple critics credited the anime adaptation for bringing a surge of international popularity to the franchise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/JoJo%27s_Bizarre_Adventure_(TV_series)), and the meme-to-viewership pipeline was why: it functioned as a precise description that made people curious enough to investigate the source, not a parody that let them feel like they'd already consumed it.

## What Separates JoJo's Meme Culture From the Rest

Plenty of anime get memed and then fade. The memes become a substitute for the work rather than an entry point into it. JoJo's memetic ecosystem is different in structure: [it regenerates interest rather than replacing it](https://uhshawkeye.com/entertainment/2026/03/27/jojos-bizarre-adventure-how-a-manga-from-1987-took-over-the-internet/). Every new wave of "Is this a JoJo reference?" posts — at this point a meme *about* JoJo's memetic ubiquity — produces new viewers. The [Know Your Meme entry on JoJo](https://knowyourmeme.com/memes/subcultures/jojos-bizarre-adventure) documents ongoing creation rather than archiving a historical moment.

The reason is that the memes remain accurate. "To Be Continued" is still a correct description of how JoJo episodes feel. "MENACING" in huge block letters is still how JoJo deploys visual sound effects — and [subtitles that bothered translating the onomatopoeia](https://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/Memes/JoJosBizarreAdventure) are what made that joke legible to Western audiences. "It was me, Dio!" still captures Dio's entire characterization in one line. The [fan discourse on platforms like Reddit and fan wikis](https://jojo.fandom.com/wiki/JoJo%27s_Bizarre_Adventure) continuously generates new layers of reference and in-joke — but always grounded in the actual text. The memes are an act of engagement, not replacement.

Compare this to *Sword Art Online*, where ["essentially hating on SAO itself became a meme"](https://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/Memes/SwordArtOnline) — a closed loop where the discourse replaced the text. The jokes about SAO don't function as an entry point; they function as a permission slip to not watch it. JoJo inverts this entirely: the joke is a reason to investigate what produced it. And what produced it is Araki's 35-year project of building something structurally unlike anything else in manga — visually immediate, tonally committed, perpetually surprising, built from sources that don't belong together and become strange and recognizable in their combination.

The 2012 anime didn't change this. It made it available. It preserved what Araki was doing at a level of fidelity that the internet, when it finally found the right format, could use as a mirror.

The "To Be Continued" meme is still circulating in 2026. Not because it's nostalgic. Because it's still correct.
