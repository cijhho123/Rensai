Everyone talks about Me-mania. The obsessive fan, the stalker, the man who believes Mima Kirigoe belongs to him — he's the obvious threat in *Perfect Blue*, the one who gets the threatening close-ups, the one the film codes as danger early and often. He's the external face of what the idol industry creates when it builds a parasocial fantasy too convincingly. [Film analysis](https://filmcolossus.com/perfect-blue-movie-explained), [academic essays](https://muse.jhu.edu/article/874951), [feminist readings](https://www.animefeminist.com/feature-the-fractured-and-famous-celebrity-culture-and-control-in-perfect-blue/) — they all give him significant weight, and fairly so. He is genuinely terrifying.

But Me-mania is not the film's deepest horror. He is a symptom. The disease has a name, and it's Rumi Hidaka.

## The Reading That Stops Too Soon

*Perfect Blue* is conventionally understood as a story about Mima losing her identity. She leaves CHAM, the pop idol group that defined her public self, to pursue acting. The transition is rocky — the roles she takes are darker than her fans want, her "pure" image is dismantled scene by scene, and she begins experiencing dissociative episodes where she can't distinguish her life from the characters she plays and the persona she abandoned. The film uses [Satoshi Kon's signature match-cut editing](https://otakufilms.wordpress.com/2015/02/18/satoshi-kon-techniques-and-utilizations-of-edits/) to make the audience experience her dissolution from the inside — cuts that slide between reality, performance, fantasy, and nightmare without signaling which is which.

This reading is accurate. Mima's psychological fragmentation is the engine of the film. But it locates the horror in Mima's *response* to her circumstances rather than in the circumstances themselves. And the more important question is: what machinery is producing those circumstances?

The machinery has a human face. It belongs to Rumi.

## What Rumi Actually Is

Rumi Hidaka is introduced as Mima's manager — warm, supportive, protective. She's a former pop idol herself, though the film is quiet about this detail until late. Her idol career ended the way most idol careers end in Japan: she aged out, or failed to maintain the standards required, or both. The industry that once needed her stopped needing her, and she transitioned to managing others.

This is not an unusual career trajectory. The Japanese idol industry [has always had a short shelf life for its performers](https://epress.earlham.edu/animeandgender/2018/02/11/purity-culture-and-idols-bay-erickson/) — idols are expected to project youth, innocence, and unattainability, qualities that are definitionally temporary. The industry builds enormous parasocial investment in these personas and then discards the people who inhabit them. What it does to the performers it keeps is well-documented; what it does to the ones it discards is less examined. Rumi is the film's answer to that question.

She never left. Not really. The obsession Rumi developed for Mima wasn't maternal attachment or professional investment — it was the idol industry's logic running inside a human mind that had no other frame of reference. She built Mima's idol persona the way she once inhabited her own, and when Mima started dismantling it, Rumi experienced it as an attack on the only thing that had ever mattered.

The "Mima's Room" website — the fake online diary that documents Mima's every move, written in first person as if by Mima herself — [was Rumi's creation](https://filmcolossus.com/perfect-blue-movie-explained). So was Me-mania. She hired him, cultivated him, weaponized him. The external threat that seems to come from outside the industry is actually the industry's own violence, delegated and directed by someone inside.

## The Distinction That Changes Everything

There's a clean conceptual line between Me-mania and Rumi that the film draws carefully. Me-mania believes in the image. He worships the pure pop idol he fell in love with and cannot tolerate the real woman who has chosen to become someone else. His violence comes from a fantasy that was always external to Mima — he's projecting onto someone he never actually knew.

[Rumi helped build the image.](https://www.back-row.com/home/2020/8/24/post-anime-club-tokyo-idols-amp-perfect-blue) She knows it's a construction. She knows the gap between what Mima performs and who Mima is. And she wants to eliminate that gap — not by helping Mima become more herself, but by forcing Mima back into the performance. Her murder campaign isn't fan obsession in the conventional sense. It's the industry's demand for an eternal, unchanging product, taken to its logical conclusion. The actress who played the rape scene needs to be punished, not because she hurt the real Mima, but because she hurt the *image*.

This is what [the academic feminist reading](https://muse.jhu.edu/article/874951) identifies as the film's central critique of Japanese idol culture: the industry commodifies innocence, then enforces it with real violence. What Rumi enacts is the enforcement arm made literal and personal. She isn't an aberration. She is the system, expressed through one woman who internalized it so completely that she can no longer separate the product from the person.

## The Dissociation Isn't Mima's Alone

The film has been analyzed extensively through the lens of [Mima's dissociation](https://medium.com/@BleuFriday/perfect-blue-1997-a-psychological-deep-dive-78a48289a405) — her sense of being watched, her inability to distinguish performance from reality, her conversations with a reflection that taunts and judges her. These are vivid and genuinely disorienting.

But Rumi's dissociation is the film's more complete portrait. By the third act she has stopped being Rumi Hidaka and is *being* Mima Kirigoe — the perfect idol Mima, the one who never took the acting roles or the rape scene, the one who stayed pure. [She dresses in Mima's clothes, speaks in Mima's voice, enacts Mima's presence](https://vocal.media/journal/unraveling-the-enigma-perfect-blue-explained). When she attacks Mima in the final chase sequence, she isn't a villain pursuing a victim. She is an image trying to destroy the person it was copied from.

Kon frames this through mirrors and reflections throughout the film — a technique so precisely developed that [Darren Aronofsky would later replicate specific shots wholesale](https://faroutmagazine.co.uk/satoshi-kon-criticised-darren-aronofsky-copying-perfect-blue/) in *Requiem for a Dream* and *Black Swan*. Mima sees an idealized reflection of her idol self wherever she looks; Rumi eventually *becomes* that reflection. The horror of both women is the same horror, experienced from different angles. Mima is afraid she might lose herself to the image. Rumi already has.

## What the Industry Produces

Japan's idol purity culture has been well-documented in the years since *Perfect Blue* was made — and it has not become less extreme. In 2013, [AKB48 member Minami Minegishi shaved her head on camera](https://theconversation.com/akb48-headshaving-and-the-sexual-politics-of-j-pop-11995) to publicly atone for staying the night at another performer's apartment. She was twenty years old. The group's no-dating rule, which she had violated, exists explicitly to sustain the impression that the idol is romantically available to fans — [a lawyer called the punishment a human rights violation](https://abcnews.go.com/blogs/headlines/2013/02/pop-star-shaves-head-in-remorse-for-dating). The [no-dating rules, the "handshake events" where fans pay for direct parasocial contact](https://www.thecoffeecreatives.com/perfect-blue-a-twisted-take-on-pop-idol-culture/) — these are features, not bugs. They exist to produce and sustain the kind of attachment that Me-mania represents: the belief that the idol performs her purity specifically for you, that your relationship is special, that any deviation from her pure image is a betrayal of something intimate.

[A documentary on Japanese idol culture](https://www.back-row.com/home/2020/8/24/post-anime-club-tokyo-idols-amp-perfect-blue) quotes a music producer saying flatly: "Japanese men have always prized purity in women. Now, idol culture feeds off that, worshipping virginity." *Perfect Blue* was made in 1997 and this is the water the film swims in. Kon [understood the system deeply enough](https://www.cbr.com/satoshi-kons-perfect-blue-feminism/) to know it didn't need supernatural horror. The normal operations of the idol industry were sufficiently nightmarish if you followed them to their conclusions.

Rumi is that conclusion. Not a monster from outside the machine, but someone the machine made and then discarded, who then tried to perpetuate the machine through a proxy. The idol industry created the conditions for Me-mania's obsession. It also created Rumi — a woman whose entire self-concept was built around an image she was eventually required to abandon, who could not survive that abandonment, who found in Mima a chance to keep the image alive.

## The Ending as Diagnosis

Mima survives. Rumi is institutionalized. The [final scene](https://fandomwire.com/perfect-blue-ending-explained-is-mima-truly-liberated-in-the-end/) has Mima looking into the mirror — the film's central motif, the site of all its anxiety — and for the first time seeing herself clearly. Not the idol. Not the actress playing darkness. Just Mima. "I'm the real thing," she says. The crisis is over.

What the ending doesn't address, because it isn't required to, is the industry that produced the crisis. Mima gets out. She survives by becoming a person. But the system that required her to be a product, that created Me-mania's worship and Rumi's obsession and the "Mima's Room" website and every mechanism of control the film depicts — that system continues. [The Rumi Hidakas who came after](https://www.sportskeeda.com/anime/perfect-blue-parasocial-culture-made-manifest) are the managers and producers and label executives who work inside an industry whose basic logic remains unchanged.

This is why *Perfect Blue* hasn't aged. Not primarily because of the internet prophecy (though [the prescience there is striking](https://www.interviewmagazine.com/film/perfect-blue-predicted-social-media-anxieties)), and not because of Kon's formal genius (though [the editing technique remains extraordinary](https://www.academia.edu/52466194/The_Perfect_Editing_of_an_Imperfect_Psychology_Analysis_of_Perfect_Blue_Satoshi_Kon_1997_)). It hasn't aged because [idol purity culture hasn't changed](https://thoughtsthatmove.com/2017/06/23/perfect-blue-a-meditation-on-idol-culture/). The system that produces Me-manias and Rumis is still producing them.

Mima got out. That's the good news. The bad news is what she got out of.
