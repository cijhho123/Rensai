Episode ten of *Jellyfish Can't Swim in the Night* contains a single line that changes everything that came before it.[^1] Mei confronts Kano about why she is singing. Why did she start JELEE? Why does she push herself so hard, schedule herself like a professional, measure JELEE's view counts against her old idol group? Kano's answer, delivered with the casual horror of a person who only just realized it herself: she formed JELEE to get her mother's attention.

That is the show's real argument. Everything else — the VTuber personas, the anonymous group identity, the Shibuya mural, the blue umbrella concert — is built on top of that single, unbearable fact. And because the finale wraps it all in triumph, with JELEE performing alongside the Sunflower Dolls as Yukine watches from backstage, most viewers walked away thinking the show was about creative liberation.[^2] It is not. It is about children performing for absent parents, and the uncomfortable question of whether that kind of approval ever actually satisfies.

---

## The Machine That Replaced Her

To understand why Kano's confession is so devastating, you have to understand who Yukine Hayakawa is.

Yukine is Kano's estranged biological mother. She is also the producer of the Sunflower Dolls — the idol group that Kano was forced to retire from after a scandal two years before the show begins.[^3] Kano was the center. She was "Nonoka Tachibana," the face of the group. Then she was gone, and Mero was installed in her place, and Yukine kept running the machine as if nothing had happened. As if her own daughter had been a replaceable component.

This is the double wound the show builds everything on. Kano was not simply expelled from idol life. She was expelled by the industry, and the industry is her mother. The woman who chose her career over her daughters — Kano and her older sister Mion both orbit Yukine's absence throughout the series — is also the woman who controls the mechanism that discarded Kano. When Kano says she formed JELEE to get Yukine's attention, she is not just confessing a small emotional neediness. She is confessing that her entire "escape" from the idol world was another route into the same gravitational field.

## The Other Daughter

The show's darkest structural irony lives in Mero.

Mero is Kano's replacement. She took the center position in Sunflower Dolls after Kano's forced retirement. She is, in the logic of idol fandom, Kano's rival — the girl who benefited from Kano's fall. But in episode ten, Mei finds out from Mero directly: Mero sings to impress Yukine.[^4] She always has. Her entire career is oriented around earning the producer's approval.

Think about what this means. Kano and Mero are not actually competing for audiences. They are both competing for the same thing: the recognition of one woman who has never made either of them feel sufficiently seen. They are, in the most structurally precise sense, sisters in their wanting — two girls orbiting the same absent center, performing on different stages.

The show does not press this point nearly as hard as it could. Mero is a supporting character, and her revelation is delivered and then largely moved past. But it sits there in the architecture of the narrative like a load-bearing wall. JELEE is not an alternative to the idol industry. It is a different format for the same approval.

---

## Miiko, or What the Industry Actually Does

Before the show gets to Kano's confession, it gives us episode six, and Miiko is the piece of the puzzle that most discussions of *Jellyfish Can't Swim in the Night* undervalue.

Miiko — real name Shizue Baba — is a 31-year-old underground idol and single mother.[^5] She has been working in the industry for fourteen years. Her production company has told her that if her next song fails, she will be let go. The reason is not her talent. The reason is her age.

Kano is reluctant to take Miiko's commission. Part of this is practical — they are an amateur group with no experience doing professional work for hire. But part of it is that Miiko makes Kano uncomfortable, because Miiko is what happens when the machine decides you are no longer useful. She is not a has-been. She is a woman who gave fourteen years to an industry and is now being discarded for being in her thirties. Her daughter Ariel is fierce in her support of her mother because someone has to be.

What makes the Miiko episode so sharp is how quietly it indicts the system Kano is still trying to re-enter. Kano sees a happy relationship between Ariel and Miiko and feels unsettled — she did not have this, and she still wants Yukine to be what Ariel's mother already is.[^6] The commission ends up working out, the song succeeds, Miiko gets her reprieve. But the reprieve is not a resolution. The industry's verdict on Miiko was not wrong; it was just delayed. The machine that ate Kano is the same machine that will eventually eat Miiko too. Both of them know it. And Kano, watching the relationship between Ariel and Miiko, wants the thing she cannot name: to be seen by the producer in the same uncomplicated, primary way that Ariel sees her mother.

## What the Finale Actually Gives Kano

In the final episode, JELEE performs alongside Sunflower Dolls at a major collaborative event. Kano freezes at the opening moment — she still does not have a clear reason to sing. Then she looks at the stage and sees what Mahiru has illustrated: Kano's vision of an aquarium in Shibuya, the thing she said she wanted to create when she first described what JELEE was for.[^7] She starts singing.

ANN reviewer Nicholas Dupree noted in his episode eleven write-up that Kano spends that episode trying to write a song addressed directly to her mother — making explicit what had been implied all along, that JELEE's earlier songs had been "thinly veiled odes" to other members of the group.[^8] For the finale, Kano wants to sing something Yukine will recognize as meant for her.

She gets it. Yukine is present backstage. The performance is a success. The recognition arrives.

This is where the show makes its most complicated choice. It does not interrogate what Kano winning actually means. The triumph is presented as earned and complete. But the question that the show's own structure raises — is approval from the person who failed you actually liberation, or is it just a more expensive form of the same want you started with? — goes unanswered. Kano sang for her mother. Her mother heard her. The machine that discarded Kano now accommodates her. She is back inside the same system, just from a different angle.

That is not a failure of the finale. It is what the show is documenting: the way children who were not adequately loved by their parents will spend enormous creative energy trying to produce something that finally justifies a response. Kano's art was always real. Her talent was always real. But the engine driving it was not freedom. It was a very specific grief.

---

## The Question the Show Leaves Open

*Jellyfish Can't Swim in the Night* is, despite all of this, a fundamentally generous show. Produced by Doga Kobo as part of their fiftieth anniversary celebration and directed by Ryohei Takeshita with a script by Yuki Yaku, it has warmth and visual ambition in abundance.[^9] ANN's critics praised its "elastic vibrancy" in character animation and the neon-lit Shibuya nightscapes that make the city feel like a stage designed specifically for the confusion of being seventeen.[^10] The show wears its emotional investment openly; it wants you to love these girls, and you do.

But the show's generosity toward its characters coexists with a structural argument that is more uncomfortable than the finale acknowledges. Every member of JELEE is performing for someone. Mahiru draws for Kano's approval, and traces everything back to the childhood moment when her friends laughed at her mural — a moment that Rebecca Silverman's ANN preview review correctly identified as the single most painful scene in the first episode.[^11] Kiui built a VTuber persona as an alternative to a social world that would not accept them at prep school, and ultimately has to break cover to assert their identity publicly.[^12] Mei attached herself to Kano with the intensity of a person who had projected enormous emotional significance onto a stranger.

Four girls, four different relationships to the audience they are performing for. Kano's is the most structurally damning because her audience is also the person who hurt her. She could not write the song until she knew what she wanted Yukine to hear. That is not liberation. That is a very long, very artistically accomplished letter to someone who never wrote back.

The show is honest enough to show you this. The aquarium in Shibuya is beautiful. Kano sings. Yukine watches. Whether any of that is enough — whether it was ever the right question — the show wisely does not say.
